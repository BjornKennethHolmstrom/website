# Adversarial review — Sweden: Whose Signals Count? v0.1

Nov 12, 2026 · @Björn Kenneth Holmström

## Verdict

v0.1 is publishable as an exploratory working paper only after a short list of corrections, and it supports no causal or legal conclusion. Neither the companion's thesis nor the parent's Competence Trap diagnosis is adequately supported by the evidence either paper presents.

| Question | Answer | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Safe to publish as an exploratory working paper? | **Conditional** | The numbers and dates I could check are accurate, and the hedging is disciplined. But the title and "case study" headings promise findings the paper does not have, the Sámi legal account omits the facts that matter most, and the English and Swedish versions make different claims at the legally sensitive points. Findings C1–C3 and M1–M12 below must be fixed first. |
| Safe to draw causal or legal conclusions? | **No** | The paper contains no evidence about differential responsiveness, which is its own thesis. Its strongest hypothesis about power (F3) and its privacy hypothesis (H-A4) cannot be falsified as written. No decision file, permit or dataset has been examined, as §10 itself admits. |

Three things survived scrutiny:

- **The narrowing moves are sound.** Rejecting "agencies cannot share", separating consultation from veto from consent, and keeping income Gini apart from wealth all hold against the primary sources.
- **The Sámi reframing is right, and understated.** The statute, the 2025 CERD observations and a 2026 analysis in Svensk Juristtidning support "rights are not a coordination variable" more strongly than the paper says.
- **The one audited case favours neither paper's headline.** Riksrevisionen's own explanation of weak information exchange is organisational: routines, staff turnover and uncertainty about the rules. That is neither a legal blockade or cultural filter (parent) nor a power effect (the DeepSeek critique).

The central thesis did not survive in any informative form. "Information matters only when someone has authority, resources and incentives to act" is true of every government and predicts nothing about Sweden. The stronger reading, that Swedish adaptation is selective by power, has no evidence behind it in v0.1.

I verified claims against sources I could open on 12 November 2026. I am not a lawyer; the legal points are readings of the statutory text and need a Swedish legal reviewer, as the package's own README recommends.

## Methodological assessment

The companion is a research protocol written in the form of a country analysis. Judged as a protocol it is careful; judged as an analysis it has nothing to report, and its packaging hides that.

### Strongest aspects

- **Accurate figures.** Every number I checked matched its source: Gini 0.314 / 0.310 / 0.246, 13.1 % low economic standard, 4,452 to 5,697 complaints, 60 / 49 / 22 for the climate assembly, the 1 December 2025 and 1 March 2024 dates, and CERD paragraphs 38–39.
- **Claim typing.** §2.2 separates observation, mechanism, causal attribution, legal finding and recommendation. Few working papers in this genre do.
- **Rival explanations built in.** H-A4 treats a privacy boundary as possibly protective rather than as a failure. §6 states that centralisation is not always domination. §7 allows that advisory bodies can matter.
- **Refusal of the strong claims.** §1 declines the three DeepSeek assertions (silos serve wealth holders, segregation from suppressed signals, green colonialism as a finding) and asks for evidence instead.
- **Honest status.** §10 and the ledger say plainly that no primary file, dataset or interview exists yet.

### Weakest aspects

- **No evidence on its own question.** The thesis concerns whose signals produce a response. The data cited (Gini, complaint counts, median incomes) describe outcomes and reporting behaviour. None measures responsiveness by group.
- **Hypotheses that cannot lose.** F1–F4 overlap, and the "would weaken" column for F3 is satisfied by any competent administration whether or not power is at work. H-A4 rests on a counterfactual.
- **A pre-selected cast.** The lenses chosen can only find unheard signals among disadvantaged groups and rights-holders. The parent's own flagship example, frontline staff whose integration warnings were allegedly discounted, is never tested.
- **Thin law in the flagship case.** The Sámi section cites the statute and CERD but omits the statute's exclusions, the absence of any sanction, the Supreme Court's Girjas judgment, CERD's 2020 finding against Sweden, and the Truth Commission's final report of 30 September 2026.
- **Selection on the outcome.** All three proposed cases are known problem areas. The parent itself says successes and failures must both be studied.
- **No reflexive disclosure.** §2.1 requires disclosing who framed the question and selected the evidence. The companion does not say how it was produced, unlike the parent's Appendix E.

### Overall

The companion is more epistemically disciplined than the parent and less substantive than it looks. Its real contribution is negative: it removes overclaims from both the parent and the DeepSeek critique. Its positive contribution, the four-way hypothesis frame, needs repair before it can discriminate between explanations.

## The foundational thesis and its strongest rival

The three mechanisms are distinct in concept but not as the paper specifies them, and a plainer explanation from political science accounts for the central pattern better than either paper. The competing accounts below come from the published literature as I know it; I did not re-verify sources for this section.

### Are information, incentive and representation failures distinct?

- **Information against the rest: separable.** "Did the decision-maker know in time?" can be answered from dated records. The audited child-crime pathway shows the method works: the audit found that a report of concern does not necessarily lead to an intervention.
- **Authority and capacity against incentive: not separable.** Budgets, staffing and mandates are the product of earlier political priorities. A municipality that "lacks resources" (F2) reflects an agenda decision one level up (F3).
- **Representation and rights: two different things under one label.** Representation is about inputs to a decision. Rights limit which outputs are allowed. F4 treats them as one.
- **The best case contradicts the title.** Sámi institutions' objections have been sent, received and recorded for years, including in UN proceedings. Nothing is unheard. The dispute is over who decides. Calling it a "signal" problem mislabels a dispute about authority, which the paper half-concedes in §5.
- **The set is not exhaustive.** It omits interventions that do not work, limited attention, and legitimate disagreement about goals, which the parent lists in §2.5.

### The strongest competing explanation

**Disproportionate attention.** Governments receive far more signals than they can process. Agenda space is scarce, institutions add friction, and so most problems get little response until a focusing event shifts attention. The response is then large and compressed. Political scientists call this punctuated equilibrium, and it has been documented across many countries and policy fields.

This explains the parent's Drift Loop without its two special assumptions. It does not need competence to cause blindness, and it does not need power to filter signals. It also fits what was verified here:

- **The audit.** The cause found was friction: routines, turnover and uncertainty. That is what the attention account expects.
- **NATO.** The parent now concedes that an external shock can rationally change policy. A focusing event is the attention account's trigger.
- **Generality.** Lag followed by lurch appears in poorly governed states too. A pattern found regardless of competence is weak evidence that competence causes it.

The three accounts predict different things:

| Account | Delay should be longest where… |
| --- | --- |
| Competence Trap (parent) | past performance is highest |
| Selective responsiveness (companion) | the cost of acting falls on influential actors |
| Disproportionate attention | the agenda is most crowded and procedures add most friction, whoever bears the cost |

Neither paper collects the data that would tell these apart. The attention account has limits of its own: it is hard to test for a single country, and it says nothing about rights.

### Which diagnosis is more convincing?

| Domain | What the evidence checked here supports |
| --- | --- |
| Child-crime pathway (case A) | Organisational and response failure. Not a legal blockade, a cultural filter or a power effect. |
| Whose hardship is legible (case B) | Nothing. No evidence either way. |
| Sámi land use (case C) | A dispute about authority and rights, fixed in the statute's design. The companion is right against the parent here, and should say so with the legal facts. |
| Municipal capacity (case D) | Nothing beyond what the parent's revision already conceded. |

The companion's central thesis is not shown to be wrong. In its weak form it cannot be wrong, and that is its problem. The parent's diagnosis is not more convincing: its causal core is untested and its examples are equally well explained by attention and party politics.

## The analytical lenses and asymmetric scrutiny

The lenses are used cautiously and mostly as vocabulary, so they impose few conclusions. The bias is in which hypotheses they allow to be asked, not in how the paper reasons about them.

### Do the lenses reveal mechanisms?

| Lens | What it does in v0.1 | Conventional equivalent | What the lens adds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Critical theory, political economy | A checklist of seven kinds of power (§2.3). Names no actor, resource or decision. | Interest-group theory (concentrated against diffuse interests); principal–agent and transaction-cost analysis | Little. The conventional versions make sharper predictions and apply to every organised group, including unions, municipal associations and advocacy bodies. |
| Intersectionality | Asks for disparities in one service pathway by combinations of characteristics (§4) | Subgroup and interaction analysis in programme evaluation | A reminder to look at combinations. The careful parts (power, small cells, no inference to individuals) are standard statistics. |
| Decolonial and Indigenous-rights analysis | Frames the Sámi case; the working content is statutory scope and consultation against consent | Ordinary legal analysis of statute, case law and treaty-body output | The framing. The legal analysis stands without it and is currently weaker than a plain legal memo would be (finding C2). |
| Critical realism | Named; used only as "test other mechanisms" | Rival-hypothesis testing | Nothing beyond the phrase. |
| Ecological economics | One metric line in §8.2 | Cumulative-impact assessment | Nothing analytic yet. |
| Second-order, reflexive | Demanded of pilots and evaluators | Conflict-of-interest and provenance disclosure | Not applied to the paper itself (finding M10). |

Two cautions on the decolonial lens. It treats the state–Sámi relation as the only axis, although CERD para. 38(c) itself raises the exclusion of non-reindeer-herding Sámi, and other northern residents and national minorities do not appear. It also implies a historical account the paper does not document; the state's own Truth Commission report now offers an official source.

Spiral Dynamics is correctly demoted to a tentative values vocabulary in §2.1. The parent's Appendix A still uses stage language, and the crosswalk does not say so (finding M9).

### Where the paper is even-handed

- **H-A4** allows that a barrier may be protective.
- **§6** says decentralisation can entrench local interests.
- **§7** says advisory bodies are not always decorative.
- **§8.1 step 3** codes missing links as missing, not as suppression.

### Where scrutiny is asymmetric

- **Who can be unheard.** Only disadvantaged groups and rights-holders. Staff, majority residents and people who bear the costs of interventions cannot appear as the unheard party (finding M5).
- **Vocabulary.** Officials may show "deliberate inaction", "agenda dominance", "publication suppression" and "retaliation". Affected groups supply "testimony", "co-design" and "validation". Strategic behaviour by complainants, advocacy bodies or self-selected participants is not considered.
- **Capture.** §8.2 guards against funders, contractors and local elites. It does not guard against organised advocacy, the research team's own priors, or co-design partners who help define the categories they are then measured by. The brief asks about this directly.
- **Harm accounting.** Harms from sharing are counted; harms from not sharing are not (finding M12).
- **Statistics.** Only the adverse movements are reported (finding M7).
- **Case choice.** Every case is a known problem area (finding M6).

### A bias in the other direction

The Sámi section hedges toward the state. It says three times that criticism is not a court ruling. It does not say that CERD found violations by Sweden in a specific mining case in 2020, or that the Supreme Court upheld a sameby's exclusive right against the state the same year. The paper's lean is therefore toward non-commitment, not consistently toward marginalised actors.

### Are constraints read as domination?

Mostly not. Two slips remain. The summary speaks of "preserving asymmetries" in whose evidence shapes the response, which presumes they exist. F3 refers to "influential actors" facing costs, which presumes they have been identified.

## Implications for Framtidskommuner

The companion has no findings yet, so nothing in it requires a change to Framtidskommuner. Three changes are required anyway by the law as it stands, and none of them depends on the critical lenses.

### Changes the legal facts require

1. **Narrow or drop the northern testbed.** A municipality's consultation duty covers adopting or amending plans. Mining concessions are decided by the state mining authority, and environmental permits by courts and permitting delegations that the Act does not cover. A municipal pilot cannot deliver rights-respecting land-use decisions. Limit that testbed to municipal services, or leave it out of the first cohort.
2. **Bar exemptions from consultation and land-use rules.** The 2025 Statskontoret assignment that the parent cites as its route for trials was announced as support for municipalities with large industrial establishments. Regulatory relief in exactly those places is where a rights conflict would arise.
3. **Rewrite the data testbeds.** The 2025 gateway serves control purposes, excludes health-care secrecy, and does not reach independent schools before 2027. Parent §3.4 still describes linking housing, school, health and social-services data. That contradicts parent §5.3 and has no identified legal basis.

### The companion's safeguards, one by one

| Safeguard | Assessment | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Separate Sámi consultation and rights track; no rights-diluting exemptions (§9) | Justified, feasible | Sharpen as in points 1–2 above. |
| Covenants create no disclosure power; legal assessment per purpose (§3, §8.2) | Justified, redundant | Parent §5.3 and §6.3 already say this. |
| Minimal sufficient coordination (§8.2) | Redundant for data; undefined for authority | For data it restates data minimisation. For authority there is no test of "sufficient". |
| Preregistration, baselines, comparison data (§8.2) | Justified, limited feasibility | Parent §6.3 already preregisters. Three to five volunteers out of 290 municipalities give no credible comparison group. |
| Stopping and repair rules for rights violations (§8.2) | Justified, new, cheap | Must say who decides and at what threshold. |
| Distributional endpoints by intersection (§8.2) | Justified in aim, not feasible at pilot scale | Finding M11. Collecting sensitive categories in small municipalities creates the exposure H-A4 warns about. |
| Co-design with affected residents (§4) | Useful for access, risky for independence | Conflicts with preregistration if partners define categories and success. Needs a rule on who has the final say. |
| Representation and speaking-time audit of the citizens' body (§8.2) | Justified, feasible | Matches the OECD guidelines' inclusiveness criteria. |
| Anti-capture indicators (§8.2) | Justified, incomplete | One-sided and without measures. The parent's own exposures come first: the Trojan Horse framing, "political cover", and selection by an unnamed foundation. |
| Contestability, appeal and independent review (§9) | Potentially counterproductive | Adds veto points and delay. Say what is added to the legality review that, to my understanding, already applies to municipal decisions. |
| Ecological indicators where material (§8.2) | Justified, risk of duplication | Cumulative land-use effects are assessed in state permitting, not by the municipality. |
| Publish selection criteria and reasons for non-participation (§8.2) | Justified, cheap | Addresses self-selection directly. |

### Two consequences for the design as a whole

- **The pilots cannot test the companion's question.** The summary says Framtidskommuner should test whether better sensing improves outcomes without more surveillance or concentrated voice. A handful of self-selected municipalities can show feasibility and surface harms. They cannot estimate effects, least of all by subgroup. Both papers should say so.
- **Evaluation load selects against the intended participants.** The parent wants a declining rural municipality and a high-need one in the cohort. Six families of indicators plus co-design, audits and reviews will be met most easily by the well-resourced. Keep a small core set and fund the evaluation centrally.

## What would falsify each hypothesis

Of fourteen hypotheses, three can be tested with records that realistically exist, seven can be tested in part, and four cannot be tested as written. The untestable ones include the companion's central thesis in its weak form and the parent's Competence Trap.

| Hypothesis | Observation that would weaken or overturn it | Realistically testable? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Companion thesis, weak form: information matters only where authority, resources and incentives exist | None. It is true by construction. | **No.** Not an empirical claim. |
| Companion thesis, strong form: response varies with who raises a signal or who bears the cost | Equal response times and outcomes across sources and cost-bearers for problems of equal severity | **Partly.** Needs a pathway where warnings are logged by source. Not testable in 3–5 pilots. |
| H-A1 legal bottleneck | For each sampled exchange a lawful gateway existed and staff knew it | **Yes**, by desk analysis per use. Already weakened for case-level crime prevention. Still open for independent schools until 2027, health-care data and learning uses. |
| H-A2 operational bottleneck | Lawful exchanges happened promptly and in usable form | **Yes**, given access to case files, which needs ethics approval and each authority's secrecy assessment. The 2024 audit already supports it. |
| H-A3 response bottleneck | Timely information plus adequate mandate and staff produced timely action | **Yes** where receipt and decisions are dated. Must be kept apart from "acted, but the measure failed". |
| H-A4 protective boundary | After 1 December 2025: no rise in wrongful disclosures, privacy complaints, or avoidance of services by affected groups | **Partly.** As worded it is a counterfactual. Avoidance is hard to measure and baselines mostly do not exist. The September 2026 directive says effects cannot yet be evaluated. |
| Case B: a discriminatory or legibility filter in one pathway | Equal referral, waiting time and outcome at equal need | **Partly.** Need is unobserved, so both gaps and parity are ambiguous. Matched-inquiry experiments would test it cleanly. I did not check whether national individual-level data on absence and open-care measures exist. |
| Case C: systematic exclusion of Sámi rights-holders | Across a sample of decisions, consultation came early and changed conditions or outcomes | **Partly.** Requests and decisions are public records, and counts exist: about 406 requests and 37 consultations at Sametinget to end-2023. | Municipal Trap | Municipalities with equal need but different margins after equalisation respond equally fast | **Partly.** Fiscal data are public. "Response" has no agreed measure. |
| Local capture | Local discretion is used in proportion to need, not to organised local interests | **No** as written. Capture is undefined. |
| Deliberation as a legitimacy device | Recommendations are adopted, or rejected with reasons, more often than comparable proposals from other channels | **Partly.** Uptake can be traced. The counterfactual cannot. |
| F3 agenda or incentive deficit | Transparent, reasoned decisions | **No** as written. Every competent refusal passes, and non-decisions leave no record. Testable only comparatively, or where the decision-maker states a reason, as in the 2025 asset-data decision. |
