## 1. The Coherence–Velocity Trap

### 1.1 Opening: The Governance Laboratory at the Edge of Capability

The original May 2026 report opens with the November 2023 OpenAI leadership crisis. Its account describes a nonprofit board removing the CEO, a statement concerning candour, an employee letter and pressure from commercial actors, followed by reinstatement and changes in board membership within days. More specifically, the original says that Sam Altman was removed, that over 700 of 770 employees signed a letter threatening to resign and join him at Microsoft unless the decision was reversed, and that he was reinstated within five days. It also describes legal and financial pressure from investors and a restructuring of the board. These are the original's dated narrative leads, not independently verified facts. The employee count, letter, Microsoft option, timing, investor role, applicable governance instruments and chronology must be checked against contemporaneous records. The report does not know the confidential reasons behind the decision. It cannot establish that the board's concern was an alignment risk or that particular executives or investors intended to defeat safety governance.

The episode raises a question that is more precise than whether a board was sufficiently informed. A decision was reportedly taken and then reversed under pressure. If corroborated, that sequence makes the distinction between formal authority, effective authority and durability of action especially salient. It does not establish a universal conflict between a nonprofit and a commercial organisation. A disagreement about process, management or evidence may be a legitimate explanation, and a reversal may be justified. The proper comparison is between actual decisions, applicable rights, stated reasons and independent outcomes, including episodes in which escalation succeeded without a public crisis.

### 1.2 The Alignment–Deployment Oscillation Loop

The original seven-stage narrative is retained as a possible sequence of events, not a prediction. Each stage has a separate observable claim and may fail to occur.

**Competitive pressure.** Funding milestones, talent competition, commercial demand and geopolitical policy may reward deployment. Determine the specific exposure and its timing; competition may also reward credible safety and reliability.

**Deployment acceleration.** An organisation may shorten release intervals, expand access or integrate more products in response. Test whether competitive pressure caused the change rather than technical progress, demand or a prior plan. Faster deployment does not automatically mean less safe deployment.

**Alignment concern escalation.** Evaluation, internal dissent, user experience or independent research may reveal concerns as capabilities change. A rise in recorded concerns could also reflect better detection rather than increased hazard. Warnings are evidence that a channel exists, not evidence that the organisation cannot see the risk.

**Safety intervention.** An authorised actor may halt, delay, condition or investigate a release, or change governance arrangements. The existence of a recommendation is not the same as an implemented decision. A safety body might lack authority or resources, or its recommendation might be declined for good reasons.

**Organisational crisis.** Interventions may trigger leadership disputes, staff departures, litigation or investor concern. But successful intervention may be routine and quiet. Determine whether the intervention contributed causally to the crisis, and distinguish competing explanations and the consequences of the underlying hazard.

**Temporary accommodation.** A compromise may restructure a board, change a commitment or reallocate responsibility. It could be a procedural concession that leaves the problem unresolved, or a durable improvement. The distinction requires follow-up evidence rather than an interpretation of the announcement.

**Repeat.** Recurrence is a testable possibility. Trust, resources and authority may weaken, remain stable or recover. Do not infer that every turn of the loop erodes coherence. Define the relevant recurrence window and comparison before examining outcomes.

A separate, slower hypothesis concerns the closing envelope of institutional alternatives. A governance arrangement that fails may lose funding, staff, credibility or access to independent evaluation, making future experiments harder. But entrants, spinouts, learning, open institutions and alternative funding may replenish viable options. Even an increasing count of organisations can conceal functional dependence on the same infrastructure. Conversely a small number of genuinely distinct alternatives may enable recovery. Measure switching costs, exit, entry, substitutability and recovery capacity separately from the fast oscillation. H4 and Appendix D.6 specify how the envelope might remain open. W8 provides a counterexample to inevitable depletion.

### 1.3 The Coherence–Velocity Trap Defined

The term denotes a conditional incompatibility between adequate safety coherence and sustained competitive viability, under specified constraints. It does not denote the inability to maximize every desirable outcome at once. Safety coherence here means maintaining justified safety commitments, observing relevant risks, and having the authority and resources to respond. Neither that concept nor viability is represented by a validated universal scalar.

The original contrast identifies five properties that may support coherence: *latency*, the time allowed to investigate and resolve safety concerns; *deliberation*, the opportunity to hear distinct evidence and affected perspectives; *distributed sensing*, access to independent sources and methods; *reversibility*, the ability to withdraw or contain an action; and *oversight*, accountable authority to review and constrain decisions. Each has costs and limits. Delay may be appropriate or harmful; deliberation without decision rights can become ritual; reversibility may be incomplete.

It also identifies five properties sometimes associated with velocity: *compression* of decision procedures, *centralisation* of decision rights, *iteration speed*, *proprietary advantage* and *first-mover capture*. These may reduce transaction costs, protect sensitive information or facilitate adaptation; they may also weaken challenge, encourage premature release or concentrate benefits. The properties are not mutually exclusive by definition. An institution may use fast, pre-authorised protective actions alongside slow constitutional review; better safety tooling may reduce both uncertainty and delay.

Disjoint maximizers of two objectives do not demonstrate that both minimum requirements cannot be satisfied. W9 distinguishes unilateral feasibility against a stated rival baseline, robust feasibility across a specified range of rival profiles, and joint feasibility under authorized coordinated policies. A single organisation may have an adequate response even if another does not. If coordination enables adequate joint outcomes, that does not establish equilibrium, legal permission or the necessity of the proposed Commons. H0 tests those claims separately.

### 1.4 The Recursive Governance Deficit

The original argument contrasts technological change with the speed at which institutional arrangements can change. Corporate boards, legal instruments and public regulatory procedures may operate on different timescales from software development and deployment. A response that arrives after an irreversible action is a different problem from one that arrives in time but is inadequate. The relevant window must be established for a particular hazard and action, not assigned universally to a type of institution.

Distinguish measured capability changes, AI-assisted R&D, genuinely autonomous recursive technical improvement and forecasts of future acceleration. The report has not established that frontier systems recursively improve themselves autonomously. Also distinguish organisational learning: updating an evaluation, reporting a failure or changing a decision process may enable institutional adaptation. This is a possible resource against the deficit, rather than technical recursion. H3 compares actual capability changes, response deadlines, escalation, remediation and revision capacity. Delay can reflect a deliberate safeguard, not incapacity. A rapidly adapting institution may be adequate, while even a slow-changing technology may present a problem if its governance lacks authority.

### 1.5 Task-relative adequacy rather than a company Variety Gap score

The original value-architecture argument treated objectives, metrics and commitments as a finite-dimensional observation channel. That identification and its dimension-count interpretation are withdrawn. A company can observe a signal while giving it little weight, or value an outcome without being able to measure it. A board may see and understand a danger but lack effective power to respond. A capable controller may face a legal or physical action constraint. Appendix C gives conditional witnesses for these distinctions.

For a specified risk and affected population, record (1) task and success criteria, (2) evidence, channel and uncertainty, (3) objectives and incentives, (4) decision rights and escalation, and (5) feasible actions, actual implementation and independently assessed outcomes. Do not aggregate these into a company score. A benchmark, a revenue metric, a safety research result or a deployment dashboard can inform a decision without establishing any of the other dimensions. The original comparison of OpenAI growth and evaluation metrics and Anthropic alignment research and deployment metrics is a set of leads to verify. It does not imply that either organisation is unaware of the dimensions it gives less attention to.

The original gives concrete examples of possible omitted or underweighted concerns at OpenAI: erosion of public trust, cumulative regulatory risk across jurisdictions, dependence on a single supply chain, and long-term systemic effects of increasingly autonomous agents. At Anthropic it identifies competitive responsiveness if rivals accelerate, talent retention under slower deployment, and investor patience as concerns to examine. These are attributed hypotheses, not demonstrations of missing observation. A signal might be available and weighed but have little influence, or an actor might lack authority to act on it. For each example test actual evidence, attention, decision rights, feasible responses and outcomes separately. Apply the same standard to both companies.

### 1.6 The Capital Architecture as Governance Mechanism

Funding arrangements may change the constraints under which managers operate. Fund horizons, expectations of liquidity, capital-intensive infrastructure, investor rights and contractual commitments are mechanisms to investigate. The original suggests a five-to-ten-year venture-fund horizon and a shorter horizon for particular milestones; those figures and the actual contract terms are unverified leads, not universal features of frontier AI finance. Capital can also finance costly safety work and patient research. The identity and duties of a director cannot be inferred merely from who nominated them; applicable law and actual instruments matter.

The hypothesis is that some funding arrangements reward deployment decisions more reliably or sooner than they reward independently verified risk reduction. If so, attention and decisions may be affected even when information is available. Compare firms or episodes with different funding conditions, and test whether the association persists after controlling for technical and market changes. Long-horizon capital, liability, customer demand for reliability and reputation may reverse the relationship. The capital argument is a pathway through incentives and authority, not an information-theoretic identity.

To make the time-horizon issue concrete, consider the original's example in chapter 2: a fund expecting liquidity within ten years and a risk that could materialise in fifteen. This contrast illustrates a possible incentive mismatch, not proof that the fund cannot observe or price the risk. The actual terms and exposure, including effects on current valuation and beneficiaries' longer-term interests, are empirical questions.

### 1.8 The Genuine Strengths

The original highlights technical talent, research programmes and mission-driven cultures as possible resources for governance. Its references to Anthropic interpretability research, OpenAI safety research and DeepMind ethics work are historical leads rather than comparative findings. The original's xAI account suggests that concentrated decision pathways might enable rapid action. Its DeepSeek account makes only a concessive reference to technical sophistication, not a favourable finding about patient capital. The correction introduces two additional rival hypotheses for DeepSeek: technical capacity might support effective independent assessment, and longer-horizon institutional or funding arrangements might support restraint. These are newly introduced questions, not claims attributed to the original or verified descriptions of DeepSeek. Assess those potential strengths using the same evidence rules as alleged deficiencies. Research quality and institutional effectiveness are distinct, and sincerity cannot be inferred from the company's form.

Learning from deployment, updating safety practices and revising governance may be resources for adaptive coherence. The original's description of an organisational self-improvement ethos is not evidence of technical recursive improvement. The question is whether learning is operationally integrated, with observable changes to decisions and outcomes. H3 permits such learning to disconfirm a governance deficit.

### 1.9 The Real Question

The familiar debates over voluntary commitments, open versus proprietary release, safety and innovation, and trustworthy stewardship remain important. The architectural question is whether particular arrangements can perceive, interpret and act on relevant risks while maintaining adequate safety and viability. The answer may involve organisational reform, shared infrastructure, public oversight or existing mechanisms. It is not predetermined that single organisations cannot succeed or that collective governance is necessary. The following mechanisms and proposals are candidates for investigation.

### 1.10 Diagnosis tests H0–H5

Prespecify the task, population, cases, time window, evidence standards, authorized access, comparison group, rival explanations and outcome criteria. Include favourable and unfavourable episodes. No forced access to protected information and no speculation about confidential events. Diagnosis is distinct from evaluating the proposed intervention in §5.5.

**H0 — Conditional strategic incompatibility.** Prespecify justified safety and financial/operational viability floors, horizon, uncertainty and rival profiles. Compare unilateral feasible policies against a stated rival baseline and authorized jointly feasible profiles separately, using existing arrangements and matched episodes. Robust unilateral feasibility requires a single admissible policy satisfying the floors across the stipulated set of rival profiles; adaptation to observed rivals is a different policy class. Sustained adequate restraint despite rival acceleration weakens unilateral impossibility; observed joint adequacy under existing coordination weakens joint impossibility, but does not demonstrate need for the Commons. Safety investment associated with neutral or positive viability under credible matched conditions also weakens the claimed conflict. Historical choices do not identify the feasible frontier. Account for resources, risk, entry, exit, failed firms and survivorship; unknown alternatives remain unknown. No inference of equilibrium or authority follows.

**H1 — Decision-pathway deficit.** Trace signals, interpretation, mandate, effective authority, implementation and outcomes in comparable cases. Adequate existing channels and action in traced tasks, or technical limits accounting for differences, weaken the proposed diagnosis. Distinguish missing evidence from known evidence not acted upon.

**H2 — Oscillation.** Code each of the seven stages separately, including ordering, recurrence and common shocks. Compare management disputes, uncertainty and genuine policy change as rival accounts. Durable restraint or reform without crisis, benign accommodation, no predicted recurrence, or no association after confounding weakens the hypothesis. Public crises are more observable than private successful escalations, quiet restraint and unreleased models. Scope conclusions to observable episodes; absence of recorded restraint is not evidence of absence.

**H3 — Recursive/timing deficit.** Measure capability change, response windows, escalation and remediation, revision capacity and task performance. Distinguish autonomous recursion, AI-assisted R&D and learning. Effective timely responses, stable or narrowing latency gaps or appropriate deliberate delay weaken the diagnosis. Organisational learning is a rival, not proof of incapacity.

**H4 — Monoculture and depletion.** Begin with the original five heterogeneous archetypes, its account of Anthropic's 2021 founding by former OpenAI staff and Suleyman's move to found Inflection, all unverified leads potentially inconsistent with monotone depletion. Other entry dates and DeepSeek's release model were reviewer-supplied leads, not manuscript facts. Compare existing evaluation regimes and organisational alternatives: common-mode errors, switching costs, functional substitution, exit, entry and recoverability. Durable independent alternatives, replenishment balancing exit, uncorrelated errors or common standards enabling challenge weaken the hypothesis. No protected data are demanded to create comparisons.

**H5 — Safety-washing.** Prespecify an observable mismatch between commitment and operational practice, severity and relevant time horizon. Trace independent corroboration, decision rights, remediation and disclosure. Durable implementation and independent challenge changing decisions, or legitimate confidentiality or uncertainty explaining a mismatch, weaken the hypothesis. Sincere belief is not evidence of safety-washing; intent cannot be inferred without evidence. Apply the same test to every company, including Mythos. Non-disclosure alone is neither evidence of misconduct nor proof of responsibility.
