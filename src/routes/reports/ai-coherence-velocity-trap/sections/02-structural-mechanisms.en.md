## 2. Structural Mechanisms: How Frontier AI Governance May Fail

### 2.1 What "Adaptive Coherence" Means

Adaptive coherence is an advocated design objective: the capacity to sustain justified safety commitments and viable operations while responding to changing circumstances. It is not a measured property of the five companies, nor a single objective that can speak for humanity. The operational question is whether relevant evidence reaches a legitimate decision-maker, whether the decision is justified, and whether its implementation and effects remain corrigible. The following mechanisms are possible explanations of failures in those pathways. Their interaction is not a universal law.

### 2.2 Capital Architecture as an Incentive and Attention Channel

A funding relationship can influence what is collected, what receives attention and which decisions are affordable. Growth forecasts, revenue, model performance, release milestones and anticipated liquidity events are signals that may matter to investors. Long-term risk reduction can be difficult to value using those signals, especially if the benefit is avoidance of a rare event. This is a hypothesis about the contractual incentives and information practices of particular institutions. The original account of venture cycles and investor-appointed directors needs verification of actual fund terms, governing documents and applicable fiduciary duties.

An investor may also seek reliable products, durable customer relationships and avoidance of legal exposure. Patient capital, public-interest mandates and insurance requirements may favour safety. A capital provider may receive a safety report and legitimately disagree with its interpretation, or may agree with it but not possess authority to act. For H1 and H0, compare financial covenants, board rights, evaluation budgets, release decisions and independent outcomes, not just the types of capital employed. No objective is itself a sensor. Distinguish information loss from incentives and from legally authorized constraints.

The original's longer-horizon example is useful: a risk that may materialise fifteen years from now and a fund that expects liquidity within ten years can create a mismatch. It is not necessarily invisible to the fund; a risk may affect valuations today or be relevant to beneficiaries who retain exposure after the fund closes. Compare actual risk estimates, exit incentives, funding conditions and investment decisions. Societal externalities, geopolitical fragility and loss of public trust may not be captured by short-term revenue and growth metrics. That is a reason to examine the availability of other evidence and whether it has a legitimate path to affect a decision. It is not a theorem that risk necessarily accumulates until a crisis. A patient investor and an effective board may combine short-term operating metrics with long-term evidence.

### 2.3 Founder-Centric Compression of Observability

Concentrating decision authority can shorten escalation paths, reduce negotiation costs and make a responsible person identifiable. It can also create dependence on a narrow set of interpretive assumptions or reduce independent challenge. A founder may have access to multiple channels and respond effectively; a large committee may ignore the same evidence. The proposed mechanism is a concentration of decision rights and possible filtering of evidence, not a count of the founder's cognitive dimensions.

The original uses xAI's founder-centred architecture and rapid iteration as an illustration, and draws comparisons with founder influence in OpenAI. The Suleyman example belongs to the separate DeepMind discussion (§2.4). The dates, governance rights, and actual roles of the people involved are unverified. Treat these as questions about escalation pathways, delegated rights, retention of institutional memory and whether contrary evidence changes decisions. No conclusion about personalities or motives follows from organisational structure. H1 can be weakened by documented independent escalation and timely correction in a concentrated architecture. H3 can be weakened if rapid decision pathways improve governance adaptation.

The attraction of founder-centred governance in the original is not just decision speed. A founder can bring technical intuition and coherent strategic direction to a difficult problem, reallocate resources quickly and act without duplicated approval. The risk is a bottleneck when safety concerns must pass through the same person who determines deployment priorities. Consider an engineer whose concern has already been recognized by the founder: the problem, if no appropriate response follows, could be a disagreement about evidence or an implementation constraint rather than a failure of sensing. Independent channels for workers, users and other affected people may supplement internal expertise. Their inclusion must be authorized and proportionate to the particular decision. Compare a founder-centred firm with effective delegated safety rights against a dispersed organisation with ineffective handoffs; either could be more adequate. The original xAI account identifies Elon Musk, a small team and streamlined decision-making as leads. Verify the dated claims rather than assuming these features remain in force.

### 2.4 Scale-Induced Fragmentation: Google DeepMind

The original narrative follows DeepMind from its research roots into a larger corporate structure, with separate teams, product units and the eventual consolidation of research activities. Verify the acquisition, organisational changes, dates and responsibilities rather than assuming a current structure. Its analytical concern is that a large organisation may possess extensive expertise but encounter problems integrating findings across boundaries. A safety concern known to one unit may not reach the people responsible for a particular release. It might reach them but be assigned different weight, or the unit might lack a mandate to act across the boundary.

Scale also makes possible specialist teams, diverse methods, resources for long investigations and parallel challenge. Central integration can help coordinate otherwise fragmented work, but can also create a common failure mode. Test which pathway is actually operative using handoffs, escalation time, decision rights, independent outcomes and documented changes after reorganisation. A finding that independently produced evidence is successfully combined and acted upon would weaken the proposed fragmentation mechanism. The mere existence of separate research units is not evidence of a governance deficit.

The original chronology names DeepMind's founding in 2010, Google's acquisition in 2014, the research achievements AlphaGo and AlphaFold, the 2023 merger with Google Brain, Mustafa Suleyman's departure to found Inflection AI and his subsequent move to Microsoft. It also describes a period of relative research autonomy and leadership advocacy for mission protection. These dates, achievements, statements and institutional relationships are verification leads. They motivate a comparison of autonomy before and after organisational change. For example, research autonomy might protect exploratory safety work while making communication with product teams more difficult; integration might solve the communication problem while reducing an independent challenge function. An evidence-to-decision trace would ask whether a concern raised in research changes a product roadmap, and distinguish lack of transfer from a reasoned disagreement. It would also ask whether the parent company's duties and the research unit's mandate are actually in tension, rather than infer the answer from the subsidiary relationship. Personnel movement may represent a search for alternatives, or an unrelated career decision; it is not proof of governance failure.

### 2.5 The Alignment-First Architecture Under Competitive Pressure: Anthropic

The original identifies Anthropic's founding in 2021 by former OpenAI employees, public benefit corporation status, Long-Term Benefit Trust and Constitutional AI research as possible institutional innovations. Verify each claim and its relevant date, including the trust's actual appointment rights and how those rights were exercised. Constitutional AI concerns model-development methods; it does not itself confer corporate oversight power. A long time horizon can coexist with excellent deployment feedback, and an institution may weigh competitive pressure without prioritising it. No comparative claim that this company has greater observational variety or excludes deployment velocity is warranted.

The proposed question is whether governance commitments can remain operationally binding under changing financial, talent and infrastructure constraints. The original account of an April 2026 Mythos access decision is a particularly important research lead: it reports restricted rather than general access, a structured partner programme called Project Glasswing, a safety document said to be 244 pages long, approximately forty partners and a coordinated defensive cybersecurity programme. These numbers, timing, risk descriptions and access rules are unverified. The original also recounts red-team findings and a reported 6–18-month estimate for capability changes, whose meaning, date and attribution require verification. Verify these against primary sources and independent corroboration; do not reproduce operational exploit details. The report cannot establish the model's actual capabilities, the company's motives or whether its documentation accurately represents its decisions.

If corroborated, withholding may be a candidate episode of unilateral restraint. A self-authored system card, a restricted access programme or a stated safety reason alone establishes neither effective restraint nor safety-washing. Apply the same evidence standard to every company: check actual access, independently corroborated outcomes, relevant counterfactuals, decision authority, durability, and viability under a stated rival baseline. Reviewer-supplied leads about subsequent releases and an export-control suspension are queued separately, not silently added to the May account. The episode cannot simultaneously serve as an unqualified counterexample to the trap and proof of the proposed Commons. It may weaken H0 or H2 in its specified scope; the value of the Commons must be evaluated independently.

### 2.6 The Nonprofit/For-Profit Hybrid Instability: OpenAI

A hybrid arrangement may try to combine a public-interest mandate with commercial resources. Its attraction is the possibility of a mission-protecting authority that can access the finance and talent needed for development. Its potential weakness is a discrepancy between the authority stated in legal instruments and what can be exercised when contractual obligations, employment relationships or financing arrangements are put under stress. The November 2023 account in §1.1 motivates investigation of that distinction. It does not show that a board failed to perceive a concern: the account itself reports an exercise of board authority, later reversed. The concern's substantive grounds and reasons for the reversal require verification.

A legal structure can evolve in response to a crisis. Changes might weaken an oversight body, improve accountability or clarify authority. Compare the actual instruments at each date, the exercise of powers, procedural fairness, independent evidence and durability of changes. No inference about the current governance arrangements follows from a historical episode. An institution may have legitimate reasons to constrain or replace a board's decision, just as a board may have legitimate reasons to halt a deployment. The hypothesis is not that hybrid organisations necessarily break. It is that mismatches between formal rights and effective implementation may become salient under pressure, a claim H1 and H2 permit the evidence to weaken.

The original describes capped investor returns as part of the historical hybrid structure, intended to limit the profit motive while enabling investment. It also claims in §2.3 that post-crisis restructuring concentrated executive authority and reduced board independence. Both are dated leads for comparison of the operative instruments and effective decision rights, not findings about the current structure. In particular, a return cap does not establish that financial incentives are aligned, and a change in board composition does not by itself establish that oversight weakened.

The original's distinctive institutional suggestion is that there was “no constitutional court, no independent arbiter, no pre-agreed escalation protocol” (the hyphenation is normalised here). Whether any such mechanism existed or was usable requires verification. The design question survives independently of the historical diagnosis: when a safety mandate and commercial decision rights conflict, which body can hear the dispute, on what evidence, under what deadlines, and with what power to enforce its determination? A pre-agreed procedure could help protect both legitimate restraint and legitimate objections to a stop; it could also introduce delay or be captured. This is the rationale for the four mechanisms in §3.2, not a conclusion that every hybrid needs a new arbiter.

### 2.7 State-Coupled Epistemic Closure: DeepSeek as a historical hypothesis

The original proposes state coupling as a possible influence on an AI developer's decisions. It contrasts commercial pressures with policy priorities, geopolitical incentives and constraints on external scrutiny. The legal form, financing, applicable laws, actual decision pathways, release model and any relationship with government actors require verification. The hypothesis must not be converted into a factual assertion of state control, uniform censorship or absence of independent safety assessment. Nor is a company's national location a substitute for evidence.

Government relationships may provide resources, long-horizon investment and public-interest requirements; they can also restrict some decisions or information. Western developers likewise operate within regulatory, procurement, export-control and national-security arrangements. Compare concrete constraints, available evidence, independent scrutiny, authority and outcomes using the same rubric in each case. A release model or an accessible technical artefact is not by itself proof of safety or openness in the broader governance sense. Findings of adequate independent challenge, or evidence that another mechanism explains observed constraints, would weaken the proposed explanation. H1, H4 and H5 are available without inferring hidden motives.

### 2.8 Safety-Washing as a Possible Immune Response

The original argues that organisations may acquire legitimacy through safety commitments that do not change practice. This is a claim-to-practice mismatch hypothesis, not a diagnosis of sincerity. Voluntary commitments may establish real obligations; published research may change the engineering process; an advisory body may affect decisions without a veto; selective disclosure may be necessary to avoid misuse or protect confidential information. Each mechanism needs evidence about implementation rather than a presumed purpose.

Four pathways motivate the tests in §4.3: public commitments without operational follow-through; safety research disconnected from release decisions; advisory bodies whose recommendations have no effective route to action; and public disclosure that obscures material facts. The report's own illustration of a concern that is acknowledged, studied and published but not acted on is an authority or action question as much as an information question. The proposed Commons can reproduce these failures through certification, conflicted auditing or common benchmarks. H5 requires independently corroborated mismatches, meaningful correction and attention to alternative explanations. An organisation's sincere belief in its commitments cannot confirm the diagnosis. That self-sealing clause of the original is withdrawn.

A related claim in the original concerns co-optation and the density of safety signals. Successive commitments, research publications and appointments of advisory bodies could make criticism harder to sustain if outsiders infer operational effectiveness from these visible signals. An organisation could then gain reputational protection without improving the underlying decisions. Test this pathway by tracing the timing of commitments, external scrutiny, actual changes to release decisions and independently corroborated outcomes. An accumulation of signals accompanied by effective remediation, or criticism remaining influential despite them, would weaken the hypothesis. Co-optation is not established simply by a safety researcher joining an institution or an advisory panel being created. The same test applies to proposed Commons certification and to all five companies.

### 2.9 The Cultural Operating System of Frontier AI

The original sketches four interacting cultural elements. Technological optimism may frame faster innovation as a contribution to welfare, although optimism may also motivate safety investment. Expectations about scaling may support resource allocation and attract talent; the technical hypothesis and its social use are separate questions. An engineering orientation may productively address technical hazards, while treating a question of authority as exclusively technical could obscure alternatives. Competitive urgency may frame delay as existential, but the actual costs and benefits of delay must be assessed, not presumed.

These elements could reinforce funding and organisational arrangements: funding may favour particular timelines, recruitment may select for preferences consistent with those timelines, a public identity may shape which alternatives are considered, and safety signalling may affect stakeholder confidence. The direction can also reverse; new evidence, changes in markets or effective oversight may alter the culture and funding conditions. Avoid assigning one worldview to all staff or attributing the same motive to every organisation. Test each link against cases of changed practice and rival explanations; no evidence of feedback follows simply from coherence of the narrative.

### 2.10 How the Mechanisms May Reinforce Each Other — and Fuel the Oscillation

The seven stages in §1.2 can interact with the mechanisms above. For instance, funding might reward a shorter release window; concentrated decision rights may reduce challenge; an evidence handoff may fail; a resulting concern may prompt a contested intervention; a procedural accommodation may or may not change effective authority. These are conditional pathways, not an assertion that deployment suppresses all safety signals. The alternative paths include improved detection, successful escalation, durable restraint, legitimate rejection of an intervention, stable or replenished independent alternatives and absence of material hazard.

Each arrow is a hypothesis, not a causal estimate. At each transition, the process can stop, reverse or lead to effective correction. A slow envelope of viable alternatives can contract or expand independently. The original diagram is not included in the supplied source; do not render the old diagram until independently inspected and corrected.

The original calls Anthropic a possible partial break in the loop. Its stated institutional innovations and the reported Mythos decision are candidate counterexamples, not established successes. An apparent break may be durable, temporary or explained by other factors, including externally imposed constraints. Evaluate its authority, actual actions and outcomes on the same basis as any other company. No universal claim that the loop cannot be exited unilaterally survives.
