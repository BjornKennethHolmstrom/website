## 3. What Building Adaptive Coherence Would Look Like

### 3.1 The Principle: Multi-Scalar Governance as a Candidate Design

The report advocates governance at organisational, cross-organisational, societal and international scales. The rationale is that decisions about individual releases, shared evidence, affected populations and cross-border risks can have different scopes and deadlines. Assigning all tasks to one body may overload it or omit affected interests; assigning each to a separate body may introduce conflicts, duplicated costs and delay. Appendix C withdraws a universal impossibility result about single-scale control. The architecture is a candidate to compare with simpler authorized arrangements, not a mathematical requirement. H0 asks whether any joint coordination is needed in the first place. Legitimate disagreement and no additional coordination are possible outcomes.

### 3.2 Organisational-Level: Constitutional Governance for Frontier AI

Organisational reforms address the authority and action parts of H1, not just the amount of evidence available. Four mechanisms are proposed. Their implementation depends on actual legal powers and resources, and the competence of existing institutions should be assessed first.

**Independent safety board.** A corporate instrument or applicable law could give a qualified board a defined power to halt or condition particular releases when predeclared safety requirements are not met. Appointment and removal, independent budget, conflicts, access to evidence and authority to require implementation should be specified. The original suggests a multi-stakeholder appointments committee with research staff, external safety experts and civil society as illustrative participants. Compare this with other lawful appointment mechanisms, including how members are selected or removed and how conflicts, competence and capture are addressed. It is a design option, not evidence of representative legitimacy or a transfer of public power. Emergency stops need time-bounded review, reasons, appeal and remedy. A board does not acquire public regulatory power merely by being independent; it must act within its lawful remit. Test whether decisions are actually carried out, not only announced.

**Multi-stakeholder oversight.** Researchers, civil society and affected groups could contribute distinct evidence and challenge to a defined body. Where legally authorized, the body could request relevant information, commission investigations or require responses. Participation does not prove representative legitimacy; selection, accessibility, contested representation, conflicts and nonparticipants' rights need attention. No group is presumed to consent on behalf of humanity. Distinguish advisory input from binding authority.

**Protected dissent channels.** Employees could raise safety concerns through secure pathways to an independent board and competent external authorities, with protection from retaliation. The original explicitly raises non-disparagement agreements, confidentiality clauses and protections that outlast employment. A proposed protected channel should not be defeated by a contractual restriction on lawful reporting or by retaliation after departure. Compare actual whistleblower law, applicable employment protections and legitimate confidentiality obligations in each jurisdiction; do not assume blanket immunity or a right to disclose sensitive material publicly. Disclosure should be purpose-limited, with confidential channels for sensitive material and independent review of claims. The ability to disagree without retaliation should not depend on a participant waiving legitimate privacy or security rights.

**Internal audit.** A properly resourced audit team could trace commitments to decisions and implementation, reporting independently of the executives whose actions are examined. Access would be authorized and limited to relevant systems and records, not an unrestricted right to proprietary or personal data. Publish safe aggregate findings, uncertainty and remediation status; retain protected material for competent review. Provide a mechanism to contest, correct and appeal findings. Evaluate auditor independence and whether identified shortcomings are actually corrected.

These four mechanisms can be combined or separately adopted. Compare their costs, duplication, latency, quality of challenge and effect on independently measured outcomes. Effective existing arrangements may make some unnecessary. A safety board that reports but lacks effective decision rights may leave the problem unchanged; a board with overly broad powers may create unjustified delay. The design question is how to match observation to legitimate authority and implementable action.

### 3.3 Cross-Organisational: Shared Safety Infrastructure as a Governed Commons

The proposal is to pool particular resources where doing so improves access to relevant evidence or reduces unnecessary duplication. A shared infrastructure may lower the cost of evaluating some hazards and allow smaller organisations to benefit from tools they could not build alone. Those potential benefits must be compared with confidentiality costs, competitive constraints, concentration and common-mode errors. Participation alone is not evidence of safety. The design principle is to consolidate authorized substrate while diversifying interpretation (D.6); the boundary between these is contestable because evaluation sets and reporting standards already embody assumptions.

**Shared evaluation platforms** could support independent assessment of specified robustness, interpretability, misuse and capability questions. Multiple approaches, independently governed evaluators, protected dissent, separately developed holdouts and exit/failover should remain available. A common benchmark may be gamed or produce correlated errors even when each evaluator improves (W3, W5). Report limitations, calibration, disagreements and safe aggregate results; do not disclose operational exploit details or compel access to protected assets. A flag must lead through a separately authorized decision and remediation pathway, with false alarms evaluated as well as missed hazards.

**Interoperable protocols** could specify task-relative evidence and testing requirements without imposing a single technique or universal human value objective. A common format can make comparisons easier while losing important distinctions. Document what a protocol covers, what it does not cover, how it is revised and how independent evidence can challenge it. Require equivalent nonmember pathways where applicable. Compliance is not a demonstrated guarantee of alignment.

**Distributed audit and monitoring** could trace public claims to implementation, with secure access only where authorized and with qualified independent assessment. Accreditation, appointment, conflicts, evaluator liability, remedy and oversight of the auditors themselves are part of the proposal. Proprietary information, security-sensitive findings and personal data should not become publicly available merely because an auditor can lawfully examine them. The commons must allow protected challenge and correction of its own findings.

**Compute monitoring** might help anticipate changes in scale or resource use, subject to independent validation. Compute is a proxy, not a direct measure of capability or safety; changing efficiency, model architecture and task requirements can alter the relationship. The value of reporting thresholds depends on the decision, the precision needed, uncertainty, evasion risk and actual legal authority. Use the least intrusive evidence consistent with the task, and assess privacy, competition and security effects before establishing a registry.

**Governance and accountability.** The body would need defined admission, revision, sanction, appeal and exit procedures, independent oversight and an accountable funding design. Developer participation supplies expertise but not an entitlement to govern its own assessment. §5.2 compares structural funding alternatives. Private membership rules are not statutory powers. Affected communities may contribute evidence or challenge but are not presumed to be represented or to endorse the proposal.

The May report's Mythos example described restricted partner access, safety documentation and a coordinated defensive programme. These are unverified leads, not an existing implementation of this Commons. The reported developer-controlled admission and protocol arrangements would be materially different from independently governed assessment. Apply the same withholding and durability tests as in §2.10, and evaluate any Commons against independent outcomes instead of treating this episode as confirmation.

### 3.4 Societal-Level: Deliberative Infrastructure for AI Governance

The original proposes standing citizens' assemblies, expert commissions and participatory technology assessment. These can serve different purposes. Assemblies might make affected people's concerns accessible and examine trade-offs that are not represented by revenue or technical benchmarks. Expert commissions might assess evidence, uncertainty and alternative responses. Participatory assessment might connect the technical design to lived consequences in a particular service or community. None speaks for everyone, and participation is not a substitute for lawful democratic authority.

Design bodies as advisory by default, with an obligation to consider and respond to recommendations only where validly adopted by the competent authority. Protect dissent, accessible testimony, participation by affected nonmembers, privacy and the right not to participate. Selection and representation may be contested; the proposal does not settle those disputes. Evaluate whether such processes improve decisions relative to existing public consultation, independent regulators, courts and other pathways. They may provide new information, but the authority to decide and implement must be specified separately.

The original points to citizens' assemblies on constitutional questions in Ireland and climate policy in France as precedents to verify, not as proof of effectiveness or transferability. It envisages a standing assembly with random selection, demographic coverage, facilitated small-group and plenary deliberation, expert testimony and technical support. The four illustrative agenda questions are acceptable uses of facial recognition, governance of autonomous weapons, distribution of gains from AI-driven automation, and conditions for deploying models above specified capability thresholds. These are examples for authorized deliberation, not predetermined answers. Developers, safety researchers, ethicists, economists, civil society and affected communities could contribute different evidence where participation is legitimate; the assembly would issue public, reasoned, nonbinding recommendations. Any mandatory response would require a valid mandate, and affected people outside the assembly must retain routes to challenge and remedy. Verify the precedents and compare this design with existing consultation, representative institutions and narrower deliberative arrangements.

### 3.5 International-Level: Fractal Coordination, Not Centralised Control

A three-timescale structure is retained as a design preference. The **fast layer** would facilitate timely exchange of safety-relevant evidence and response to particular technical incidents. Developers have relevant knowledge but cannot be the sole judges of their own compliance. Independent evaluators, competent regulators and protected dissent need distinct roles, and information sharing must be authorized and safe. The **medium layer** would address shared testing requirements, interoperability, mutual recognition and cross-border coordination through lawfully constituted arrangements. The **slow layer** would consider durable mandates, rights, treaty design and public accountability through legitimate political procedures. Each layer needs clarity about authority, responsibilities, escalation, costs and revision.

Different deadlines may justify different decision pathways; they do not prove that one controller cannot be adequate or that a frequency band determines jurisdiction. Evaluate existing national and international institutions before adding new bodies. International cooperation can be valuable even among strategic rivals, but competitor information sharing, collective exclusion, market access and export restrictions need specific legal assessment. Cooperation must not become an unauthorized agreement or displace legitimate public decision-making. The architecture can be modified or rejected if a less burdensome arrangement works.

For the medium layer the original names the United States, United Kingdom, European Union, China and other relevant actors as possible parties to arrangements over years or a decade. It proposes mutual recognition of evaluation and audit, common safety standards, coordinated hardware export controls and consultation or de-escalation during tension. These are design options subject to specific competent authorities, domestic implementation, existing regimes, competition law, export-control law, rights and nonparticipant effects; the report establishes no authority to implement them. For the slow layer it names the United Nations, G20 and OECD as possible institutional settings for deliberation on norms, distribution of benefits, risk and longer-term arrangements. Their actual mandates and powers differ and need verification. New treaty or institutional arrangements are not assumed necessary: the comparison must include existing mechanisms, targeted bilateral or multilateral arrangements, and legitimate nonadoption.

### 3.6 The Role of Open-Source and Open-Weight Models

The original contrasts open and proprietary development as possible contributors to institutional plurality. Open-source software, open weights, licensing conditions and structured access are not interchangeable. Some releases may allow independent research, local adaptation and challenge to concentrated market power; some may increase risks of misuse or irreversible dissemination. Closed access may restrict scrutiny while allowing some controls, and restricted access is not proof of safe use. These are release-specific comparisons, not intrinsic virtues or dangers.

Four proposed parameters are retained. **Graduated release protocols** could compare public release, staged access, restricted access and nonrelease against justified evidence thresholds, independent evaluation, reversibility and rights. Access revocation cannot recall copies already released. **Shared safety infrastructure** should permit appropriate independent evaluation of open and proprietary systems without forcing disclosure of protected information. **Liability frameworks** should assess lawful duties, causation, downstream responsibility, proportionality and remedies for particular harms; a threshold or membership status alone does not establish or remove liability. **International agreements** could address cross-border release and regulatory arbitrage within lawful jurisdiction, with fair access, equivalent pathways and review. Verify actual legal feasibility. Compare the risks of restricting useful research and concentrating power with the risks of unrestricted dissemination.

The design is not a universal release rule. A justified decision may differ across systems and contexts. The benefits of openness and independent challenge, the risks of irreversibility and the actual ability to respond are distinct endpoints for evaluation.

### 3.7 The Incentive Architecture for Commons Participation

The original suggests seven benefits to make participation attractive. Their value is conditional: the trap hypothesis itself says safety may impose costs under particular competitive conditions. A badge or benefit may reward compliance without improving safety (W7). The alternatives below are proposals for comparison, not powers of the proposed body. No blanket immunity, weaker reporting, lower evidence threshold or expedited unsafe release is acceptable. Equivalent lawful nonmember pathways must be considered, without punishment for nonmembership.

**Liability arrangements.** Consider whether lawfully adopted, narrow provisions for demonstrably responsible conduct would reduce uncertainty without undermining duties or remedies. Compare with ordinary liability and the deterrence rationale in §3.6. A private consortium cannot confer statutory immunity.

**Compute access advantages.** Consider whether allocations to justified safety research or other public-interest tasks could improve capability to assess and respond. Assess concentration, capability risks, access fairness, export controls, antitrust and public authorization. Compute priority cannot be justified solely by membership.

**Regulatory fast lanes.** Efficient processing and avoidance of duplicate evidence requests might reduce administrative burdens, subject to competent authority, unchanged substantive scrutiny and reporting, and equal access for equivalent compliance. A faster process is not authorization of an unsafe deployment.

**Procurement preferences.** Independent evidence of task-relevant safety and reliability might inform lawful procurement criteria. Compare effects on incumbency, small entrants, costs and alternative compliance. Certification cannot replace a buyer's safety assessment.

**Insurance advantages.** Validated risk information might improve underwriting and reward effective mitigation. Assess adverse selection, uncertainty about rare harms, incentives to underreport, and whether premiums reflect actual risks rather than membership.

**Reputational certification.** Certification may convey limited, verified information about a specified assessment. It is safety signalling and must therefore be evaluated under H5-like evidence standards, with expiry, correction, challenge and transparency about scope. It is not a general safety guarantee or a substitute for accountability.

**Treaty-linked benefits.** Competent authorities might negotiate specific lawful cooperation arrangements, subject to jurisdiction, competition, export controls, due process and nonmember rights. A voluntary consortium cannot grant treaty privileges or impose public sanctions. Avoid framing nonparticipation itself as grounds for heavier scrutiny.

For each instrument compare costs and benefits, distribution, moral hazard, concentration, safety outcomes, legal authority, remedies, review and sunset. The result may favour targeted benefits, ordinary compliance arrangements, or no incentive at all. Competitive advantage and safety coherence can be complementary or conflicting under different conditions; neither is an established result of this architecture.
