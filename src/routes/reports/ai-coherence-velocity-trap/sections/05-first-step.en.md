## 5. A Concrete First Step: The AI Commons Governance Protocol

### 5.1 The Logic of a Candidate First Step

The original proposed shared sensing as the most catalytic intervention, arguing that making risk visible would outflank the Deployment Imperative and make velocity self-limiting. That inference is withdrawn. The report's own episodes describe perceived concerns, attempted interventions and published research that did not necessarily lead to sustained action. Additional evidence could help where observation is inadequate, but an authority or implementation problem needs a different remedy.

The Commons is therefore a candidate intervention with a limited remit: shared authorized evaluation, evidence and coordination resources for specified tasks. Where implementation requires a response, participating organisations or competent authorities must separately establish legitimate decision rights, escalation and enforceable actions. For example, a predeclared evaluation finding might trigger a review by an authorised safety board under §3.2. It does not confer an automatic power on the Commons to halt deployment. If such authority and actuation arrangements are absent, the first step is observation-only and must be evaluated as such. Neither information nor participation proves systemic transformation.

The proposal should be compared with current national AI safety or security institutes, the EU AI Act's general-purpose AI provisions and related codes, industry fora and other existing arrangements (scope and dates to verify). Alternatives include targeted cooperation on particular evidence or incidents, independently funded evaluation, strengthening existing regulatory or corporate authority, and no additional intervention. Consider competence, proportionality, implementation cost, duplication and harm. The proposal's purpose and any pre-deployment testing conditions should be transparent rather than framed tactically to avoid resistance.

### 5.2 The AI Commons Governance Protocol

The Protocol would be a multi-stakeholder initiative under a defined legal form, with six concrete design features. The following proposal is not implemented and does not claim authorization or endorsement.

**Shared evaluation platforms.** Participating developers could submit systems for authorized pre-deployment assessments of specified properties. Independent and plural methods, calibration, holdouts and alternative evaluation pathways should remain possible. Safe aggregate findings and limitations could be published, with protected material available only to competent authorised parties. A system card is a source to assess, not proof of safety. The original Mythos document and restricted access narrative are research leads, not verified implementations.

**Interoperable alignment protocols.** Specify the scope of evidence, common reporting formats and task-specific testing, without mandating a single alignment method or objective for humanity. Preserve organisation-specific and independent evidence alongside the common format. Document disagreements, uncertainty, version changes and conditions where a protocol is not adequate. Verify compliance separately from independent outcome improvement.

**Distributed auditing infrastructure.** Qualified auditors could examine evidence of operational practice under appropriate authorization, minimisation and confidentiality. Findings, limitations and remediation should be independently contestable. The body must not rely solely on participant self-assessment or an evaluator paid and chosen to deliver a favourable result. Auditor appointment, removal, liability, independent oversight and remedy are part of the design.

**Compute monitoring.** A registry could report relevant resource-use indicators above justified thresholds if lawful and proportionate. Independently validate proxies and account for uncertainty, efficiency changes, evasion, privacy and security. No direct inference from compute to a capability or risk threshold is warranted. Reporting should support an actual specified decision, not surveillance for its own sake.

**Multi-stakeholder governance.** Define the body's contractual remit, professional staff, appointment procedures, independent oversight and finances, together with arrangements for affected people and nonmembers to submit evidence and contest decisions. Participation or testimony does not confer representative authority over others. The fast technical layer must not be solely controlled by the developers whose safety it assesses. Affected-community participation has not occurred. Selection and representation are contestable and must be addressed before implementation. No universal consent is implied.

**Graduated sanctions.** If lawfully authorized, membership rules might permit proportionate warnings, suspension or exclusion for substantiated violations, with notice, reasons, due process, review and appeal. Do not impose automatic sanctions on incomplete evidence. Exclusion must not imply public penalties or denial of ordinary market access without separate lawful authority. No penalty for lawful nonmembership. Test whether sanctions support remediation or discourage reporting.

The proposal must distinguish a voluntary association from statutory or treaty arrangements. A voluntary body's power is limited to its lawful contractual remit; public regulation, licensing, liability provisions or treaty conditions require action by competent authorities and cannot be granted by the body itself. ICAO and Montreal rely on treaty structures, while Basel standards are implemented through domestic arrangements (verify the exact mechanisms). They are comparators with different authority bases, not proof that a non-regulatory consortium can enforce public decisions.

#### Funding, assignment and independent challenge

Disclosure of issuer-pays or participant-fee conflicts is necessary but insufficient. Compare pooled or levy funding not tied to individual evaluation outcomes (with safeguards against capture of the pool); assignment independent of the evaluated party (with oversight of appointments); rotation (balanced against expertise and continuity); and public or philanthropic core funding under independent oversight (including political or donor influence). Alternatives may be combined or rejected. Compare residual conflicts, competence, cost, capture, continuity, accountability and enforceability. Specify transparent appointment, removal, independent budget, appeal, evaluator responsibility, review and remedy; audit the Commons and its auditors themselves. No funding scheme is presumed capture-proof. A certification institution that merely discloses its conflicts while leaving the incentive unchanged may recreate the safety-washing mechanism.

### 5.3 What This Intervention Could Address — and What It Cannot

Shared evaluation could reduce the cost of testing some hazards, allow comparison and help identify omissions in existing approaches. Interoperable protocols could make evidence easier to exchange, while preserving independent interpretation. Distributed audit could reveal a mismatch between claims and practice; compute monitoring might inform planning for specified resource changes. These are distinct claims with distinct outcomes. None implies that the identified risk will be understood, that decision-makers have authority to respond or that response is implemented. Conversely, an organisation might already possess adequate observation and need only a change to decision rights.

The mechanism of improvement requires an explicit chain: a valid signal, an appropriate interpretation, lawful escalation, a competent decision, available action, implementation and independent assessment. If any link is missing, sharing evidence may not improve outcomes. If the relevant action is irreversible, the timing of evidence and authority matters. Where the intervention only affects the first links, scope the claim accordingly; do not attribute the core mechanism of every governance failure to sensing. Evaluate alternatives that change later links, including the independent board, protected dissent, pre-authorised protective response and regulator pathways. The original's language of outflanking the Deployment Imperative is retired; aims, trade-offs and constraints must be stated transparently.

### 5.4 The Incentive Architecture

The seven original incentive options in §3.7 remain candidates for evaluation at the Protocol level. Compare each against ordinary arrangements, equivalent nonmember compliance and independent safety outcomes. Liability protection must not erase duties or remedies and must be reconciled with release-specific liability in §3.6. Regulatory processing may be streamlined only without reduced substantive reporting, weaker scrutiny or premature deployment. Compute allocation needs lawful authority, competition and capability-risk assessment; procurement criteria must be justified by task-relevant evidence and fair access. Insurance should price independently supported risk reduction rather than badge possession. Reputational certification must describe its limited scope and be open to correction, expiry and challenge. Treaty-linked benefits require lawful negotiation and cannot be conferred by a private body.

Membership benefits may be outweighed by costs, encourage badge-seeking, concentrate resources or exclude viable alternatives. The toy payoff in Appendix C illustrates why compliance can be individually attractive without effective mitigation. No participation benefit establishes entitlement to lower standards. Evaluate moral hazard, adverse selection, regulatory capture, pressure on nonmembers, effects on independent methods, and the feasibility of actual enforcement. Set review and sunset conditions, with complaint and remedy. The appropriate outcome may be no benefit, a narrower public-interest instrument or a different institution. The Protocol should not claim that it can transform safety into a competitive advantage by definition; H0 examines conditions under which that claim might hold.

### 5.5 How to Measure Success

Diagnosis H0–H5 must not be inferred from adoption of this proposal. Prespecify a separate evaluation protocol: baseline existing arrangements, independently governed alternative evaluation regimes, targeted coordination and where appropriate no change; task and affected population; evidence and uncertainty; independent outcome criteria, costs and risks. Account for selection, disclosure bias, confounding, attrition and dependence. Compare actual escalation and implementation, not only detection. No unsafe experiments or forced access to protected assets. Null, adverse and inconclusive results are valid. Suspend or redesign a pathway when safety, rights or authorization requirements fail.

The six original measures are retained but their evidential roles are revised.

**Participation rate.** Number of members and share of compute represented may indicate reach. Neither universal participation nor market concentration is success by itself. Record the viability, independence and access of nonmembers, entry/exit, concentration and failover. A smaller, plural network may be preferable.

**Volume and quality of shared safety evaluations.** Counts and coverage are process measures. Compare independent calibration, hazard-conditioned misses, false alarms, severity, cost, time and actual responses using justified criteria. Shared methods may raise correlated error; do not assume independence or use artificial operational risk to demonstrate performance.

**Adoption of interoperable alignment protocols.** Uptake measures process and compatibility, not safety. Assess whether the protocol retains task-relevant distinctions, whether alternative evidence remains admissible, and whether independent challenge changes decisions. No pressure to converge on a single interpretive model.

**Detection rate of safety-relevant anomalies.** Raw counts may reflect changing hazards, exposure, reporting incentives or sensitivity. Use prespecified denominators and independent evidence where feasible. Distinguish detection from appropriate escalation, authorised response and realized outcome; evaluate false alarms and missed concerns under actual aggregation rules (W5).

**Reduction in safety-washing incidents.** Use independently corroborated claim-to-practice mismatches under a common severity and ascertainment framework, including detected and unresolved cases. A fall in reports may reflect lower detection. A rise may reflect improved scrutiny. Compare actual remediation and independent challenge, not the number of favourable certificates.

**Market recognition of certification.** Investment, talent, procurement or insurance responses may indicate perceived value of a signal, not effective mitigation. Compare the signal with independent outcomes and check whether it creates a market barrier or a common blind spot. Do not target certification becoming a universal market standard.

Report harm, benefits, costs, uncertainty, rights and independent challenge together. Compare independent nonmembers without imposing participation. Success is a justified improvement in task-specific outcomes under lawful constraints, not more coordination, a higher count or a more widely recognized badge. Set correction, sunset, stop and nonadoption criteria before evaluation; an adequate baseline or failure of the candidate intervention may favour retention of existing arrangements.

### 5.6 The Honest Acknowledgment

The proposal faces institutional and geopolitical obstacles: competing interests, uncertain evidence, costs, trust, export controls, legal jurisdiction, and the possibility that a new body becomes captured or fails to add value. These are reasons to compare actual alternatives, not evidence of inevitable failure of existing governance. Decline can be legitimate when expected benefits do not justify burdens, authority is absent or another arrangement is adequate. Procedural absorption is also possible, for instance when an announced audit or review is repeatedly delayed or stripped of effective powers; H2 and H5 require independent evidence to distinguish these accounts. Do not equate delay, nonparticipation or disagreement with obstruction. The proposal is a wager about particular conditions, not a foregone conclusion about the future of AI governance.
