## Appendix B: International Analogues and Precedents

The following analogues were offered in the original as precedents. Their factual details and transferability remain verification leads, not existing implementations or existence proofs of the proposed AI Commons. Compare the tasks, authority bases, membership, incentives, rights, enforcement, actual outcomes and failure modes of each.

### B.1 The Internet Engineering Task Force: Commons Governance in Practice

The IETF is presented as a lead for voluntary technical standard-setting and interoperable protocols. Its working arrangements may illustrate how shared specifications can support cooperation without requiring identical implementation. Verify the actual processes and effects. Protocol interoperability is not equivalent to safety certification; firms may face different incentives, security constraints and public obligations. The example does not establish an adequate response to frontier AI hazards.

The original describes working groups, area directors, rough consensus, running code and the Internet Architecture Board. Check the respective roles rather than assigning a single ultimate authority without verification. The possible advantages of interoperable specifications are balanced by slow consensus, uneven participation and competing commercial or geopolitical interests. The relevant comparison is whether voluntary technical cooperation can support independent implementations for a specified task, not whether voluntary membership can replace lawful safety oversight.

### B.2 The International Civil Aviation Organisation: Safety Standards as Global Commons

The ICAO analogy concerns shared safety standards, reporting and cross-border cooperation. Verify its treaty basis, the status of standards and recommended practices, domestic implementation, reporting and oversight arrangements. Aviation and frontier AI may differ in hazard observability, reversibility, market structure and the authority to stop operations. The analogy is a source of institutional questions, not evidence that a voluntary AI consortium can exercise statutory powers.

The original also invokes audits and safety blacklists as graduated incentives. Verify which institutions actually conduct audits, impose restrictions and provide appeal, and distinguish ICAO standards from actions of states or regional authorities. The comparison is useful precisely because it raises the question of effective enforcement: a consortium's private exclusion cannot substitute for public authorization of a restriction.

### B.3 The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision: Financial Stability as Coordination Challenge

The original points to financial stability as a domain in which individual institutions can create cross-border effects. Shared standards and supervisory cooperation may help manage such effects. Verify the actual Basel arrangements, domestic implementation, legal authority and outcomes, including arbitrage and correlated responses. Banking standards and AI safety evaluation have different evidence, incentives and risks. A common standard may produce shared blind spots, not necessarily resilience.

The original's concern about leverage and competitive returns versus systemic stability can be restated as a conditional strategic feasibility question. Assess funding costs, supervisory powers, incentives to comply and distributional effects separately from adoption. A change in one bank's incentives may depend on how others are regulated, without establishing the need for any particular supranational institution.

### B.4 The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change: Shared Sensing Infrastructure

The IPCC is offered as an example of organized assessment and synthesis of diverse scientific evidence. Verify its institutional remit, assessment methods, treatment of uncertainty and relation to public policy. Its assessment role is distinct from authority to implement responses, illustrating why shared evidence is not itself action. An AI Commons would face different confidentiality, security and time constraints. Compare whether independent interpretation and dissent remain possible.

The original identifies breadth of scientific participation, assessment cycles and political contestation as features relevant to the Commons. Slow synthesis may be appropriate for some questions and inadequate for time-sensitive decisions. A common evidence report can support political action without replacing decision authority. Check whether the proposed AI assessment can preserve the competing interpretations and correction mechanisms that matter for a particular hazard.

### B.5 The Montreal Protocol: Anticipatory Governance Under Uncertainty

The original invokes the Montreal Protocol, dated 1987, as a lead for coordination before harms fully materialize. Verify dates, treaty obligations, adjustments, enforcement, finance and outcomes. Its authority derives from an international agreement and implementation by competent parties, not voluntary membership alone. Substitute technologies, measurable outcomes, jurisdiction and political feasibility may differ markedly from AI. The lesson is a question about conditions under which anticipatory coordination works, not proof that similar coordination is necessary or feasible here.

The original specifically names trade restrictions, a multilateral fund assisting developing countries and revision as knowledge evolves. Verify each feature, including the authority and distribution of costs. These are leads for comparing incentives, support for lower-resourced participants and adaptive rule-making; they are not permission to penalize lawful nonmembership in a private protocol.
