## Appendix D: Anticipated Objections

### D.1 "The AI industry is already regulating itself. Voluntary commitments and safety research are evidence of responsible governance."

They may be. Voluntary commitments, research, staged access and independent challenge can constitute meaningful governance resources. Their effect must be traced to decisions and independently supported outcomes. Existing arrangements may already be adequate for a specified task and may be preferable to adding a new body. H1 and H5 distinguish evidence and operational effect; H0–H2 permit successful restraint and durable reform. The proposed Commons must demonstrate incremental benefit under lawful constraints rather than assume voluntary governance fails. Conversely an announced commitment cannot alone establish operational adequacy.

### D.2 "The framework assumes that AI development is inherently dangerous. This is a precautionary bias."

The report makes no assumption that a material hazard necessarily exists or that a capability threshold will inevitably be crossed. Both the presence and absence of a task-specific hazard are empirical questions, as are its severity and uncertainty. Appropriate precautions require justification and proportionality. A lack of adequate evidence can justify further inquiry or a temporary authorized response under appropriate rules, but it is not proof of danger. Independent assessment and an adequate baseline may favour ordinary governance or no additional restrictions. Formal adequacy is conditional on a specified task, not a theorem of future harm.

### D.3 "A multi-scalar governance framework is unrealistic in the current geopolitical environment."

Geopolitical competition, export restrictions, jurisdiction, market concentration and trust can constrain cooperation. Some targeted cooperation may remain feasible; other arrangements may not. Verify existing institutions and legal powers, compare specific tasks and consider unilateral and joint feasibility separately. The strategic witness shows why a joint opportunity might exist without unilateral feasibility, not that it is stable, enforceable or lawful. A narrower agreement, existing public institution or unilateral reform may work better. No universal global authority or compulsory participation is proposed; legitimate nonadoption remains possible.

### D.4 "The analysis singles out specific organisations in ways that are unfair or incomplete."

The original presents archetypes, not validated findings. Its evidence may be incomplete, outdated or mistaken. The same nine-field rubric (§2.10) applies to all favourable and unfavourable claims, including organisational strengths. Formal and effective rights, observation, actual actions, reasons, independent outcomes, rival explanations and missing evidence must be distinguished. No ranking or inferred motives. Primary-source verification and human domain review with disclosed conflicts are binding before release of company-specific findings. The original and correction involve model families from companies analysed; AI cross-review cannot substitute for the human gate. Unsupported findings must be omitted or held as explicitly unverified leads.

### D.5 "The Anthropic Mythos decision undermines the report's thesis—it shows that an organisation can exercise restraint."

The reported restricted release is a possible counterexample to a claim that unilateral restraint cannot occur or remain viable. The same evidence test applies as for any organisation: actual decisions and access, attributed reasons, independently corroborated hazards and outcomes, financial/operational viability, rival conditions and durability. The May account and reviewer-supplied later-event leads are not verified; a security restriction or a system card alone is not enough. If sustained adequate restraint against accelerating rivals is established, it weakens unilateral impossibility within that scope. It does not itself demonstrate joint feasibility, the need for this Commons or the benefit of an alignment-first architecture. If restraint was temporary or externally required, that is a different explanation. The original uses this episode both as counterexample and support; the latter inference is withdrawn. No motive or safety-washing finding is inferred.

### D.6 "The Commons Governance Protocol would be captured by the organisations it is designed to monitor, just as previous voluntary initiatives have been."

Capture is a design risk, not an established inevitable result. The developers have important expertise and may also face conflicts when assessing themselves. Independent appointment, budgets, oversight, protected dissent, appeal, remedies and accountable audit are part of the comparison. Section 5.2 requires structural alternatives to direct participant-fee dependence, including pooled funding, independent assignment, rotation and independently overseen core funding. Disclosure is not enough; assess whether appointment and incentives actually permit adverse findings and correction. A body may lack effective authority even if it is formally independent. Compare existing arrangements and no new body.

#### A Design Caution

The original makes a distinct and valuable point: a commons may avoid institutional capture yet create **synchronised blindness**. If every evaluator adopts the same threat model, benchmarks and assumptions, a defect may propagate across all of them. Independent alternatives that can challenge a dominant frame may be more important than the number of members. Even improved individual evaluations can worsen joint detection if their errors become correlated (Appendix C, W5). Independent outcomes and actual aggregation rules matter; the toy probabilities are not actual evaluation risks. Common standards may instead support independent challenge, and this is a genuine disconfirmer of the hypothesized harm.

The original design maxim is retained: **consolidate the substrate, diversify the interpretation**. Share authorized resources, protocols and evidence where justified; preserve independently developed methods, distinct threat models, protected dissent, uncertainty and alternative institutions, including nonmembers. But the boundary itself is contestable: a shared evaluation set embeds choices about what matters. Audit the assumptions and coverage of the common resources, retain failover, allow exit and support correction. Avoid equating uptake, universal participation or a market-standard certificate with safety. The success criteria must not eliminate the alternatives that make correction possible. Review security, privacy, authority, concentration and costs before sharing. Neither capture avoidance nor diversity alone guarantees adequacy.
