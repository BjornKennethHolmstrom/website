## Executive Summary

The numerical, legal, institutional and historical particulars retained here are leads from the original AI-generated report, not independently verified findings. Its original narrative and proposed interventions are retained where possible, with corrections to formal inference, causal claims, attribution and rights safeguards. Appendix E records the original provenance verbatim. This is not an authorized policy programme or advice about investments, migration or individual legal cases. The accepted Korea and R4–R8 corrections inform the methodological distinctions, not empirical evidence for Bhutan.

**The Paradox**

The original report begins with a striking constitutional aspiration: the state shall strive to promote the conditions that enable the pursuit of Gross National Happiness (GNH). It describes nine domains of wellbeing, 33 indicators, sufficiency thresholds rather than ever-rising targets, a policy screening tool and Five-Year Plans with explicit GNH goals alongside economic, social and ecological ones. The breadth of national purpose is a valuable design lead, not proof of comparative superiority, measurement validity or inclusive implementation.

And yet. According to the original report (not independently verified), in 2023 the country's civil servants received a 50 percent rise in their minimum pay scale, meant explicitly to retain talented people the state was losing. Voluntary resignations had nearly quadrupled. Monthly departures through Paro International Airport had risen from fewer than 500 before the pandemic to more than 5,000. For two years, around two-thirds of the rise was financed by extraordinary dividends from the cryptocurrency operations of Druk Holding and Investments, the state's commercial investment arm—operations financed between 2020 and 2022 by US$539 million of central-bank resources, roughly a fifth of GDP, and publicly disclosed only in April 2023.

The original’s organizing interpretation of the episode brings together **exit** through comparison of opportunities (among other possible causes), **acceleration** through a rapid pay response using previously established investment capabilities, an **interface** between those institutions and Bhutan’s wider commitments, and a **timescale mismatch** between volatile revenue and a continuing wage obligation; the earlier mining investment predates the surge, public disclosure does not date actual review, and fiscal failure is not established (§1.1).

The central question is what it takes to sustain the constitution’s commitment to GNH as institutions capable of changing the country grow faster, larger and more autonomous; this requires evidence of actual alignment, legitimate objectives and plural perspectives rather than assumptions of sincerity or abandonment (§1.1).

**The Core Diagnosis: The Interface Race—Objective–Actuator Coupling Under Acceleration**

The **Interface Race** names a hypothesis: when a governance system expands specialised institutions under pressure to create opportunity, the task-specific interfaces connecting their narrower mandates to wider legitimate objectives may need to adapt. The original chronology suggests possible late review and efforts at institutional maturation. Whether these arrangements are adequate in practice remains open; neither a general lag nor successful catch-up is demonstrated.

An *actuator* is any institution that can change the state of the country: a ministry, a state investor, a central bank, a hydropower authority, an autonomous economic zone. An *interface* is whatever connects its proximate mandate to the whole objective: an assessment before commitment, a legal duty, an audit, a published standard, an independent reviewer. Delegation to narrow mandates is not a flaw; it is how modern states function. The risk is temporal: some decision classes may acquire new risks or deadlines without adequate corresponding review and response. No scalar rate comparison is implied.

In formal terms, a richly specified objective is not an observation architecture. Neither nine domains nor 33 indicators determines what states can be reconstructed, and formal controllability does not mean an objective has authority over institutions. The questions are distinct: what can legitimately be known, what task is assigned, who may act, which responses are feasible, and whether they can be implemented in time (W1–W3, Appendix C).

This report does not claim that Bhutan has abandoned GNH for growth, that its economic institutions are uncontrolled, or that the race is being lost or won; reported charters, governance and audit arrangements are evidence leads, not proof of effective controls. The rate comparison is diagnostic shorthand only: task-specific risks, deadlines and legitimate responses must be assessed separately (§1.3; W3).

**The Signature Pattern: The Compare–Exit–Accelerate–Recouple Loop**

The original’s historical series shorthand (Japan’s preserves, Sweden’s drifts, Finland’s stalls, Korea’s competes) contrasts with Bhutan’s proposed **open-ended race, not a necessary closed loop**; these are configuration hypotheses, not national rankings, and the accepted Korea Tournament Trap is a domain-level hypothesis about early rankings and later opportunities, not a cultural predisposition to compete (§1.2).

**Compare.** Education, wellbeing and access to alternatives may change reference points. Family, study, domestic opportunities and civil-service transformation are plausible additional causes; aggregate wellbeing does not identify individual preferences.

**Exit.** The original reports that the Bhutanese population in Australia roughly doubled between 2020 and 2024, and that 53 percent of recent migrants hold degrees and nearly half previously worked in government. Mobility opportunities following border reopening, networks and career or family choices also matter. Voluntary migration is not itself failure.

**Accelerate.** The original proposes a response through the 13th Plan’s economic driving force, DHI’s tenfold growth mandate and Gelephu, an autonomous city described as covering a tenth of the country. These may also reflect prior diversification or independent policy aims. The mining programme is earlier; pay revision is a candidate response to attrition.

**Recouple.** New capabilities may require different controls; reported DHI ESG and Gelephu integrity and audit arrangements are candidate adaptations. The legal reach of national environmental law and actual application of interfaces need competent review.

The pattern does not close by itself. Timely, effective and lawful interfaces could support development; inadequate interfaces might leave important effects neglected. Existing distributed arrangements or modest changes may suffice, and constraints may remain adequate despite rapid expansion. Which outcome occurs requires task-specific evidence, not aggregate rates or an assumed motive.

**The Open-System Sufficiency Gap**

The original proposes that earlier development could contribute to later opportunity pressure. Its survey and earnings leads (unverified) are as follows. In 2022, people aged 15 to 39 recorded the highest GNH Index of any age group. Yet the same group showed the sharpest deterioration in working hours, sleep, mental health and negative emotions, and urban wellbeing stagnated despite rising resources. Migrants typically earned under Nu 40,000 a month at home and far more abroad. A sufficiency architecture asks whether people have enough; the emigrant asks whether what they have is as good as what they could have elsewhere. In an open country, being sufficiently well off and considering exit are not contradictory states.

These are aggregate survey and earnings leads, not evidence that actual migrants were the highest-GNH respondents or that psychological distress caused migration. Stated intentions differ from movements; benefits of mobility, return and nonreturn remain legitimate. W6 and H2/H4.

**The Evidence: A Heterogeneous Architecture**

The original census suggests heterogeneous arrangements, not a general disconnection. It describes ordinary budget/plan integration, environmental assessment, DHI’s charter and audit, its April 2026 ESG launch, and a narrow central-bank mandate. The reported tourism adjustment from US$200 to US$100 a night retained the high-value principle; it motivates a test of adaptation against counterfactual tourism, employment and environmental outcomes. Law, formal review and published rules are not evidence of actual application. The dated launches suggest candidate changes, not demonstrated pace or lag. Confidential review may be adequate.

**The Boundary: Whose Happiness?**

The original raises the prior question of whose flourishing GNH represents: it reports that around 100,000 Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa left Bhutan or were expelled in the early 1990s amid citizenship and cultural-integration policies, with contested accounts, evidence and allegations of forced displacement and abuses, continuing citizenship questions and human-rights proceedings. Its separately attributed UNHCR figures for people remaining in Nepal and resettled abroad are distinguished in the table above and §2.8; these accounts require verification and no individual legal status is adjudicated. The original does not thereby claim manipulation of present GNH statistics. Its principle is that “the dimensionality of an objective cannot compensate for a contested population boundary”: resident-survey validity, legal membership, rights and normative inclusion are distinct, and safe affected-community participation cannot be replaced by additional indicators (§1.7; §2.8).

**The Series Boundary Condition**

The earlier series used first- and second-order diagnoses as conceptual labels. The accepted Korea correction asks whether reform activity can coexist with opportunity constraints in specified domains; it withdraws inherited national observation, capacity and trend premises. Bhutan offers a contrasting hypothesis about connections between declared objectives and expanding institutions. Finland’s throughput analogy is likewise a lead, not proof of national differences. Neither the objective’s legitimacy nor interface adequacy is presumed.

**The Opportunity**

The proposal is selective coupling rather than a universal GNH screen: proportionate review of material risks and rights before irreversible commitment, transparent rules, protected access and independent challenge. Existing arrangements and no new layer are alternatives. The original proposes GNH materiality in DHI, investment boundaries, fiscal buffers, Gelephu reporting and migration supports; each requires independent testing and authorization. A selective framework must disclose its own omissions and cannot trade away rights.

The original calls GNH materiality the transition’s “Trojan Horse”, using familiar ESG language for national wellbeing considerations. This is historical rhetoric only: no concealed purpose or presumed acceptance. It identifies the Gelephu GNH and ecological baseline before major development as the most time-sensitive step. Verify actual development timing and existing data, and require safe, lawful participation; urgency does not justify rushed collection (§5.3).

**The Concrete First Step: The Actuator Interface Register**

The proposed Actuator Interface Register would record mandates, power, ex-ante interfaces, oversight, transparency, correction records and coverage for specified institutions. Its candidate maintenance arrangement is constitutional audit with methodological support and annual parliamentary reporting. A map of known, unknown, nonapplicable and actual interfaces is not a composite score, a new mandate or proof of adequacy. Evaluate accuracy, burden and usefulness against existing arrangements.

**The Honest Conclusion**

The original raises a prospective question, not a finding of failure or success. It identifies possible adaptations, five predictions and three candidate pilots. No claim of good motives, absence of opposition or comparative excellence follows from public statements or disclosure. Rights, membership, independent challenge and voluntary mobility remain protected. The default is unknown.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> Bhutan's first great achievement was to decide what a country should value. Its next may be to show that a society can build fast, powerful institutions without letting them forget what they are for.

This closing aspiration is retained as rhetoric, not an established comparative achievement or a prediction. See the coda.
