## 1. The Interface Race

---

### 1.1 Opening: The Raise Paid in Bitcoin

According to the original report (not independently verified), in the summer of 2023, Bhutan's civil servants received the largest pay rise in the country's modern history. The Sixth Pay Commission, constituted that February, recommended raising the minimum pay scale by half, with especially large allowances for doctors and nurses. Its report was unusually candid about the purpose. The revision was meant not only to protect living standards but to "motivate and retain talented people in public service." The state was losing them. Voluntary resignations from the civil service, which had averaged around 64 a month in the years before mid-2022, had risen to around 234 a month. In the financial year 2022–23, 3,413 civil servants resigned voluntarily, and the attrition rate reached 16 percent. Teachers and health workers led the exodus. Many of them were bound for Australia.

The stated retention aim and reported resignations do not identify individual reasons or departures as unique emigrants. The 2022 civil-service transformation and border reopening coincided with the surge in the original chronology (Appendix F).

According to the original report (not independently verified), the raise cost roughly Nu 6 billion a year—about 2.3 percent of GDP, a permanent addition to the recurrent budget of a small state whose ordinary revenues could not easily absorb it. The Pay Commission's financing plan drew on a source few outside Bhutan would have guessed: a special transfer of Nu 4 billion a year for two years from Druk Holding and Investments, the state's commercial investment arm. The International Monetary Fund later identified where much of that money came from. For those two years, extraordinary dividends from DHI's cryptocurrency operations financed, on average, around two-thirds of the wage increase. Bhutan had been mining bitcoin with its hydropower.

According to the original report (not independently verified), the mining programme was not a response to the exodus. It predated it. Between August 2020 and April 2022, the Royal Monetary Authority had acquired some US$539 million of DHI securities to finance the initial investment—a sum equivalent to roughly a fifth of GDP and around 40 percent of the country's foreign reserves at the time. Mining began in 2022. The programme became publicly known only in April 2023, after the commitment had been made. It later drew electricity that would otherwise have been exported to India, and the IMF would warn against letting the volatility of crypto income transmit into recurrent government spending.

These figures distinguish financing instruments, reserve exposure, public disclosure and investment commitment. Internal approvals, dates, energy allocation, actual returns and alternatives must be checked separately; later risk adjustment need not have been caused by publicity or IMF review.

The original offers two readings of this opening episode: improvisation under pressure to retain public servants, and a major decision that, in its account, the public and national assessment framework saw only after commitment; it asserts that “Both readings are true.” That assertion remains unverified, including the timing of actual review. Separately, Appendix F.3 presents four interpretations: strategic diversification, macroeconomic risk, governance and transparency, and adaptive correction.

The original describes the episode as containing the whole of the report: **exit**, as some educated people compare opportunities; **acceleration**, as the state seeks ways to respond; **interface**, connecting the institutions involved to wider commitments; and a **timescale mismatch**, as extraordinary revenue finances a continuing obligation. In the original chronology mining preceded the migration surge: the later pay revision, not the initial mining investment, is the candidate response to attrition. Domestic civil-service reform and border reopening are competing explanations of the surge. Public disclosure after commitment does not establish the absence of confidential ex-ante review. A financing mismatch is a risk to investigate, not a demonstrated fiscal failure.

The question is not settled by asserting either that Bhutan has abandoned GNH or that it sincerely embraces it. It is how stated commitments, legitimate objectives and effective interfaces develop as institutions acquire new capabilities; actual practice and plural perspectives require independent evidence.

### 1.2 The Compare–Exit–Accelerate–Recouple Loop

The historical series used shorthand: Japan’s preserves, Sweden’s drifts, Korea’s competes. These are hypotheses about institutional configurations, not verified national rankings or cultural predispositions. The accepted Korea correction treats the Tournament Trap as a domain-level hypothesis about how early rankings affect later opportunity; it explicitly disclaims cultural predisposition. Bhutan’s candidate pattern is different: an open-ended race, not a necessary closed loop.

**Compare.** The original report hypothesises broad multidimensional sufficiency for much of the population. Its national surveys found young adults to be, on aggregate, the happiest age group in the country. But the same success—mass education, rising capability, an open border—gives its most educated citizens a new reference point. Some may compare their lives not only with parents or neighbours but with attainable alternatives abroad, where the original reports higher earnings and varied careers. Whether the domestic sufficiency measures capture these relative evaluations, and whether the evaluations affect actual choices, requires evidence.

The aggregate and reference-point hypothesis do not establish who migrated or why; domestic reform, family and study preferences, reopening borders and other conditions are genuine rivals.

**Exit.** Comparison may contribute to movement. Monthly departures through Paro International Airport rose from fewer than 500 before the pandemic to more than 5,000 in early 2023. The Bhutanese population in Australia roughly doubled between 2020 and 2024, from about 12,400 to more than 25,300. The original reports substantial selection by qualifications: 53 percent of recent migrants hold university degrees, against around 7 percent of the working-age population, and nearly half previously worked in government. Departures and attrition records may provide more frequent evidence than the reported GNH survey waves every five to seven years. Their interpretation and response requirements differ.

Airport departures are events, not unique emigrants; the figures, cohort and denominators require verification. Migration may be beneficial and voluntary, not a failure criterion.

**Accelerate.** The original interprets economic expansion as a response to perceived opportunity constraints; pre-existing diversification and other objectives are rivals. The 13th Five-Year Plan makes economic transformation its "driving force," explicitly to "re-balance" a Gross National Happiness whose economic dimension had lagged its social, cultural and environmental ones. It sets targets of a US$5 billion economy by 2029 and a "High-Income GNH Economy" by 2034. DHI has launched a "10X Journey" aiming at tenfold corporate growth within a decade and has described an investment horizon in the tens of billions of dollars. And in the south, the Gelephu Mindfulness City—a special administrative region covering roughly a tenth of the country, with its own executive, legislature and judiciary—has been created to attract the investment and jobs the old economy could not.

The reported targets, investment ambitions and legal powers are historical leads, not actual achieved capacity or evidence of necessity.

**Recouple.** Institutional expansion may change task-specific review requirements; existing interfaces may already be adequate. The original identifies candidate steps. DHI launched a group-wide environmental, social and governance system with support from the International Finance Corporation in April 2026, about eight months after its 10X programme began. The Gelephu Mindfulness City is building its own integrity and audit institutions, has signed a cooperation agreement with the national Anti-Corruption Commission, and falls under a statutory audit arrangement being prepared by the Royal Audit Authority; the original reads national law as potentially applying where the city’s own law does not displace it, subject to competent legal review.

Each launch, arrangement and legal interpretation requires verification, including whether the default-law provision covers the actual decision. No conclusion about effective independence, constraint or keep-pace follows.

**The race.** The pattern does not close by itself. Timely, effective and lawful interfaces could support development; inadequate interfaces might leave important effects neglected. Existing distributed arrangements or modest changes may suffice, and constraints may remain adequate despite rapid expansion. Which outcome occurs requires task-specific evidence, not aggregate rates or an assumed motive. The bitcoin case motivates a test of timing and fiscal exposure; the newer arrangements motivate a test of institutional maturation. Actual review and action may differ from public chronology.

### 1.3 The Interface Race Defined

The Interface Race names a hypothesis: when a governance system expands specialised institutions under pressure to create opportunity, the task-specific interfaces connecting their narrower mandates to wider legitimate objectives may need to adapt. The original chronology suggests possible late review and efforts at institutional maturation. Whether these arrangements are adequate in practice remains open; neither a general lag nor successful catch-up is demonstrated.

Three terms in that definition carry the argument.

An **actuator** is any institution that can change the state of the country: a ministry spending its budget, a state investment company deploying capital, a central bank managing reserves, a hydropower authority building dams, a special economic zone writing its own commercial law. Modern states need many actuators, and they need many of them to be specialised. Narrow central-bank and commercial mandates can be appropriate when wider responsibilities are legitimately assigned elsewhere. Delegation to narrower mandates is not a flaw. It is how complex states get things done.

An **interface** is whatever connects an actuator's proximate mandate to the whole objective: a legal duty, an assessment before commitment, an independent reviewer, a published standard, an audit, a parliamentary hearing, a board that answers to someone who holds the wider view. Interfaces differ in what they cover, in whether they act before or after a decision, in how binding they are, and in how long they take to correct a mistake. An interface that acts after an irreversible commitment is still an interface, but it behaves very differently from one that acts before.

The **race** concerns changes in the required and actual interfaces over time, including task deadlines, risks, jurisdiction, delegation, staffing and reversibility. Capital, speed, authority, review capacity and coverage have different units; no scalar comparison is implied. Existing effective controls may remain adequate even as power expands.

This report does not claim that Bhutan has abandoned GNH for growth, that its institutions are uncontrolled, or that the race is being lost or won. The charters, governance and audit arrangements described in the original are evidence leads, not proof of effective controls.

### 1.4 The GNH Experiment

The following institutional design features are retained from the original as leads. Their actual application, validity, and comparison with other countries require evidence.

The original distinguishes the constitutional aspiration from two common caricatures: GNH as a pleasant slogan, or maximising happiness instead of income. The constitutional commitment is carefully worded: the state shall strive to promote the *conditions* that enable the *pursuit* of Gross National Happiness. It is not a quantity to be maximised but a set of conditions to be secured, leaving the pursuit itself to people's own lives. This is a stated enabling objective rather than a formula; the legitimacy and application of its interpretation remain open.

The original describes its measurement design as follows (unverified). The national GNH surveys operationalise the concept through nine domains—psychological wellbeing, health, time use, education, cultural diversity and resilience, good governance, community vitality, ecological diversity and resilience, and living standards—and 33 indicators. Each domain carries equal weight. Most unusually, each indicator is assessed against a *sufficiency* threshold. A person either has enough of something or does not; having more than enough adds nothing further to their score. The national index therefore asks whether people have enough across enough of the dimensions of a good life, not how much of any single thing they can accumulate.

Within a binary sufficiency indicator, exceeding the threshold does not add further credit on that indicator. This narrower property may reduce one incentive to escalation; incentives to change other indicators, classifications, thresholds or measured populations remain. Sufficiency does not establish tournament immunity, actual wellbeing, or a legitimate trade-off of protected rights (W4).

According to the original report (not independently verified), the architecture reaches beyond measurement. Bhutan developed a policy screening tool to test proposed policies against the GNH domains before adoption, and the historical record contains at least one striking case in which screening appears to have changed a national decision: according to GNH institutions, officials initially inclined toward joining the World Trade Organization reversed their assessment after applying the tool, and accession did not proceed at the time. (Negotiations resumed in 2026; whether and how the tool was applied to that decision is not on the public record.) Five-Year Plans carry explicit GNH targets. The Constitution requires the country to maintain at least 60 percent forest cover for all time; actual cover stands at around 70 percent.

The WTO account retains its attribution to GNH institutions; verify primary records and rival explanations. The renewed accession process and constitutional forest floor and actual cover are separate verification leads. Public ambiguity does not mean absence of assessment.

The original invokes Paper VI’s former objective-as-observation premise. That inference is withdrawn. Normative breadth may be valuable without proving empirical coverage, measurement quality, access, interpretation or legitimate use. GNH’s specified domains and planning targets are distinct from observation and response capacity (W1–W2). The question is how institutions connect legitimate objectives to feasible action.

### 1.5 Bhutan and Korea: Mirror Problems

The accepted Korea correction states: “The Tournament Trap is a candidate mechanism to investigate, not an explanation of an established national gap trend.” It examines how a university hierarchy, a divided labour market and a concentrated capital region may connect early rankings to adult opportunity, without implying national response capacity, cultural predisposition or objective collapse. Earlier reform examples are unverified leads; compare stakes-first, selection-first and combined reforms.

The original calls Bhutan almost a mirror image: a comparatively broad declared objective and sufficiency design, with possible interface challenges as specialised institutions expand. That is an attributed analogy about different hypotheses, not a measured contrast in observability, national capacity or moral worth. Both cases require inquiry into objectives, legitimacy, actual evidence and implementation.

The comparison also motivates the question of external opportunity: threshold-based domestic measurement need not encode attainable foreign alternatives. A resident may meet a sufficiency threshold and freely choose work or study abroad. Neither national society is reducible to a tournament; the aggregate cannot identify actual migration selection. No cultural predisposition is inferred.

The historical Finland throughput analogy poses a different question about the capacity to change existing arrangements. Bhutan’s candidate challenge is ensuring that legitimate accountability and response remain adequate as specialised institutions change. Neither constitutes a demonstrated national failure or a ranking.

### 1.6 Bhutan's Genuine Strengths

The original identifies favorable historical and institutional leads (unverified). Within two generations, a country that was among the least developed in the world built universal basic education and health care, extended life expectancy dramatically, electrified its valleys with renewable hydropower, and became carbon negative while keeping around 70 percent of its land under forest. In 2008 it made a peaceful transition from absolute to constitutional monarchy—initiated from the throne rather than forced upon it—and has since held competitive elections in which power has changed hands between parties.

Universal service coverage, health and life-expectancy gains, carbon-negative status, electrification, forest cover, the 2008 transition and alternation of power are all subject to the same verification standards as adverse claims.

The original identifies the tourism adjustment as a possible example of adaptive policy. When the tourism model it adopted after the pandemic—a Sustainable Development Fee raised from US$65 to US$200 a night—produced a recovery weaker than hoped, the government cut the effective fee in half in 2023 while explicitly retaining the high-value, low-volume principle behind it. The original treats this as an illustration of adjusting a parameter while retaining a stated purpose. Independent evidence of tourism demand, employment, fiscal, community and ecological outcomes, including alternatives, is required. Its environmental assessment law applies across the whole kingdom and requires review before commitment. Its audit and anti-corruption institutions have constitutional standing.

The reported ESG system and Gelephu accountability arrangements suggest candidate institutional adaptation. Their existence does not establish awareness, motives, independent review or effective constraint. The original’s “Few countries” comparison requires comparable cases; it is not a demonstrated finding about relative pace.

### 1.7 The Real Question

The original offers a prospective transition hypothesis rather than a diagnosis of an established self-reinforcing trap. Both effective existing arrangements and inadequate interfaces remain genuine possibilities.

The report hypothesises that efforts to expand opportunities may increase the need for timely, proportionate interfaces. The crypto case raises questions of reserve exposure, electricity allocation and recurrent finance; the public timing does not show when actual review occurred. Later institutional developments motivate tests of adaptation. Neither lag nor catch-up has been established.

The prior question is whose flourishing the objective legitimately represents. The original records around 100,000 Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa arriving in refugee camps after citizenship and cultural-integration policies in the early 1990s, with competing accounts of departure and documented allegations and evidence of forced displacement and abuses. Section 2.8 preserves those source-specific accounts and the separate UNHCR camp and resettlement figures. Resident-survey validity, citizenship and rights are distinct; none establishes manipulation of GNH statistics. Rights and affected communities’ participation cannot be replaced by additional indicators.

The central question, then, is not whether Gross National Happiness works, nor whether Bhutan is secretly maximising growth. It is this:

> **Can the architecture that defines what Bhutan values evolve as quickly as the institutions being built to change what Bhutan can do?**

The original closes this chapter with the aspiration of keeping declared values connected to changing institutions and opportunities. That aspiration does not require people to stay, nor establish comparative superiority.

### 1.8 Observational diagnosis protocol

H0 — Task/ownership fit: specify lawful tasks, affected people, acceptable outcomes and horizon; compare distributed responsibilities and existing modest reforms. Adequate narrow mandates or effective existing interfaces weaken a gap claim.

H1 — Evidence-to-action chain: distinguish normative domain, measurement frame/reliability, access, interpretation, relevant review, binding authority, resources, implementation and outcomes. Establish missing link and consequence rather than assuming that a policy screen or absence of disclosure decides it. Effective confidential review, justified approval and successful correction disconfirm relevant deficits.

H2 — Compare–Exit–Accelerate–Recouple: test separately (i) capability/aspirations and perceived alternatives (rival domestic constraints, family/study preferences, 2022 civil-service transformation and reopening of borders, F:11/05:11); (ii) comparison to actual voluntary migration (rival mobility barriers, circulation, career prospects, recognition, work–life balance and other reasons beyond pay, 02:105/03:102); (iii) migration to policy acceleration (rival pre-existing diversification, hydropower opportunity, global demand and other policy aims; mining predates the exit surge, the later pay revision is the original response candidate, not a demonstrated causal effect); (iv) expansion to relevant interface requirements and actual timely performance (rival existing effective controls, proportionate delegation); (v) response to future opportunity and migration (rival beneficial migration, return, new conditions). Any necessary link missing defeats the proposed complete mechanism in the tested setting; no automatic closed loop or forced retention goal.

H3 — Interface timing and fiscal exposure: predeclare decision classes, risks, opportunity costs, mandates and meaningful deadlines; trace review, commitment, disclosure and correction independently. Test fiscal sustainability against structural revenue, buffers, alternate financing and stress scenarios; compare earlier/later and quiet as well as visible cases. Effective ex-ante confidential review, sustainable finance or no preventable damage weaken relevant concerns; successful investment alone does not establish good process, poor returns alone do not establish bad process.

H4 — Boundary, sufficiency and legitimacy: distinguish survey estimand from normative inclusion and legal rights, examine evidence with residents, refugees and other affected people on voluntary safe terms; test group differences, missingness, subjective preferences and multiple dimensions. No score can resolve citizenship or rights. Test legitimacy and informal coordination as contextual hypotheses with contrary accounts, due process and independent challenge. No inference from absence of public dissent or from cultural label. Boundary is prior and independent of race; not a causal claim that displacement arose from current GNH measurement.

H5 — Friction and reflexive accountability: examine speed, confidentiality, capacity, deference, vested interests and remedy-side interests through actual mandates, incentives, decision records and evidence. Effective confidential review, beneficial discretion, independent criticism, successful reform or justified rejection are genuine alternatives. Criticism of this report, the Register or GaE is not evidence of friction or disloyalty.

For H0–H5 predeclare case frame (including routine decisions, adverse and favorable cases, missing and protected records), source quality, indicators, thresholds, time window, attribution limits, disconfirmers and independent evaluation. Adoption, rejection, success or failure of a proposed intervention does not confirm diagnosis. Default unknown; no finding from lack of available evidence.
