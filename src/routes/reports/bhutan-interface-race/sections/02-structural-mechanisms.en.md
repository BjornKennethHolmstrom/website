## 2. The Interface Race: Structural Mechanisms

---

### 2.1 What "Interface Capacity" Means

The task is to assess whether institutions perform their legitimate assigned roles and whether their interfaces provide adequate evidence, review and response. The objective itself, including its boundary and plural values, remains contestable.

An interface is anything that connects an institution's immediate mandate to the whole national objective. Its capacity can be described along seven properties, and each of them matters independently:

**Coverage.** Which dimensions of the national objective does the interface actually bring to bear? An environmental assessment covers ecology and some social impacts; a financial audit covers legality and prudence; a GNH screen, in principle, covers all nine domains.

**Timing.** Does the interface act before commitment, during implementation, or after the fact? An assessment before a dam is approved behaves very differently from an audit after it is built.

**Binding strength.** Is the interface advisory, procedural, able to veto, or constitutional?

**Transparency.** Can the public, Parliament or independent reviewers see the trade-off being made while it can still be changed?

**Independent oversight.** Is there someone outside the institution with the standing to question it?

**Correction latency.** How long does it take, once something has gone wrong, for the system to notice and respond?

**Reversibility.** Once a decision has been taken, how feasible is it to undo?

None of these properties has to be at its maximum for every institution. A central bank with its reserve decisions exposed to daily political review would be worse, not better. The engineering question is whether the interfaces around each institution are matched to its power: to the scale, speed, autonomy and irreversibility of what it can do. Interface capacity is therefore a relationship, not a quantity. It asks whether the connection is strong enough for the actuator it connects.

These seven properties are separate, task-specific descriptors. Evidence quality, actual application, lawful authority, independent challenge, execution and outcomes cannot be inferred from published rules. Privacy and protective limits are not deficiencies; neither maximal coverage nor maximal speed is a universal goal.

### 2.2 Observability Without Automatic Controllability

In a specified control model, observability concerns reconstructibility from measurements and controllability concerns reachability using admissible controls. These definitions require a model and assumptions. Institutional evidence, authority, response capacity and execution are related but different concepts; their analogues do not inherit a theorem by naming them.

The earlier Paper VI formulation treated an objective as an observation architecture. The accepted correction withdraws this equivalence. An objective can name something not measured, and measurements may concern things outside the objective. See W1.

The original reports nine domains, 33 indicators, sufficiency thresholds and planning targets: a broad normative and measurement design. Its validity, frame, access, reliability and use remain to be evaluated. No national observability ranking follows.

The Interface Race asks whether changing institutions have legitimate task-specific authority, feasible responses, resources and timely execution linked to appropriate evidence and objectives. Narrow mandates may be adequate when another actor owns the task. An objective cannot steer anything on its own; neither sincere statements nor formal authority establish effective action (W2).

The conceptual claim is limited to the separation of objective, observation and action. No implication of insincerity or benevolence, national controllability or necessary strengthening at every institution follows.

### 2.3 The Open-System Sufficiency Gap

External comparisons may be one source of opportunity pressure, alongside domestic civil-service changes, reopening borders, family, study and career preferences. The mechanism is a hypothesis (H2), not a claim that pressure originates only outside Bhutan.

According to the original report (not independently verified), the 2022 national GNH survey found that younger Bhutanese were, on the survey's own measure, doing comparatively well. People aged 15 to 39 recorded the highest GNH Index of the three age bands, at 0.797, and the highest share classified as happy, at 51.1 percent, against 45.7 percent for those aged 40 to 64 and 40.7 percent for those over 65. They had gained the most in formal education, knowledge and income since 2015.

According to the original report (not independently verified), the same survey recorded warning signs. The younger group showed the largest deterioration since 2015 in working hours, sleep, mental health and negative emotions. Urban Bhutan—where most of the educated young live—saw its GNH Index fall from about 0.811 to 0.796 and its share of happy people fall from 54.6 to 50.5 percent, even as rural Bhutan improved. The survey's authors described urban progress as stagnant despite greater access to economic resources and services. The reported living-standards indicators were relatively favorable: all three living-standards indicators showed sufficiency above 71 percent, and among people classified as happy, living standards contributed more to sufficiency than any domain except health.

The original next describes the migration surge (not independently verified). A World Bank survey of 1,208 households in 2024 found that migrants typically earned less than Nu 40,000 a month in Bhutan and at least Nu 60,000 abroad; some migrants and aspiring migrants said they would need domestic earnings above Nu 100,000 a month to reconsider—pay comparable to Bhutan's highest earners. The drivers the survey identified were earnings, career progression, education abroad and the networks that make migration easier.

According to the original report (not independently verified), the survey's own authors, constructing illustrative profiles from their subgroup data, sketched a composite young, urban, working woman whom the index classifies as "extensively happy"—and who, if circumstances do not improve, intends to go to Australia. The profile is synthetic, not a real respondent. It illustrates logical compatibility, not a real migrant or observed joint distribution. In an open country, being sufficiently well off and considering exit are not contradictory states.

The synthetic profile, aggregate age-band means, and survey of households cannot identify selection or causation. No inference that distress or a particular characteristic causes migration is warranted. Test response selection, comparable cohorts and alternatives; voluntary nonresponse and migration are legitimate (W6).

The **Open-System Sufficiency Gap** is the candidate mechanism that residents meeting domestic sufficiency criteria may nevertheless evaluate attainable opportunities elsewhere. The original notes that satisfaction, emotions, time use and governance already capture some relevant circumstances; whether relative comparison is unmeasured or actually affects choices is an empirical question. A threshold and a preference are not identical. H2 and W4/W6 distinguish these.

Education can increase both wellbeing and options to move. A country may improve its measured indicators while people voluntarily leave. Whether education and external comparisons account for particular movements, or other circumstances do, remains open. Retention is not by itself an adequate objective.

### 2.4 Multi-Rate Sensing

According to the original report (not independently verified), the national GNH survey is an unusually rich instrument, but it is slow. Its national waves ran in 2010, 2015 and 2022; the most recent fieldwork took place between April and July 2022. The emigration surge accelerated after that fieldwork ended. A slow survey alone may miss changes occurring between waves; actual evidence, alternatives and deadlines determine adequacy.

According to the original report (not independently verified), Bhutan's government did not wait for the next survey. The 13th Five-Year Plan's economic turn draws on faster signals: youth unemployment, financial insecurity, the structural weakness of the economy and, explicitly, the outflow of educated citizens. It then frames the response as a rebalancing of Gross National Happiness—a correction to its lagging economic dimension.

A combination of slower survey evidence and faster administrative or economic signals may be useful, but neither its existence nor heterogeneous timescales proves adequacy. The unconditional Paper II/I–V single-rate impossibility claim is withdrawn: a sufficiently fast single controller in a stipulated model can respond to fast and slow changes (W3).

Evaluate when evidence is sampled, interpreted, authorized, acted upon and corrected, and which constraints and tasks apply. Event-triggered records, existing fast signals or other arrangements may suffice; no single cadence is necessarily required.

### 2.5 The Interface Census

The original’s seven-class census is retained as a set of institutional leads. Compare favorable, adverse, routine and confidential cases, distinguish statutory rules from actual review, and avoid treating qualitative descriptions as a national ranking.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> **The ordinary budget and ministries** are the most tightly coupled. The 13th Five-Year Plan incorporates the GNH Index into national planning, with a target of raising it from 0.781 to 0.850 by 2029 alongside social, ecological, governance and economic targets. Annual budgets must align with the Plan's deliverables. Public spending is audited by the Royal Audit Authority, which has constitutional standing, and audit findings are followed up through Parliament's Public Accounts Committee. The policy screening architecture still exists in revised form. It has not always been applied comprehensively—in 2019 the National Council noted that only some draft policies had been screened and recommended screening every nationally important plan—and a 2024 peer-reviewed study found that Bhutanese programme evaluation had not yet fully incorporated GNH principles. But the ordinary machinery of government is recognisably what a GNH state should look like.

The plan targets, audit arrangements, 2019 screening observation and 2024 evaluation study are all leads. Rules and target values do not establish measurement validity or implementation. The original’s concluding favorable appearance judgment is not evidence of GNH adequacy.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> **Hydropower and major projects** are coupled strongly on specified dimensions. The Environmental Assessment Act of 2000 extends to the whole kingdom and requires assessment before clearance of projects that may affect the environment; clearance depends on acceptable impacts, attention to affected people, and the conservation of natural and cultural heritage. Energy proposals undergo techno-economic and socio-environmental vetting, and audits have identified substantial irregularities in hydropower projects, showing that scrutiny is not merely formal. These interfaces do not cover all nine GNH domains, but they act before commitment and bind.

Audit findings of irregularities can indicate both possible detection capacity and adverse project performance. Actual follow-up, corrective authority, outcomes and environmental clearance application must be checked independently. Neither statutory requirements nor identified irregularities establish effective constraint.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> **Tourism** is the clearest case of adaptive coupling—and the strongest evidence against any simple story in which economic pressure overrides everything else. When the Sustainable Development Fee was raised from US$65 to US$200 a night in 2022, it embodied the country's high-value, low-volume approach to protecting culture and environment. When arrivals in 2023 reached only around a third of their 2019 level, the government cut the effective fee in half, to US$100, explicitly citing jobs, foreign exchange and growth. But it kept the fee, kept the principle, made the concession time-limited and reserved the right to revise it. That is exactly what a system holding several objectives at once should do: adjust a parameter under pressure without abandoning the purpose.

The fee adjustment is a candidate adaptation, not proof of preserved culture, employment or public confidence. Compare other causes of arrivals, alternative policies, distributional and ecological outcomes, including effects of fee levels. The same condition applies in §1.6 and Appendix B.

The original describes **Druk Holding and Investments** as wholly state-owned, created by Royal Charter, board-governed, subject to company law and audited externally and by the Royal Audit Authority. Its vision reportedly names GNH, while its commercial mandate gives it “full commercial discretion and flexibility” over investments and divestments, with historical emphasis on financial prudence, compliance and portfolio performance. The original found no public evidence of national GNH screening for investment decisions and interpreted this as designed delegation rather than malfunction. It reports 2025 taxes and dividends of around Nu 9.5 billion (about 12 percent of domestic revenue), describes the 10X strategy as central to economic transformation, and reports that group-wide ESG development began in April 2026 with International Finance Corporation support. The original leaves effectiveness open. All particulars are attributed and unverified.

DHI’s reported commercial mandate, scale, contributions and ESG launch are unverified leads. Verify its board and audit arrangements, contribution denominator and actual pre-investment review; lack of publicly available evidence cannot establish no GNH review. Neither designed delegation nor the launch establishes effectiveness.

The original describes **the Royal Monetary Authority** as having a narrow mandate of price and financial stability and reserve management under safety, liquidity and return principles. It regards that narrowness as appropriate, and describes its interface with wider objectives as statutory and financial rather than multidimensional. The original argues that this mattered when its balance sheet financed a national-scale strategic investment. These are unverified institutional claims and an attributed normative interpretation.

The original’s view that a narrow central-bank mandate is correct is normative, not a finding of adequacy. Verify RMA’s statutory duties, reserve exposures and the DHI financing channel; effective distributed review and justified narrow delegation are genuine alternatives (R5).

The original report’s account (unverified):

> **The Gelephu Mindfulness City** is the largest and most autonomous actuator of all. Established by Royal Charter as a special administrative region covering roughly 4,046 square kilometres—about a tenth of the country—it has its own executive, legislative and judicial authority, and its Authority is chaired by the King, who appoints its members. Its charter writes wellbeing, sustainability and Gross National Happiness into its purposes. Its accountability architecture is *nested* rather than isolated. Its Application of Laws Act keeps Bhutanese law in force for matters its own laws do not govern, which on the published framework means the national Environmental Assessment Act continues to apply there unless displaced. National transport assessments already model the city's cumulative impacts on forests, water and wildlife corridors. The Royal Audit Authority is preparing statutory audit arrangements for it, and its own Audit and Corruption Investigation Bureau signed a cooperation agreement with the national Anti-Corruption Commission in 2026. Whether its internal constraints bind under real economic pressure cannot yet be known; its decisive decisions lie ahead.

Gelephu’s autonomy, purposes, default-law interpretation, environmental assessment and nested oversight are unverified legal and institutional leads. Competent legal review must resolve exact scope and displacement of law. Test actual independence, review and decision outcomes, including appropriate approvals and protected evidence.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> **International commitments** form a final category. The historical WTO case is the strongest documented example of GNH screening changing a national deliberation. When Bhutan resumed accession negotiations in July 2026, after an eighteen-year hiatus, the Cabinet decision and the inter-agency preparation were publicly documented; no contemporary GNH screening record was. That does not establish that screening was bypassed. It raises a question about scope that the public record does not answer: which classes of state decision—policies, treaties, sovereign investments, special regimes—fall within the multidimensional screen at all.

The historical WTO screening account is explicitly attributed to GNH institutions (as in §1.4); verify the primary record and other reasons for the decision. The renewed process may have used a lawful confidential or alternative review; the absence of a public record cannot decide that question.

The original calls the observed small-sample relationship suggestive, not a law. Age, scale, speed and actual interface effectiveness are separate; the strongest reason not to overstate it is the possibility of existing effective controls or other institutional arrangements. The prospective keep-pace question remains unknown.

### 2.6 The Crypto Episode: Testing the Timing of Coupling

The crypto episode is the original’s principal example of possible late visibility and fiscal time mismatch. Whether actual wider review occurred before commitment remains an open question.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> Between August 2020 and April 2022, the Royal Monetary Authority acquired US$539 million of DHI securities, financing the state's entry into cryptocurrency mining. The sum was roughly 19 percent of GDP and around 40 percent of the foreign reserves held before the transaction. The authorities' rationale was coherent: Bhutan could use its renewable hydropower to diversify its economy, build capabilities relevant to future data-centre industries and earn returns on energy that was not always fully used. Mining began in 2022. The programme was publicly disclosed in April 2023.

The reported investment rationale is a plausible hypothesis, not proof of merit or motive; verify reserve and GDP denominators, dates, financing instruments and prior assessments.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> Its consequences reached well beyond DHI's balance sheet. According to the IMF, domestic electricity consumption rose by more than 60 percent in 2023, largely because of mining, while electricity exports to India fell by about a third and dry-season imports increased. Reserves came under pressure. The IMF raised governance and transparency concerns and recommended safeguards.

Check the IMF account, energy counterfactuals (including unused capacity and export/import opportunities), reserve exposure and net public returns. Adverse and favorable outcomes both matter.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> The system was not open-loop. By the 2025 IMF consultation, the operation had a stop-loss strategy, the authorities were limiting further reserve exposure, and extraordinary crypto transfers for the budget were no longer envisaged. Correction happened. But its timing tells the story. Commitment came in 2020–22, public visibility in 2023, systematic external risk assessment in 2024, and observable adjustment in 2025–26. That is a coupling latency measured in years, around a decision whose scale was measured in a fifth of GDP.

This is a chronology of publicly known events, not an estimate of actual interface latency. Effective confidential review or earlier adjustment remain possible; later changes do not identify their cause.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> The episode also contains a second mechanism. The civil-service pay revision of 2023 created a permanent recurrent cost of around Nu 6 billion a year. For its first two years, extraordinary dividends from DHI's crypto operations financed around two-thirds of it. Crypto income is volatile and was expected to taper as mining equipment aged; wages, once raised, do not fall. The IMF warned specifically against allowing crypto boom-and-bust cycles to transmit into recurrent spending. This report calls the pattern an **actuator timescale mismatch**: a fast, volatile resource used to fund a slow, sticky commitment. It does not foretell a fiscal crisis—the transfers have ended and attrition has since fallen, to around 5 percent of the civil service in 2024–25, for reasons that include but are not limited to pay. It does show that when actuators operating on different timescales are connected without an interface designed for that connection, the fast one can leave the slow one carrying a lasting obligation.

The financing pattern motivates the W5 test; structural revenue, sustainable buffers or other lawful financing may suffice. The reported attrition decline does not identify the effect of wages (W7). Civil-service reform, reopening borders, career, study, recognition and work–life balance are explicit alternatives.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> The lesson of the crypto episode is not that the investment was wrong. Reasonable people differ on that. It is that a decision of this magnitude reached the public, and Bhutan's broader architecture for weighing national choices, only after it had been made.

The narrower conclusion concerns public disclosure timing. Whether any wider review occurred before the decision must be independently established. Appendix F preserves four competing readings.

### 2.7 The Race in Time

The following dated table is the original chronology (unverified); it records publicly described developments, not dates of all internal assessments or measurements of two comparable rates.

The original argues that timing distinguishes a race from a lag and reads the dated record as “more encouraging than the crypto episode alone would suggest”. This is its favorable interpretation, not an established comparison: the timing, scope and effectiveness of actual interfaces remain to be assessed (IC-ad).

| Period | Actuator power | Interface development |
|--------|----------------|-----------------------|
| **2020–22** | RMA finances DHI's crypto investment; mining begins | No public evidence of a wider-objective interface before commitment |
| **April 2023** | Crypto programme disclosed | Public and macroeconomic scrutiny becomes possible |
| **2024–25** | Mining expands; DHI grows more central to diversification | IMF risk assessment; stop-loss and exposure limits follow |
| **February 2024** | Gelephu Mindfulness City established as an autonomous region | Charter embeds wellbeing, sustainability and GNH in its purposes |
| **December 2024** | Gelephu begins enacting its own legal system | Bhutanese law remains the default where Gelephu law is silent |
| **August 2025** | DHI launches its 10X Journey | — |
| **February 2026** | DHI describes a US$40 billion investment horizon over 10–15 years | — |
| **April 2026** | — | DHI launches a group-wide ESG system with IFC support |
| **June 2026** | Gelephu institutions continue scaling | Gelephu's audit bureau signs cooperation agreement with the national Anti-Corruption Commission |
| **August 2026** | — | Royal Audit Authority prepares statutory audit arrangements for Gelephu |

The table motivates two interpretations: an earlier possible review/timing and financing concern, and subsequent candidate institutional maturation. DHI’s ESG launch reportedly followed 10X by about eight months; Gelephu’s reported legal, audit and integrity arrangements developed within roughly two years. Neither proves late review, timely effective constraint or a race won. The prospective question requires actual decision classes, risks, review dates, feasible action and independent outcomes (P1–P5).

### 2.8 The Boundary: Whose Happiness?

Everything in this report so far concerns whether Bhutan's institutions serve the objective the country has defined. There is a prior question that no interface can answer: who is included in the population whose flourishing the objective represents.

The following historical and legal account is retained from the original, not newly verified. Distinguish official positions, refugee testimony, independent scholarship, human-rights reports and state replies; do not equate documented violations with mere opinion or adjudicate individual cases without evidence. No personal case details or forced identification are sought.

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, Bhutan tightened its citizenship regime and pursued policies of national cultural integration. The census of the period classified many residents of the south—predominantly Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa—as non-nationals. By the early 1990s, around 100,000 Lhotshampa had arrived in refugee camps in eastern Nepal. The facts of how they came to leave remain contested. Bhutan's position, as recorded by the UN refugee agency, has been that many left voluntarily or were not citizens; the refugees have maintained that they were forced out because of their ethnicity. Independent scholarship documents the tightening citizenship rules and the homogenising national-cultural project that preceded and accompanied the crisis, and human rights organisations have documented discriminatory laws, abuses and large-scale forced displacement.

The problem is historical in origin but not merely historical in consequence. More than 113,500 refugees were eventually resettled abroad, most of them in the United States; according to UNHCR, 6,365 remained in the two settlements at Beldangi and Pathari-Sanischare in 2025, still without a durable solution. The citizenship architecture of that era remains in force: the Constitution requires both parents to be Bhutanese citizens for natural-born citizenship, naturalisation requires fifteen years of lawful residence and knowledge of Dzongkha and Bhutanese culture, and the 1985 Citizenship Act allows applications to be refused without reasons. The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child has continued to raise the lack of progress for Lhotshampa refugees and children. Human Rights Watch reported in 2025 that at least 32 people it considers political prisoners remained in detention, most in cases linked to the conflicts around 1990, and UN special procedures raised allegations concerning nineteen long-term prisoners in the same year; Bhutan replied to that process. In 2025, Bhutanese refugees deported from the United States were reported to have been unable to remain in Bhutan.

The camp count and resettled count refer to different reported populations; dates, status and legal claims must be independently checked, including the state response and protections for children and detainees. No inference about any particular person’s status or remedy follows.

This history does not establish manipulation of current resident surveys. A well-defined resident-population estimand may legitimately exclude nonresidents; it cannot adjudicate citizenship, statelessness, displacement or the rights of affected people. The original states: **“the dimensionality of an objective cannot compensate for a contested population boundary.”** It continues: “A state can measure nine domains, or ninety, and still fail a universality test if some people's membership in the governed population is denied.” This preserves its normative question, not an adjudication of the contested history or of any person’s status. Legitimacy must be assessed separately from survey validity and interface effectiveness. The normative question requires legal review and safe community-designated participation, not an expanded score (W6).

The boundary also shows that the dimensions within Gross National Happiness can pull against each other. Scholarship on Bhutan's development philosophy has described two faces of GNH: a universal account of wellbeing, and a nation-building project centred on a particular national culture. The domain of "cultural diversity and resilience" can mean the resilience of Bhutan's many cultures, or the preservation of a dominant national identity. The original interprets the crisis as a predominance of the nation-building face; examine the historical evidence and affected communities’ accounts. This report treats the question of whose flourishing counts as a boundary condition that sits logically before the Interface Race, not as part of it. An architecture perfectly coupled from objective to actuator would still need to answer it.

Plural cultural interpretation and community-designated expertise must precede any use of cultural criteria in investment decisions. Rights, lawful due process and refusal are not compensable by economic or aggregate GNH benefits.

### 2.9 The Cultural Operating System

The series uses a cultural operating-system metaphor to investigate how institutional practices and values may interact. It is a heuristic, not a classification of individuals or a causal national character.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> The first layer is **Gross National Happiness** itself, which is best understood as a cultural achievement before it was a measurement system. It draws on a Buddhist understanding of the middle way—the idea that wellbeing lies in balance rather than accumulation—and it gives Bhutanese public life a shared vocabulary in which economic growth is a means rather than an end. This is why the economic turn of the 13th Plan is framed as rebalancing GNH rather than departing from it. The language constrains what can be said, and what can be said constrains, at least in part, what can be done.

The middle-way and shared-vocabulary account is a contextual hypothesis. Test actual plural meanings, planning practice and whether stated language affects decisions; no universal endorsement or inferred sincerity. Independent criticism and civic-space conditions matter.

The original describes **kidu** as the monarch’s constitutional prerogative to provide citizens relief and welfare directly, interpreting it as a longstanding, valued form of responsiveness from the throne. It links this tradition to royal leadership in the transition: the King’s reported vision and chairmanship of Gelephu’s Authority and the 13th Plan’s reported attribution of its strategic shift to his guidance. The original interprets leadership as an integrative channel carrying legitimacy for economic transformation as a continuation of GNH, and asks whether that alignment can become institutionalized and reproducible through procedures as well as shared purpose. This is an unverified contextual account and open structural question.

Kidu’s constitutional scope and actual distribution, the reported leadership roles and the Plan’s attribution require verification. Direct care and continuity could support coordination, but neither establishes legitimacy, unanimity or a measured gain. Examine decision rights, plural accounts and safe independent challenge.

The original describes **driglam namzha** as a code of etiquette, dress and conduct expressing national identity and a source of cultural cohesion and pride. It also notes that the 1989 decree enforcing national dress and etiquette is widely cited among integration policies preceding the Lhotshampa crisis (§2.8). Its interpretation is that a resource supporting shared identity has also been used to define its edges, a cultural counterpart of the population-boundary question. These are attributed, unverified historical and interpretive claims.

The 1989 decree and its relationship to the Lhotshampa crisis require historical and legal verification (§2.8). Cultural pride and exclusion are distinct possible experiences, not characteristics to infer from membership. Community-designated expertise, plural interpretation and safe dissent are indispensable.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> Bhutanese discussions of national values often add two further ideas: *tha damtshig*, the sacred commitment and loyalty between people and to one's obligations, and *ley jumdrey*, the understanding of cause and effect. Both have obvious resonance for the Interface Race. Commitment is what an interface formalises; cause and effect is what a well-designed interface tries to trace before, rather than after, a decision takes effect.

These two concepts are contextual analogies, not evidence that an interface is effective or that loyalty substitutes for scrutiny. Test actual accountability, evidence of consequences, and the ability to contest decisions without retaliation.

### 2.10 How the Mechanisms Fit Together

The original mechanisms form a candidate causal story, not a demonstrated loop. H2 identifies the links and disconfirmers; the population boundary is logically prior and separate.

The original links broad GNH aims and reported improvements in education, health and living standards to relative opportunities (Compare), movements (Exit), economic strategy (Accelerate) and evolving interfaces (Recouple). Alternative domestic and international causes, prior investments, beneficial migration, and adequate distributed interfaces can break these links. A later pay response may draw on earlier mining investment without that investment having been caused by exit. The heterogeneous census and dated launches motivate prospective tests, not a finding of high observability, lag or catch-up.

Compare, Exit, Accelerate and Recouple name the four stages of the candidate mechanism. Each relationship must be examined separately, including the possibility of new conditions and beneficial migration or return. The loop does not close automatically.

Outside this story stands the **population boundary**, which asks whose flourishing the objective counts at all. It interacts with the race only indirectly—a Gelephu or a DHI that serves Bhutan's objective perfectly would still serve the objective as the boundary defines it—but it is logically prior to everything else in this report.

The architecture can be summarised in the language of the series:

> **A multidimensional declared objective is distinct from measurement validity and response capacity. The Interface Race asks whether legitimate task-specific interfaces remain adequate as institutions change.**

That is the question the rest of this report takes up: what building interface capacity would look like, what slows it, and how Bhutan might begin.
