## 3. What Building Interface Capacity Would Look Like

---

### 3.1 The Principle: Couple What Matters, Where It Matters, Before It Is Too Late

The first temptation, faced with the Interface Race, is to reach for the tool Bhutan already has: extend the GNH screen to everything. Every DHI investment, every Gelephu concession, every reserve decision could be scored against nine domains and 33 indicators before it proceeds.

A universal screen could impose excessive delay, cost or incompatible mandates on commercial investment, reserve management and special jurisdictions. The original’s three specific rationales are that DHI needs commercial discipline and speed, that a central bank’s reserve management benefits from narrow responsibilities, and that Gelephu’s autonomous legal and administrative environment is intended to move faster for investors. These institutional and comparative propositions are unverified and do not establish that every screen would be harmful or that speed overrides rights. Narrow responsibility may be appropriate; whether additional review is necessary depends on material risks, rights, available alternatives and deadlines, not the number of dimensions. Existing controls and targeted review may suffice.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> The international benchmarks point the other way. Norway's Government Pension Fund and Singapore's Temasek are both commercially autonomous; neither government directs individual investments. Both have spent decades building explicit interfaces between that autonomy and selected wider national commitments. Neither attempts to make every investment maximise national wellbeing. What they have in common is that the boundary between commercial freedom and wider values is explicit, institutionalised and inspectable.

Norway and Temasek are comparative leads, not evidence of a universally transferable architecture. Verify actual autonomy, legal rules, independent review and results, including adverse cases.

The organising principle for Bhutan follows:

> **Couple what matters, where it matters, before commitment becomes irreversible.**

Not every dimension of Gross National Happiness needs an interface at every actuator. The dimensions that do are those where a decision can cause large, lasting or irreversible harm to the whole objective: reserve exposure, fiscal commitments, ecological integrity, community displacement, cultural heritage, and the transparency that allows the public to weigh these trade-offs at all. For those dimensions, and above a threshold of scale, interfaces should act before commitment rather than after it. Below that threshold, and on other dimensions, specialised institutions can and should be left to do their work.

A GDP or reserve threshold may miss small decisions with severe rights or ecological consequences. A separate audit suggestion is risk/rights escalation below scale thresholds; justify exclusions, alternative ownership and lawful emergency exceptions with retrospective review. No rights waiver follows from a small budget.

The sections that follow apply this principle to the actuators examined in Section 2. They end with the two questions no interface design can settle on its own: how to answer the comparison that is driving exit, and whose flourishing the objective includes.

### 3.2 Selective Coupling: Which Decisions Need Interfaces Before Commitment

The crypto episode raises a question about the scope of policy screening and other review. The public record alone cannot establish that it fell outside an actual authorized interface. Clarify decision classes and compare existing arrangements before introducing a new requirement.

A workable definition would rest on four triggers. A decision would require an ex-ante interface if it:

- commits public resources above a defined share of GDP or of foreign reserves;
- creates obligations lasting beyond the current plan period, including recurrent spending;
- is difficult to reverse, such as major infrastructure, land allocation, or long-term concessions and international treaties;
- materially affects a GNH domain protected by constitutional or statutory limits, such as forest cover or cultural heritage.

For decisions that meet a trigger, the interface need not be the full GNH screen. It should be proportionate: a structured assessment of the triggered dimensions, a record of the trade-off, a reviewer independent of the deciding institution, and—unless there is a compelling commercial or security reason for delay—public disclosure before commitment or within a short, defined period afterwards.

The four triggers above are the original proposals, not law. Confirm authority and thresholds; compare existing controls, proportionate escalation, emergency procedures and retrospective review. Publish rules and reasons with justified redaction, protected independent access and challenge. No universal screen or implied mandate expansion.

The WTO episode is attributed to GNH institutions in the original; confirm the primary account and lawful alternative assessment. Treaties, sovereign investments, reserve deployments and special-jurisdiction decisions may require different review. A published statement of scope might clarify responsibilities; actual burden, costs and review quality must be tested.

### 3.3 Internalised Interfaces: What DHI's ESG System Could Become

The original report’s account (unverified):

> Singapore's Temasek shows one route to coupling a commercial actuator to wider values: build the values into the actuator itself. Temasek assesses environmental, social and governance factors from pre-investment through post-investment, applies an internal carbon price to its decisions, sets portfolio emissions targets and reports on sustainability annually, with board-level oversight. None of this is imposed by a government screen. It is part of how the institution decides.

The Temasek details are attributed leads. Verify actual review, board oversight and performance, including adverse cases; no direct transplant is inferred.

The reported 2026 DHI ESG framework offers a candidate route, subject to verification of IFC support and actual procedures. The original proposes four features to distinguish a decision instrument from reporting alone:

**Pre-investment integration.** ESG assessment should occur before investment commitment, not only in annual reporting on the portfolio.

**Disclosed materiality.** DHI should publish which environmental, social and governance factors it treats as material, and why—including those derived explicitly from GNH domains such as community vitality and ecological resilience.

**Consequential cases.** The original proposes recording investments modified, conditioned or declined on ESG grounds. Include appropriate unchanged approvals, missed material risks, inappropriate interventions and independent outcomes. A visible costly veto is not necessary or sufficient for effectiveness.

**Board accountability.** Responsibility for ESG outcomes should sit with the board, with a defined link to its reporting to the Ministry of Finance as shareholder.

GNH domains could inform DHI’s ESG materiality, while financial materiality, impacts on people and environment, and national objectives remain distinct. Disclose the rationale for selecting domains and any omitted domains; the original six-domain selection omits health, education and living standards (see §5.2). Validate criteria for the actual task and require plural cultural interpretation and community-designated expertise before applying cultural criteria. No culture-based exclusion or rights trade-off is permissible. Independent reviewers, recusal, affected-person voice, protected access, challenge and evaluation against current ESG or no additional layer are required. No claim of comparative uniqueness or guaranteed investor acceptance.

### 3.4 Explicit Boundary Interfaces: The Norwegian Lesson

The original report’s account (unverified):

> Norway shows a second route: leave the investment institution commercially focused, but draw an explicit, legislated boundary around it. Norway's fund aims at the highest return consistent with acceptable risk; its management is delegated to Norges Bank without day-to-day political direction. Around that autonomy sit ethical guidelines specifying investments incompatible with wider national norms, historically administered through an independent Council on Ethics whose recommendations were public, and an annual report from the Ministry of Finance to Parliament. Norway has recently been reviewing parts of this arrangement, but the principle—that the boundary between financial autonomy and wider values is legislated and inspectable—has not changed.

The Norway account is a comparative lead including the recent review; verify the current legal framework and actual compliance. Do not infer efficacy or applicability to Bhutan.

For Bhutan, the relevant boundary concerns less the ethics of foreign equity holdings than the national consequences of domestic strategic investment. A Bhutanese analogue might take the form of a published **investment boundary framework** for DHI and the RMA's strategic exposures, setting out:

- limits on the share of reserves that can be committed to any single strategic investment, and the procedure for exceeding them;
- categories of investment that require Cabinet and parliamentary notification before commitment;
- the independent body—the Royal Audit Authority, a parliamentary committee, or a new standing panel—that reviews compliance with the framework and reports publicly.

The framework would not tell DHI what to invest in. It would tell the country, in advance, where DHI's freedom ends and the wider architecture begins.

The three listed elements are verbatim original candidate options, not current requirements. Additional audit suggestions include eligible-asset, concentration, risk/return and reserve-adequacy checks. Identify competent legal owners and independent review, protected access and appeal; compare existing financial controls and burden. No investment advice or authorization.

### 3.5 Timescale Matching: Keep Volatile Revenue Away from Permanent Commitments

The reported extraordinary dividends supporting a recurrent wage revision raise a fiscal time-profile question. Temporary financing does not establish that the long-term budget is unsustainable or that an interface failed; test structural revenue, buffers, alternative financing and stress scenarios (W5).

One original proposal draws on resource-revenue management practices. Revenues that are volatile, extraordinary or finite—crypto dividends, windfall hydropower earnings, one-off asset sales—should flow first into a stabilisation or savings buffer, with transfers to the budget governed by a published rule. Recurrent commitments such as wages should be financed from structural revenue, or from buffer transfers that the rule shows to be sustainable. The IMF has already recommended safeguards of this kind to prevent crypto volatility from transmitting into recurrent spending.

The IMF recommendation is an unverified lead. Evaluate liquidity, distribution, structural revenue, lawful emergency exceptions and feasible alternatives independently; the rule may not be sufficient or appropriate in all circumstances.

Future lumpy returns and hydropower earnings may renew the temptation to finance continuing obligations with temporary revenue. A proposed fiscal rule could help align those time profiles; it is not presumed cheap or guaranteed to prevent recurrence. Test its own costs, independent outcome and alternatives.

### 3.6 Institutionalising the Coupling at Gelephu

The original describes Gelephu’s emerging architecture as nested: national law potentially applying by default, national audit arrangements, internal integrity cooperation, and charter commitments to wellbeing, sustainability and GNH. These are legal and institutional leads. Qualified legal review must establish the application of national and local law, competent owners, access, actual oversight and enforceable constraints. No independence or effectiveness follows from the charter alone.

The task now is to make those commitments observable and binding as the city grows. Four developments would do so:

**Multidimensional outcome reporting.** Gelephu should publish, alongside investment, company formation and employment, indicators for the other dimensions its charter names: ecological integrity of its forests and wildlife corridors, community outcomes for residents already living in the region, cultural continuity and wellbeing. The GNH survey methodology offers a ready foundation.

Co-design indicators and baseline with affected residents and community-designated expertise; distinguish ecological and resident estimands, land acquisition/pooling, consent and compensation, livelihood and cultural outcomes, and possible displacement. No compulsory identifiers, unsafe disclosure or tracking. Compare existing data and no new collection.

**Explicit environmental law.** If Gelephu enacts its own environmental framework, it should be at least as protective as the national Environmental Assessment Act, and the relationship between the two should be stated in law rather than left to default provisions.

This is an original design proposal conditional on competent lawmaking and legal review, not a statement that national law currently applies to every decision. Lawful protective requirements and actual application must be evaluated separately.

**Codified coupling.** The original describes shared royal leadership as a possible alignment channel and proposes translating such commitments into durable standards, review and procedures. Legitimacy, independence and contestability require evidence and plural voices; neither leadership nor formalization establishes effectiveness. Effective informal coordination is a genuine rival. No inferred endorsement or immunity from scrutiny.

The original explicitly says, “This is not a comment on present leadership.” Its proposal concerns institutional durability beyond founders, not immunity from scrutiny of current leadership or proof of its quality.

**Visible constraint bite.** The original proposes an example where a corridor is protected against a profitable development or a concession is conditioned on community outcomes. Such cases could test whether constraints matter, but no economic loss or veto is required: appropriate approvals and alternatives avoiding harm may be stronger evidence. Test actual ecological, social, legal and economic outcomes with protected review; public disclosure may be appropriately limited.

### 3.7 Answering the Comparison, Not Only the Exit

The original interprets acceleration as a response to migration and proposes complementary ways to improve domestic opportunities. Migration has many possible causes and benefits; voluntary departure, return or nonreturn are legitimate. The aim of support is access, rights and wellbeing, not forced retention.

The World Bank's migration research points to several. **Career progression in the public service** matters as much as pay: exit surveys continue to cite further study, career prospects, recognition and work–life balance as reasons for leaving, and reforms that improve progression address the comparison without adding to recurrent costs on the scale of a general pay rise. **Training and certification in critical occupations**, particularly health and education, can be designed to be recognised both at home and abroad, so that skills gained in Bhutan remain valuable wherever their holders are. And **support across the whole migration cycle**—departure, work abroad and return—can turn a one-way exodus into circulation. A diaspora of more than 65,000 people, many of them highly educated and still closely tied to Bhutan, is also a resource: for remittances, for investment, and for skills that return.

The World Bank and other reported findings are verification leads; assess career progression, portable certification, preparation for departure, work and return, and diaspora contributions on voluntary terms. No restriction on movement or benefits conditional on staying. Test effects against existing services and other reforms, including staff and access equity.

These measures may complement job creation or other opportunities. Their costs, benefits and interactions are open questions; reduced exit pressure is not required for success. Respect people’s choices and independent evidence.

### 3.8 The Boundary: What a Universal Objective Would Require

The population boundary is not solved by coupling. This external report has no standing to determine individual status or prescribe a settlement. It retains the original three candidate directions for legitimate deliberation by affected people and competent authorities; survey coverage, rights and legal membership are separate.

A national objective that aspires to universality needs a boundary that can be examined and defended. Three steps would move in that direction. The first is a **durable solution for the remaining refugees**—more than six thousand people still in settlements in eastern Nepal, and the smaller number of deportees and stateless people whose status has become newly uncertain—developed with Nepal and UNHCR, and including a credible pathway for verified return where that is sought. The second is a **review of the long-term detention cases** raised by UN special procedures and human rights organisations, through a process whose findings are made public. The third is attention to the **interpretation of the cultural domain** within Gross National Happiness itself: making explicit that cultural diversity and resilience include the languages, faiths and practices of all the communities within Bhutan's borders, and measuring them accordingly.

The remaining camp population and resettled population are distinct reported figures (see §2.8). Durable solutions require voluntary informed choice, no forced return, independent legal review with Nepal and UNHCR where appropriate, and individualized due process. Detention review must protect privacy, safety and lawful rights; publish only safe findings, not personal files. Plural cultural criteria require affected communities’ meaningful participation and community-designated expertise, including minority and refugee voices, without compelled identification. No inference of consent, no adjudication or legal recommendation for individual cases.

These candidate directions address rights and inclusion on their own terms. Neither the Interface Race nor aggregate GNH outcomes can decide them; their legitimacy and feasibility depend on competent authority, evidence and affected people’s participation.

### 3.9 Cultural Software Update: From Guardianship to Architecture

The series’ cultural operating-system metaphor frames a hypothesis about institutional continuity, not a hierarchy of cultures. The original proposes retaining a commitment to multidimensional wellbeing while developing institutional procedures that remain open to change and challenge.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> What must change is how that idea is carried. For half a century, Gross National Happiness has been carried largely by guardianship: by the vision and commitment of successive monarchs, by the moral authority of Bhutanese Buddhist culture, and by a small, closely connected elite that shared the philosophy and could apply it through personal judgement. That form of carriage served Bhutan remarkably well while its institutions were few and its economy small. It is harder to sustain when the institutions multiply, speed up and must attract foreign capital and talent that do not share the culture.

The guardianship account is a historical interpretation, not a verified claim of unanimous legitimacy, exceptional performance or inadequate existing arrangements. Independent scrutiny and plural perspectives are indispensable.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> The evolution this report proposes is from guardianship to **architecture**: from a philosophy carried by the people who hold it to one embedded in the procedures of every institution powerful enough to affect it. *Tha damtshig*, the sacred commitment, becomes a published framework that says in advance what each institution is bound by. *Ley jumdrey*, the attention to cause and effect, becomes an assessment made before a decision rather than a correction made after it. *Kidu*, the throne's direct care for the people, is complemented by institutions that extend the same care as a matter of routine.

The analogy preserves the original meanings of tha damtshig, ley jumdrey and kidu. Whether particular procedures realize those values is open; neither shared leadership nor law by itself guarantees effective review. All interpretations must remain open to legitimate disagreement and independent scrutiny.

The proposed shift from guardianship to architecture is a conditional institutional design, not an assertion that Bhutan should discard effective informal coordination or that all citizens endorse a single vision. Existing arrangements and justified nonadoption remain alternatives.
