## Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

### How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek) from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. He chose the topic, posed the questions, set the framework the models were asked to apply, and decided to publish the result. He wrote little of the text himself.

The models were prompted through a staged process. They first mapped how Gross National Happiness travels from the constitution through measurement, screening, planning, budgeting and implementation. They then set competing hypotheses against each other and revised them over several passes. Some initial hypotheses were dropped or weakened along the way, including that the GNH Index directly triggered the recent economic turn and that Bhutan has recentred on growth. Others were narrowed into the "interface race" described in this report. The steps, the evidence they cite and the conclusions they drew were all produced by the models.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about Bhutanese institutions, laws, survey results, dates and figures may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. Where the text says "evidence shows", that is the models' characterisation of sources, not a verified finding.

### Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them have any standing within Bhutan's institutional life. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not Bhutanese, does not live in Bhutan and has no lived experience of its institutions, culture or history; his background is in engineering physics and an interest in systems and governance theory. Much of the texture of Bhutanese public life, religious culture and policy-making is inaccessible from outside. Bhutanese readers will see what this report misses, and corrections are welcome.

### The Country Reports Series

This is the eighteenth report in the Country Reports for Systemic Change series, all produced the same way. The earlier reports covered Germany, France, Sweden, India, the European Union, the United Kingdom, Brazil, Russia, the United States, Finland, China, Japan, Nigeria, Israel, Spain, Switzerland and South Korea, each through a different proposed deficit.

Bhutan was chosen as a contrasting case: a country whose national objective is unusually rich and explicit. The question it raises is whether knowing what a country values is enough to keep fast-growing institutions serving it. The series as a whole is a set of hypotheses built with one framework, untested by people with domain expertise.
