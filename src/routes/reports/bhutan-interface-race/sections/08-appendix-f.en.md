## Appendix F: The Crypto–Pay Case File

### F.1 Why This Case

The original selects this case as its clearest illustration of possible review latency and fiscal time mismatch. The case does not establish either actual absence of ex-ante review or investment merit. All reported dates and figures below are unverified, including source-specific IMF and Pay Commission accounts.

### F.2 Chronology

**August 2020 – April 2022.** The Royal Monetary Authority acquires approximately US$539 million of DHI securities, financing DHI's entry into cryptocurrency mining using domestic hydropower. The sum is roughly 19 percent of GDP and around 40 percent of pre-transaction foreign reserves. Mining begins in 2022.

**June 2022 – February 2023.** Voluntary civil-service resignations rise from around 64 a month to around 234 a month. The 2022 civil-service transformation and the reopening of borders coincide with the surge.

The reported civil-service transformation and reopening are explicit domestic rivals to the external-comparison hypothesis. Distinguish unique movers, resignations, reasons and timing. The mining investment predates the surge; the later wage revision is the response candidate.

**February 2023.** The Sixth Pay Commission is constituted, with retention of talented public servants among its explicit objectives.

**April 2023.** DHI's crypto programme is publicly disclosed.

**2023.** The pay revision raises the minimum pay scale by 50 percent at an annual cost of around Nu 6 billion (about 2.3 percent of GDP). The financing plan includes Nu 4 billion from DHI in each of FY2023/24 and FY2024/25. Domestic electricity consumption rises by more than 60 percent, largely because of mining; electricity exports to India fall by about a third.

**2024.** The IMF finds that extraordinary crypto dividends financed around two-thirds of the wage increase across the two fiscal years, and warns against allowing crypto volatility to transmit into recurrent spending.

**2025.** Extraordinary transfers for the wage increase are no longer envisaged; the operation has a stop-loss strategy and further reserve exposure is being limited. Civil-service attrition falls to around 5 percent in FY2024–25.

The chronology is a lead, not proof of when all review occurred, causal correction, sustainable finance or failure. Distinguish reported estimates, proposed transfers, receipts, reserves, GDP, energy exports/imports and outcomes; compare counterfactual uses and independent assessment.

### F.3 Competing Interpretations

The original report’s account (unverified):

> **Strategic diversification.** The investment used renewable energy with limited alternative uses at certain times, built capabilities relevant to future data-centre industries, and produced substantial returns that funded public priorities.

Test diversification against alternative investments and alternative uses of electricity, including exports and domestic demand. Verify capabilities, realized net returns and allocation to public priorities; a beneficial investment would not establish adequate review.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> **Macroeconomic risk.** The investment exposed a significant share of reserves to a volatile asset, reallocated electricity from exports, and created fiscal dependence on uncertain revenue.

Test the reported exposure, opportunity cost of electricity and fiscal dependence against actual reserves, financing terms, buffers, revenue and expenditure trajectories (W5). A risk is not a demonstrated loss or inevitable crisis; sustainable financing remains possible.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> **Governance and transparency.** A commitment of national scale was made through the DHI–RMA channel and disclosed only after substantial commitment.

Distinguish commitment, authorization, internal review and public disclosure. Protected but effective pre-commitment assessment is a genuine alternative; neither opacity nor transparency establishes decision quality.

The original report’s account (unverified):

> **Adaptive correction.** Once risks were visible, the system responded: transfers ended, exposure was limited, and risk management was strengthened.

Verify the dates, scope and effectiveness of transfer changes, exposure limits and risk management. Earlier controls, market developments and other decisions are rivals to correction caused by scrutiny; chronology alone does not identify causation (W7).

### F.4 What the Case Shows

The four interpretations can coexist or compete. The reported 2020–22 commitment, 2023 disclosure, 2024 external assessment and later adjustments motivate timing analysis; confidential ex-ante review and effective earlier responses remain possible. Extraordinary dividends financing recurrent wages motivate W5’s time-profile test; durable structural revenue, lawful reallocation or sustainable buffers may address the gap. Separate actual decisions, energy allocation, fiscal effects and independent outcomes. W7 bars causal attribution from timing alone. The default is unknown.
