## 3. What Building Requisite Monetary Governance Would Look Like

### 3.1 The Principle: Mandates, Instruments and Democratic Accountability Without Political Capture

The original design ambition is to preserve price stability, operational independence, expertise and the credibility of the monetary framework while improving its capacity to recognise and respond to relevant financial, distributional, fiscal and ecological consequences. It is an advocated design direction, not a deduction that the existing architecture cannot work. Marginal improvements to forecasting, communication or financial stability arrangements may suffice for some tasks; the wider institutional architecture may require revision for others. The early fractality claims in the Governance as Engineering series are historical; Appendix C W5 establishes only a restricted counterexample to universal single-scale impossibility, not a dynamic stability theorem.

Compare four institutional approaches for each identified bottleneck: (1) improving central-bank evidence within the existing mandate; (2) expanding objectives or instruments by competent democratic authorization; (3) distributing analysis and response among statistical, fiscal, prudential, environmental and monetary authorities with effective coordination; (4) retaining existing arrangements. The original's preference for multi-dimensional governance does not settle which institution should own each task. A model-based estimate of a distributional consequence does not confer legal permission to redistribute. The same standard applies to all proposals in this chapter: specify the task, evidence, uncertainty, legitimate owner, possible actions, coordination, rights, burdens and independent outcomes. Include dispersed knowledge and compare less centralised alternatives. No expansion of a central bank's power or data access is implied by better analysis.

### 3.2 Multi-Dimensional Mandates

The original proposes retaining price stability while explicitly recognising financial stability, employment, distributional equity and climate resilience. Its design distinguishes a primary price-stability objective and secondary objectives pursued consistently with it, with explicit treatment of trade-offs. This formulation is an advocated option to examine, not a description of a universal mandate. Some jurisdictions already have multiple objectives or obligations to support other economic policies; verify their wording and precedence. The original cites the Bank of England's remit concerning government economic objectives and the ECB's secondary objective of supporting general Union policies. These are dated legal leads, not a finding that those objectives are insufficiently operationalised. The New Zealand remit in Appendix B is another lead; the reviewer adds post-2019 changes to the verification queue.

A candidate multidimensional mandate would specify outcomes and responsibilities, explicit priority or decision procedures for conflicts, transparent reports, and analysis of interactions among financial and price stability, employment, aggregate demand, distribution and climate-related risks. The original's reasoning is that explicit recognition might prevent certain consequences from being treated as afterthoughts. A rival possibility is that unclear multiple objectives weaken accountability or expectations, or that distributional and environmental goals are better pursued by institutions with other instruments and expertise. A primary-objective constraint is not by itself a deficit. Compare the ability to achieve justified objectives and manage trade-offs over the relevant horizons.

Mandate expansion, where warranted, requires competent legislative or other democratic authorization. A committee cannot acquire a new objective by publishing an analysis. Operational discretion under a clear lawful remit remains distinct from goal-setting. Public reasons should explain how relevant secondary considerations were addressed, including legitimate reasons not to take an action. Analyse the proposed expansion for the same concentration of discretionary power and knowledge claims criticised in §2.9 and §2.13. The case for reform cannot be that independence is guaranteed to collapse without it. Institutional durability and public legitimacy are outcomes to investigate.

### 3.3 Multi-Instrument Frameworks

The policy rate remains a central candidate instrument for monetary conditions; the original proposes matching additional instruments to differentiated disturbances rather than relying exclusively on a uniform response. The proposed instrument set includes the following options, without assuming their availability, effectiveness or ownership in a particular jurisdiction.

- **Financial stability:** countercyclical capital buffers adjusted with credit conditions, loan-to-value limits in housing markets, sectoral capital requirements for concentrated risk, and associated stress testing. Prudential authorities may own some or all of these tools rather than the monetary committee.
- **Credit and distribution:** differential reserve requirements, sectoral lending facilities and changes to eligible collateral that could affect financing for smaller businesses and underserved communities. Compare targeted fiscal transfers, public credit programmes and regulatory reforms; examine market distortion, credit risk, incidence, fiscal exposure and democratic responsibility. More granular knowledge does not demonstrate the ability to allocate credit well.
- **Climate-related operations:** climate-aligned asset-purchase tilts, risk-based collateral measures and targeted refinancing for green investment. Distinguish risk management within existing authority from a new allocative or environmental policy objective. Compare fiscal and environmental instruments and portfolio rules. Do not assume compatibility with monetary objectives or lawful authority.

The original argues that such differentiation could reduce the averaging problem: a housing-market instrument might address a particular source of fragility without imposing the same effect on small-business financing. This is a testable hypothesis about incremental and spillover effects. The response matrix is not known from the instrument list. Instruments can interact, have collinear effects or create contradictory incentives. Boundaries on their use, operational delays, balance-sheet risk and effects on expectations may constrain feasible paths. W3 shows why a larger instrument count is neither necessary nor sufficient for adequacy. A scalar instrument may already address a particular set of disturbances, while two nominal instruments may fail.

The original also proposes matching instruments to different timescales. Emergency liquidity and market safeguards, scheduled policy decisions, prudential buffers and climate-related risk assessments may operate on different horizons. Verify actual deadlines and transmission rather than assume prudential tools can always respond faster than the rate. A fast reaction to noise may be harmful; a long-horizon intervention can have immediate price and distribution effects. Compare the attainable paths under the proposed division of authority, including the coordination required between owners. The operational challenges — uncertain effects, political sensitivity, administrative cost and legal scope — are part of the comparison, not minor obstacles to a predetermined architecture. Retain existing arrangements where they are adequate.

### 3.4 The Monetary Policy Distributional Impact Assessment

The original proposes making distributional consequences operationally visible through an independently produced Assessment of each major monetary-policy decision. It is a candidate mechanism for a demonstrated evidence or accountability gap, not proof that distributional effects are currently invisible. The Assessment could complement existing research and improve scrutiny even when the policy committee has no independent inequality-reduction objective. If the relevant evidence already exists and is considered adequately, additional reporting may not be warranted.

The proposed scope covers rate decisions, asset purchase programmes and forward guidance. Estimate effects under a range of assumptions and explicitly stated alternatives; distinguish direct and indirect channels, asset values, incomes, cash flow, employment, wages, housing and regional or demographic exposure. Do not present precise transfers from the bottom half to the top decile as known without credible causal evidence. The uncertainty includes both parameter variation and model misspecification. Some distinctions may not be estimable at the proposed frequency. Aggregate or sampled evidence may suffice; publish limitations rather than claim real-time precision.

The original envisages a dedicated unit independent of the policy committee, a fixed nonrenewable director, independently protected resources and access to relevant data. Chapter 5 supplies the detailed design. Secure predecision delivery can potentially inform the committee's deliberation; publication alongside the decision or with a justified delay serves public scrutiny. These functions are distinct. A report first received on release cannot inform a completed decision. Publication itself may influence market expectations, so the communication design must be evaluated alongside information fairness, confidentiality, leakage and security. Statutory duties and independence may need legislation even if the bank's policy objectives remain unchanged.

A mandatory written response, where lawfully established, requires reason-giving rather than agreement. The committee may contest assumptions, explain its weighting of authorized objectives, or note that a response is the responsibility of another institution. It should not be obliged to change the rate or adopt a new mandate. A report may improve accountability without changing a decision, and a changed decision is not automatically better. Compare existing internal analyses, independent research, parliamentary capacity and no extra reporting using predeclared endpoints. Do not equate the production of a report with successful correction.

### 3.5 Model Humility Infrastructure

The original proposes three complementary components for an institution that takes its analytical limitations seriously. The aim is not to abandon modelling but to improve the capacity to detect and correct consequential model errors. Existing evaluation and alternative forecasts may already perform these functions; compare their coverage and incremental benefits.

First, **institutionalised red-team functions.** The original envisages a standing, well-resourced challenge unit protected from modelling staff, identifying consequential assumptions, testing alternative scenarios and presenting findings alongside the baseline forecast at every policy meeting. Its work would not be an annual compliance exercise alone. The candidate unit needs independent appointment, secure funding, protected escalation, transparent response and follow-up, and independent oversight of its own quality. Compare pooled or institutionally separated resources and external review rather than assuming disclosure eliminates dependence. Access to confidential evidence must remain lawful and proportionate. Evaluate common-model and common-data dependencies, missed material risks, false alarms and opportunity costs. A red team using the same threat model and assumptions may add little independent detection; disagreement counts are not success metrics.

Second, **published uncertainty ranges including the possibility of model failure.** The original contrasts fan charts around a central projection with uncertainty about the framework itself. Verify how actual institutions construct and communicate uncertainty. In addition to calibrated forecast distributions, consider explicit assumptions, alternative models, stress scenarios, possible regime changes and circumstances under which a forecast should be reconsidered. Historical episodes that a model cannot reproduce may motivate complementary analysis. Do not imply that all scenario uncertainty can be quantified reliably. Evaluate forecast calibration and decision value rather than rewarding ever wider intervals. Protect market-sensitive information and distinguish justified confidential material from inappropriate obstruction of challenge.

Third, **professional norms supporting the identification of limitations.** Candidate arrangements include recruitment valuing intellectual diversity alongside technical skill, publications critically examining institutional models, advancement recognising substantive methodological self-critique, and protection of those who raise well-founded concerns. The original's young-economist vignette motivates testing whether current reward structures discourage challenge; it is not evidence of any actual penalty or mental state. Do not incentivise contrarianism as an end in itself. Require independent assessment of whether protected criticism improves accuracy or decisions, and whether the same protections and standards apply to critiques of the proposed Assessment and other reforms.

### 3.6 Deliberative Infrastructure for Monetary Policy

The design seeks democratic scrutiny without political direction of individual instrument decisions. Whether additional deliberative arrangements are necessary depends on existing hearings, minutes, mandates and routes of challenge. The original proposes two components.

First, **citizens' assemblies on monetary policy trade-offs.** Random selection with demographic inclusion, a defined deliberative period, balanced expert testimony from central bank officials, economists and civil society, professional facilitation and public reasoned nonbinding recommendations are the core design. Example agenda questions from the original are the relative weight of price stability and employment, distributional consequences of asset purchases, moral hazard associated with prolonged low rates, and financial exposure to climate risk. The assembly does not set the policy rate or confer legal authorization for a new objective. Competent democratic institutions retain goal-setting authority under applicable law. The design needs accessible participation, compensation, conflict disclosure, dissent records, transparent agenda selection, representativeness limits and protection from coercion. The relevant outcomes include understanding, quality of reasons, inclusion and actual accountable response rather than agreement with the report's proposals.

The original cites assemblies in Ireland on marriage equality, abortion and climate and the French Citizens' Convention on Climate. Verify their process, outputs, actual implementation and public legitimacy rather than treating them as proof of effectiveness in monetary policy. Complex monetary decisions may have different confidentiality, timing and expertise requirements. A pilot can investigate transferability without assuming that its participants represent every affected person or that participation establishes consent to policy.

Second, **expanded parliamentary analytical capacity.** The original proposes a dedicated parliamentary office for monetary-policy analysis, analogous in function to the Congressional Budget Office or the Office for Budget Responsibility. It would offer independent analysis of mandate performance, distribution, fiscal implications and scenarios not adequately covered by the central bank's own reports. Legislators could examine evidence and question decisions without instructing a rate-setting committee. Compare existing legislative research, fiscal councils, auditors and oversight mechanisms. Define statutory powers, independent funding and appointments, information boundaries, methods and correction procedures. A new analytical office also faces model dependence and political capture risks. Evaluate the marginal benefit and burden, including whether existing capacity could be strengthened more simply. Neither a public assembly nor a parliamentary office establishes by its existence that independence and legitimacy have improved.

### 3.7 Distributed Sensing for the Financial System

The original proposes drawing on four distributed data streams: household and firm transactions, financial-institution balance sheets, corporate supply chains, and environmental geospatial monitoring. It mentions payment-system information on consumption, market-infrastructure data on prices, volumes and exposures, and satellite imagery of agriculture, urban development and environmental conditions. These are candidate sources, not evidence that the data exist at usable quality or that a central bank has the right to collect them. For a specific task, determine whether existing aggregate or research evidence is already adequate and what further distinction would materially improve the authorized decision. New sensing is not a necessary condition in general.

The original imagines the central bank as a platform integrating heterogeneous streams into a coherent picture. That option must itself face the Pretence of Knowledge critique. A platform may amplify correlated errors, strip local context, concentrate authority or create false precision. Compare federated analysis, secure queries, statistical aggregation and sampling, independent research providers and distributed institutions with appropriate local knowledge. The relevant owner might be a statistical agency, prudential supervisor, fiscal authority or environmental body rather than a monetary committee. Document provenance, coverage, uncertainty, bias and the risk of using proxies that do not represent the affected group. Real-time availability does not establish timeliness of reliable causal inference; a transaction is not a direct observation of the welfare effect of a policy.

The original explicitly warns that the central bank should not become a surveillance state. Strengthen that constraint through lawful purpose, necessity, data minimisation, security, independently audited access, retention limits, transparent aggregate reporting, and correction and remedy. Non-identifiability cannot be assumed simply because names are removed: assess inference, linkage and reidentification, group harms and distribution of burdens and benefits. Consider community governance where appropriate; individual consent alone does not cure power asymmetries. Refusal and limits on data collection are not automatically observational defects. Cross-use for supervision, enforcement or other purposes requires an independently valid basis. Do not publish microdata in the name of transparency. If a proportionate and authorized design cannot be established, redesign or stop. Assess incremental decision value and privacy costs against existing alternatives before investing in infrastructure.
