## 4. The Political Immune System: The Pretence of Knowledge

### 4.1 The Pretence of Knowledge Defined

The political immune system is a metaphor for institutional patterns that may protect established arrangements from effective challenge. The original compared bureaucratic inertia in Germany, the Stability Bias in Japan, the Extraction Coalition in Nigeria, the Security First Responder in Israel, the Administrative Imperative in healthcare and the Performative Reform Trap in universities. These are historical research labels from other reports, not findings imported into this one. Their applicability requires evidence and the relevant correction status must be respected.

For central banking the original uses the Pretence of Knowledge, drawing on Hayek's critique of economic modelling confidence. The hypothesis is that professional identity, intellectual investment and institutional legitimacy may sometimes discourage examination of consequential assumptions. It is not an assertion of cynicism or dishonesty. The original's young economist trained in DSGE, representative agents and linearised dynamics is a fictional illustration of potential professional incentives, not evidence that economists cannot think outside those models. A person may be both an accomplished practitioner and a rigorous critic of a modelling tradition.

The report proposes two distinct pathways. A critic of a crisis forecast might encounter an institution that commissions research on financial frictions within its established framework. This might be procedural absorption of a more fundamental concern, but it might also be substantive and appropriate model improvement. Examine the original criticism, the proposed alternatives, the revised model's performance, committee use and follow-up. A civil society organisation requesting distributional analysis might receive a response that uncertainty or the existing mandate limits the scope of the analysis. This could deflect accountability, or it could be a well-grounded statement of legal responsibilities or evidential limitations. Determine whether relevant information reaches the competent authority, whether the reasons are sound and whether other feasible remedies exist. Do not treat a refusal as proof of closure.

The original worries that credibility can depend on projecting confidence and that admitting limitations might be perceived as threatening that credibility. The rival hypothesis is that credible commitment and transparent uncertainty are compatible and that existing scrutiny is meaningful. H5 tests these alternatives through documented challenge, reason-giving, correction and protected dissent. The same test applies to the proposed Assessment, Red Team and data platform. A reform body can become invested in its own analytical methods and treat criticism as a threat. The diagnosis is not established simply because an institution disagrees with the author.

### 4.2 Who Benefits—Named Honestly

The original identifies five groups whose incentives may shape the stability of the current arrangement. Treat these as symmetric, testable hypotheses about institutions and behaviour, not allegations about the motivations of particular individuals. Benefits, costs, conflicts and countervailing incentives should be documented, as should variation within each group.

**Central bankers themselves.** Professional expertise, established career paths, institutional prestige and long investment in particular methods could make changes to analytical practice costly. Conversely, professional responsibility, independent scrutiny and a commitment to forecast accuracy could reward correction and methodological diversity. Examine recruitment, promotion, funding of alternative approaches, responses to criticism and observable decisions. Do not infer resistance from expertise alone.

**Academic economists in the DSGE tradition.** The original draws attention to journals, hiring, promotion and conferences as institutions that may favour established paradigms. A shift in research priorities could change the value of particular intellectual investments. Rival possibilities include research competition that rewards novelty, development of heterogeneous-agent and nonlinear models, and collaboration with other disciplines. Investigate actual publication, hiring and model adoption patterns; do not treat academic participation as evidence of self-interest or closure.

**Financial institutions.** A predictable policy environment may be valuable for pricing, contracts, liquidity planning and risk management. The original hypothesises that institutions benefiting from predictable conditions might favour established central-bank practices. But financial institutions can also benefit from improved stability, accurate risk disclosure and credible correction; their exposures and incentives differ. A policy that benefits one asset class may harm another. Trace actual positions, financing effects and behaviour rather than presuming a unified preference for a supposed Pretence.

**Governments and fiscal authorities.** Monetary decisions and balance-sheet operations can affect borrowing costs, revenue, risk and fiscal space. The original suggests that some arrangements may obscure responsibility for allocation or distribution. The alternative is that clear legal separation, fiscal reporting, indemnities and democratic oversight make responsibilities explicit. Verify actual accounting and decision authority. A government may benefit from lower financing costs while still accepting an independent central bank's lawful tightening. Do not infer hidden financing or dominance solely from interest remittances or asset holdings.

**The broader political class.** The original raises the possibility that politicians can deflect responsibility for distributional outcomes by referring to the independence of monetary decisions. The opposite may occur: political debate, legislation, fiscal measures and hearings may clarify responsibility for outcomes. Determine which actors actually possess and exercise authority and how public reasons allocate responsibility. Distinguish criticism of a decision from an attempt to direct an independent committee for short-term advantage.

The original also distinguishes actors who believe in an established framework from actors who benefit from its continuation regardless of their beliefs (original §4.2). These are different possible mechanisms: intellectual commitment may shape which challenges receive attention, while material or institutional incentives may favour preserving arrangements even where their intellectual premises are disputed. Neither mechanism establishes the beliefs or motives of any person or group. Test the two separately through documented reasons, incentives, responses to challenge and actual behaviour, including instances in which beneficiaries support correction.

The five groups are not a single coalition. Some actors may support a change and others may reasonably oppose it. Analyse the concrete mechanisms in particular cases, including incentives for independent research, correction and accountability. A finding that existing scrutiny works or that a proposed intervention is disproportionate would weaken the report's account of institutional closure. No institutional actor is presumed to benefit from an inaccurate model simply because the model is familiar.

### 4.3 The Narrative Strategy

The original's master narrative is that model humility can be a source of institutional credibility rather than a threat to it. This remains an openly advocated proposition to evaluate. It contrasts a credibility based on apparent omniscience with credibility based on honest communication of limitations, rigorous methods and a willingness to correct consequential errors. It does not establish that any specific increase in transparency or democratic deliberation will improve credibility. Market-sensitive publication, uncertain estimates and political interference may create costs. Those concerns deserve the same evidence standards as the arguments for reform.

The original frames expanding observation as “an investment in institutional durability” (original §4.3). That argument has three conditional pathways. First, when relevant distributional consequences are overlooked or left without a credible response, subsequent public experience may undermine the legitimacy on which operational independence depends. Better identification of those consequences, coupled with honest reasons and effective accountability, might protect legitimacy without assigning redistribution to the monetary committee. Second, if evidence of growing financial fragility is missed or discounted, a subsequent crisis may damage confidence in the institution's competence. Independent challenge and better stress evidence might preserve crisis credibility if they lead to proportionate responses by the authorized authorities. Third, climate-related changes in exposures, production or prices may surprise an institution whose relevant analytical assumptions are not revisited; timely evidence and appropriate coordination might improve its capacity to explain and respond to such changes. These are candidate pathways to institutional durability, not predictions of inevitable loss of legitimacy, crisis or climate surprise.

Test the incidence, financial and climate pathways under H4 and the challenge, explanation and accountability mechanisms under H5 using existing episodes and practices. Evidence of durable trust despite narrow analytical coverage, absorbed financial stress, effective use of existing climate evidence, or improved credibility without expanded observation would weaken the respective claims. A rival is that additional uncertain projections, confused objectives or contradictory communications might reduce public understanding, weaken confidence in commitments or create market disruption; more information may also divert resources from relevant tasks. Compare communication clarity, credibility and subsequent outcomes against these rivals, including existing or distributed sources of evidence and legitimate reasons not to expand. The Assessment itself must be evaluated separately under §5.5; adopting it is not evidence that any diagnosis is true.

The original sees the Assessment as a concrete expression of this narrative: the committee explains what its decision is intended to accomplish, the independent analysis estimates how consequences may differ across groups, and the committee publicly explains its judgment or disagreement. The communication is illustrative, not a real central-bank statement. A secure predecision analysis and subsequent public release have different functions. The Assessment can strengthen accountability even if the decision remains unchanged; conversely, an attractive report may offer no incremental value or may inadvertently act as forward guidance. Chapter 5 provides separate evaluation criteria.

Central banks may learn from peers and benchmark their analytical and communication practices. The original cites Bank of England distributional research as a possible lead for such diffusion. Peer learning may improve practices, but the rate of adoption is not an outcome measure and common frameworks may increase common-mode errors. Independent alternatives and legitimate nonadoption should remain available. The reform's aims, uncertainties and potential expansion of responsibilities must be stated openly; the strategy is not to outflank democratic or institutional scrutiny. Acknowledging the genuine achievements of current arrangements is compatible with testing the proposed improvements and with accepting evidence that they are unnecessary. The default outcome remains unknown.
