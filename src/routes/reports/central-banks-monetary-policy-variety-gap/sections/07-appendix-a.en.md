## Appendix A: Value Systems and Policy Mindsets — A Guide for the Central Banking Context

### A Note on This Appendix

The original uses a value-system vocabulary inspired by Spiral Dynamics. Retain it as an attributed interpretive lens and collection of institutional design preferences, not a validated developmental hierarchy or classification of individuals. No institutional capacity or observed behaviour follows from a colour label. Specific claims require observable evidence and alternatives.

### A.1 The Basic Insight

Different policy traditions can emphasise procedural stability, measurable performance, inclusion or integrative problem-solving. The original's concern is that any one emphasis may neglect other legitimate considerations. A legal commitment can protect independence; a technically sophisticated model can improve decisions; public engagement can improve accountability; integration can help coordinate tasks. None is necessarily deficient or superior. Their adequacy depends on how they operate together in a specific arrangement. The portraits below are stylised, not findings about particular institutions or people.

### A.2 The Value Systems in the Central Banking Arena

**Order and Stability (sometimes called “Blue”) — the Institutional and Procedural Central Bank.** The original associates this orientation with legal mandates, statutory independence, committee procedures and procedural integrity. Potential strengths are protection from short-term political instruction, clarity of objectives and consistent decisions that may support credibility. Potential blind spots are procedural rigidity, excessive reliance on a narrow target in deliberation and use of a mandate as a reason not to consider otherwise relevant evidence. A mandate restriction may instead be legitimate task allocation. Test the actual scope of challenge, reasons and correction; no inference from procedural fidelity to epistemic closure follows.

**Achievement and Efficiency (sometimes called “Orange”) — the Technocratic and Model-Driven Central Bank.** The original associates this orientation with quantitative analysis, technical expertise, model development and measurable performance. Potential strengths include analytical rigour, sophisticated forecasting infrastructure and credibility through demonstrated competence. Potential blind spots are reliance on a representation that omits consequential distinctions and incentives to refine a familiar model without comparing alternatives. The original invokes the Legibility Compression Mechanism; its universal version is withdrawn. Model refinement can be appropriate learning, and quantitative expertise may identify its own limitations. Compare actual model portfolios and performance rather than rank practitioners by worldview.

**Inclusion and Care (sometimes called “Green”) — the Accountable and Responsive Central Bank.** The original associates this orientation with communication, transparency, diversity and inclusion, climate and distributional analysis, and the interests of affected communities. Potential strengths include recognition of consequences beyond the target, scrutiny and responsiveness. Potential blind spots are incorporation of public-facing commitments without operational consequence: distributional research that does not inform an authorized decision, climate speeches without effective response, or minutes published but inaccessible to much of the public. These are conditional discrepancies to investigate, not conclusions from the existence of publications or nonadoption. Research and communication can be valuable even without a new instrument; some outcomes belong to other authorities. Assess both substantive responses and legitimate limits.

**Integrative and Systemic (sometimes called “Yellow”) — the Adaptive and Multi-Dimensional Central Bank.** The original describes functional fit, uncertainty tolerance, integration of perspectives and design of mechanisms that connect different tasks. Candidate strengths are recognising interactions and coordinating trade-offs while respecting distinct responsibilities. The original's Assessment and Red Team are expressions of this preference. Potential blind spots include appearing detached, overly analytical or politically unrealistic, and actual design risks from an excessively complex or centralized architecture. Perceived detachment is not proof of design failure; equally a self-described integrative approach does not demonstrate adequacy. Compare simpler arrangements, dispersed knowledge and legal feasibility.

### A.3 The Monetary Policy Variety Gap as a Value-System Configuration Question

The original proposes a translation layer between procedural stability, technical rigour, responsiveness and systemic coordination. That is an advocated design value, not a necessary developmental transition. Different configurations can support adequate governance; some functions may be better distributed across institutions. Test whether actual evidence, challenge, decision rights and responses are effective and whether coordination is proportionate. The Stability–Instability Spiral is not established by a configuration of colours. The same humility applies to this interpretive lens: if it adds no explanatory or decision value beyond observable mechanisms, it can be set aside.
