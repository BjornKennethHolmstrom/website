## Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

### How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek) from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. Several models were prompted in parallel to analyse central banking governance from different analytical angles, applying the Governance as Engineering framework that he had set out. He chose the topic, posed the questions and decided to publish the result, but wrote little of the text himself.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about specific central banks, policy decisions, mandates, figures and sources may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. Like any work of synthesis, it is provisional.

### Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them write from within central banking. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not a central banker, holds no position in any central bank or financial regulatory institution, and has no financial interest in the institutions discussed. The framework applied here draws on control theory, information theory and developmental psychology.

That distance from the central banking community restricts access to the granular, day‑to‑day texture of monetary policy decision‑making. An outside view is free of institutional loyalties and professional orthodoxies, which can sometimes surface questions insiders no longer ask — but it is equally likely to miss what practitioners know and why established practices exist. Feedback, criticism and corrections are welcome.

### The Organizational Reports Series

This report is the fourth in the Organizational Reports Series, an extension of the governance‑as‑engineering framework from nation‑states to the complex adaptive coordination systems that shape our world. All reports in the series were produced in the same way. The first report examined frontier AI governance, proposing a Coherence–Velocity Trap. The second examined healthcare systems, proposing a Clinical Observability Gap. The third examined universities, proposing an Integration Deficit. This fourth report proposes a Monetary Policy Variety Gap in central banks.

The Organizational Reports Series builds on sixteen preceding reports in the Country Reports for Systemic Change series, which proposed governance deficits in nation‑states across a wide range of adaptive capacity challenges. Together, the reports propose a global diagnostic framework — from first‑order deficits of execution and integration to second‑order constraints of velocity and paradigm lock‑in, to the foundational challenges of substrate construction and boundary resolution, and now to the governance of the institutions that shape monetary, financial, and macroeconomic reality. The series does not claim to be complete. It is meant as a foundation on which further analysis, deeper testing, and better design can be built. Central banks — perhaps the most explicitly control‑theoretic governance institutions ever built — may be the domain where the Variety Gap Framework is easiest to test, and where it is most likely to be shown wrong if it is.
