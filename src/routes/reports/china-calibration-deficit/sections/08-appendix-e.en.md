# Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

## How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. Several models were prompted in parallel to approach China's governance situation from their respective analytical angles, applying the systems-thinking framework he had set out. He chose the topic, posed the questions and decided to publish the result, but wrote little of the text himself.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about Chinese institutions, laws, events, figures and sources may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. Like any work of synthesis, it is provisional.

One note specific to the China report: DeepSeek, the Chinese-developed language model that contributed substantially to other reports in the series, declined to engage with questions about Chinese governance failure modes — responses were either filtered to no response or redirected to topics the system was permitted to discuss. Read through the report's own framework, this is itself an illustration of the calibration deficit: an AI system trained on Chinese internet data and subject to Chinese content regulation exhibits the kind of information management behaviour that the report describes in the governance system itself. The China report was therefore generated primarily with Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and Mistral — models whose training data and deployment contexts impose different constraints.

Where several models converged on the same observation, the convergence was given more weight than any single model's assessment. Where models diverged, the divergence was treated as an invitation to examine the analytical lens behind each position rather than simply averaging the outputs. Convergence between language models is weaker evidence than it may appear, however: the models share much of their training data, including the same English-language coverage of China, and can agree on the same mistakes.

## Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them write from within China's institutional core. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not Chinese, does not live in China, does not read Chinese and has no lived experience of Chinese governance. The framework applied here draws on complexity science, developmental psychology (Spiral Dynamics), governance theory, and control-theoretic approaches to institutional design.

That distance limits access to the granular, day-to-day texture of Chinese governance — the unwritten norms, the informal power structures, the lived reality that no formal framework can fully capture. An outside lens can sometimes raise questions that insiders have learned not to hear, but it can just as easily miss what insiders know. This report does not claim insider knowledge. It offers a lens that may or may not prove useful to those who hold institutional positions and are searching for frameworks that make sense of what they are experiencing.

Writing about China from outside China, and without Chinese language proficiency, is particularly difficult. The models drew mainly on English-language sources — academic, journalistic, policy-analytical — so the report is vulnerable to the systematic biases of English-language China coverage, which tends to emphasise certain failure modes and underweight certain strengths. The report tries to compensate by beginning from the development miracle's genuine achievements rather than from the failure modes, and by grounding its prescriptions in Chinese governance precedents rather than Western models. Whether that is sufficient is a question that Chinese readers, and readers with deeper knowledge of the Chinese context, are best placed to assess. Feedback, criticism, and corrections are welcome.

The report refers to related material on this website — the Global Governance Frameworks, the Governance as Engineering working papers and the Country Reports for Systemic Change — which were produced in the same way.

## The Country Reports Series

This report is the eleventh in a series of Country Reports for Systemic Change, all produced in the same way. The first examined Germany through the lens of an execution deficit. The second examined France through the lens of an integration deficit. The third examined Sweden through the lens of a feedback deficit. The fourth examined India through the lens of a synchronisation deficit. The fifth examined the European Union through the lens of a coherence deficit. The sixth examined the United Kingdom through the lens of a control-delivery deficit. The seventh examined Brazil through the lens of an accumulation deficit. The eighth examined Russia through the lens of a power-vertical deficit. The ninth examined the United States through the lens of an integration deficit. The tenth examined Finland through the lens of a throughput constraint. The eleventh examines China through the lens of a calibration deficit.

Together, the eleven reports propose a global diagnostic framework covering a wide range of adaptive capacity failures — from first-order deficits of execution and integration to second-order constraints of velocity and throughput, and from the boundary conditions of authoritarian entropy and authoritarian over-integration to the frontier condition of a high-trust democracy that has largely solved its first-order challenges and is discovering its second-order limits. The series does not claim to be complete. It is meant as a foundation on which further analysis, deeper testing, and better design can be built — and as an honest acknowledgement of where the framework's own limits lie.
