## 1. The Variety Gap: The Structural Mismatch

**Revised research edition, November 2026 (GA-R02-C v0.1, review candidate).** Originally published May 2026. This edition reconciles the capstone with corrected Governance as Engineering Papers VI and VIII (v0.2) and XX (v0.2). It withdraws the universal necessity and threshold claims of the original synthesis. It does not constitute a new empirical audit of the reports or of Papers I–V and VII. The original edition remains in the evidence archive.

### 1.1 What the historical reports contribute

The Country Reports for Systemic Change and the organisational reports began with a recurring question: why might an institution with considerable expertise, resources and legitimate authority fail at a task for which those assets appear well suited? The aim was to develop a vocabulary for investigating architecture, not simply to assign blame. The historical comparison tables in Appendices B–D document **twenty cases: fifteen jurisdictions (including the European Union) and five organisational domains**. The earlier claim of twenty-one cases and sixteen nation-states cannot be reconciled with those tables. A further case has not been identified in the supplied record. Later reports are not retrospectively added to the corpus.

The case analyses include Germany's proposed execution deficit, France's integration deficit, Sweden's feedback deficit, India's synchronisation deficit, the EU's coherence deficit, the UK's control–delivery mismatch, Brazil's accumulation deficit, Russia's legibility deficit, the United States' integration deficit, Finland's throughput constraint, China's calibration deficit, Japan's continuity trap, Nigeria's substrate deficit, Israel's boundary deficit and Spain's integrative closure deficit. Organisational work examined frontier AI laboratories, central banks, courts, healthcare and universities. These are historical report interpretations [HR/H], not established diagnoses or national scores. A jurisdiction can exhibit different mechanisms across tasks, times and affected groups.

The reports used a shared interpretive instrument that evolved during the project, including iterative AI-assisted comparison and synthesis. This is exploratory theory-building. Their recurrence of terminology does not establish independence, prevalence, causal necessity or generalisation beyond those cases. Appendix F describes a prospective test of the proposed vocabulary.

### 1.2 The competent failure paradox

Competence at one task does not guarantee competence at another. A procedural system may be excellent at protecting due process while slow at coordinating an urgent investment. A disciplinary institution may produce deep specialist knowledge while struggling to organise cooperation across disciplines. These are useful puzzles precisely because a single global ranking of institutional quality would conceal the distinction. They are not proof that competence itself causes failure.

There are at least five different ways to investigate such a mismatch, following revised VI and VIII:

| Axis | Question |
|---|---|
| Evaluation | Are the consequential outcomes and affected interests adequately represented in the objective or assessment? |
| Observation | Is the information needed to distinguish relevant conditions accessible? |
| Modelling | Can the available evidence distinguish competing explanations and predict action consequences? |
| Decision | Do decision rules and authority respond appropriately to evidence and legitimate objectives? |
| Actuation | Can the chosen intervention be delivered, within resources, rights and deadlines? |

Failures may occur jointly or separately. Awareness of a problem does not establish authority to respond. An institution may observe a variable it does not reward, or value an outcome it cannot measure reliably. A narrow objective may be entirely adequate for a narrow task. What matters is the relation among the task, the observation and action architectures, and the conditions under which they operate.

### 1.3 The primitives stated

The historical eight primitives are retained as a non-exhaustive working vocabulary, not eight independent variables, a complete taxonomy, or invariants of every governance system.

| Candidate primitive | Analytical role | Question to test |
|---|---|---|
| Observation channel degradation | Information mechanism | Which evidence changes or disappears along the channel, relative to a reference? |
| Variety mismatch | Task-relative inadequacy | Which relevant states cannot be distinguished or adequately handled? |
| Frequency mismatch | Temporal constraint | Do sensing and delivery fit the relevant response window? |
| Feedback failure | Correction-pathway failure | Where does evidence cease to affect correction? |
| Immune systems | Stabilisation or resistance | Which rules and incentives preserve the arrangement, and why? |
| Oscillation dynamics | Outcome pattern | Is there a repeated sequence, and what explains it? |
| Bypass architectures | Alternative arrangement | Which function is supplied by a different route, with what consequences? |
| Performative adaptation | Candidate behavioural mechanism | Is apparent reform decoupled from the intended change? |

One episode may receive multiple codes; some may receive none. The five axes specify what needs to work for a particular task, while the eight primitives suggest mechanisms or patterns worth investigating. Neither catalogue is a universal scalar, and a code is not a causal finding.

### 1.4 The variety gap defined

A variety gap in the revised sense is a task-relative consequential limitation of observation. In the static, noiseless, additive linear model of corrected Paper VI, let D be a disturbance subspace, L a tolerated outcome subspace, C the observation map, and K = D ∩ ker C. A deterministic correction based on Cd must leave d + u in L for every d in D. The necessary condition is K ⊆ L; with all corrections in −D available, it is also sufficient. The hidden consequential gap is dim K − dim(K ∩ L). A rank-count lower bound is dim D − dim(D ∩ L) − rank(C restricted to D). A nonpositive lower bound does not certify adequacy.

Consider D = ℝ² and L = span(e₂). Observing the first coordinate leaves only tolerated variation hidden; observing the second coordinate leaves the first, consequential coordinate hidden. The two maps have the same rank, but their hidden gaps are respectively zero and one. A difference of environmental and observational dimensions cannot distinguish the cases. This is an exact result within the specified model, not a diagnosis of any country.

In dynamic systems the relevant history, feasible policies, noise, causal model and action restrictions matter. An initially hidden state can become distinguishable through subsequent observations, and perfect distinction need not permit adequate action. The empirical counterpart is an investigation of a specified task, not the estimation of an all-purpose dimensionality score. Paper VIII's former composite, intervals and critical threshold have been withdrawn; none is used here.

### 1.5 The legibility compression principle

The useful observation behind this principle is that a representation may discard distinctions. An aggregate mean does not in general determine the underlying distribution. If two different situations produce the same recorded information but require incompatible interventions, a deterministic rule restricted to that information cannot guarantee the desired response in both. Extra information, feasible experiments, robust common actions or changed task requirements can alter this conclusion. A many-to-one representation is not necessarily inadequate; lossless representation of the distinctions that matter may suffice.

The historical reports compare diagnostic codes, economic aggregates, citations, adjudicated cases and performance measures. Each is a lead for examining the difference between what is recorded and what a task needs. Shannon's communication theory and Conant and Ashby's good-regulator result are mathematical background, not proofs that every compression causes externalities. Harm depends on the plant, decisions, incentives and safeguards. Excluded information may be irrelevant to the task, available elsewhere, or recoverable through history. No general crisis trajectory follows.

### 1.6 From observations to conditional explanations

An exact impossibility result needs a specified model, a task and a feasible information/action set. Corrected XX provides a separate action-cover counting bound and a proxy–target ordering criterion; these are not identical to the observational condition. For example, in a finite set of pairs (0,0), (0,1), (2,0), (2,1), a proxy M(a,b)=a and target T(a,b)=a+b have no strict proxy improvement that decreases the target, despite omission of a target-relevant coordinate. A proxy tie can still conceal harm. The feasible set matters; adding lotteries may change the result.

The capstone proposes that some observed institutional problems may be explained by information loss, specialised incentives, obstructed correction or mismatched authority. To establish such an explanation requires source evidence, comparison with alternatives and, where feasible, interventions or credible causal designs. Early-series delay, representation and commons models are historical leads pending their own audits. The mathematical results motivate discriminating questions. They do not turn the case narratives into necessity theorems.
