## 2. Resolution Lock-In: The Dynamic Trap

### 2.1 The mechanism stated

Resolution Lock-In is a proposed explanation of persistent mismatch between an institution's established specialisation and a changing task. Its hypothesis is that a set of mutually supporting arrangements can make a change in observation, goals, authority or practice costly, even when existing routines remain competent on their original terms. Specialisation can be valuable; persistence may reflect legitimate continuity, statutory mandate, scarce capacity, uncertainty or a better decision not to change. Lock-in requires evidence of a consequential mismatch and a barrier to correction, not merely an institution with a narrow remit.

Consider a hospital with a coding system supporting payment and coordination. A proposal to record information needed by a different clinical task may encounter constraints in software, reimbursement, staff time and data protection. Whether the existing resolution is maladaptive depends on that task, available clinical narratives, costs and outcomes. The fact that a code is coarse does not show that the treating clinician cannot understand the patient. The relevant chain crosses several of the five axes rather than collapsing into a single observation deficit.

### 2.2 Illustrative contrasts across domains

The earlier organisational reports supplied the following comparisons [HR/H]. Their proposed specialisations and excluded dimensions should be treated as hypotheses about episodes and mechanisms, not assessments of whole professions.

- Courts: individual dispute resolution versus aggregate consequences of repeated decisions. The appropriate distinction is between the court's lawful task and the separate institutional capacity to monitor systemic consequences. Appellate procedures, legislative review, public records and other channels may already perform parts of that function.
- Central banks: inflation objectives and macroeconomic models versus financial, distributional or ecological effects. Statutory mandates, available data, supervisory functions, policy instruments and other authorities must be distinguished. A central bank may observe effects without being authorised to target them.
- Universities: disciplinary expertise versus cross-disciplinary problems. Specialisation enables rigor; the empirical question concerns the incentives and mechanisms for integration, not whether disciplines are intrinsically blind.
- Healthcare: administrative reporting versus clinical judgement and continuity of care. Standardisation may help as well as hinder; study what information different actors actually need and receive.
- Frontier AI organisations: deployment and development incentives versus safety evaluation and authority to act on it. Distinguish available evaluation evidence from decisions to deploy and the enforceability of oversight.
- Japan's continuity-trap interpretation: established stability arrangements may make some reforms costly. Alternative explanations include demographic, economic, political and international constraints; the historical label is not evidence of inability to renew.

Appendix C retains the original case matrix as a historical hypothesis inventory. It can help identify contrasts for investigation, including institutions that have changed their resolution successfully.

### 2.3 The lock-in reinforcement loop

Four components are proposed, each of which can operate separately:

1. **Professional identity:** specialised roles and expertise may make alternative practices unfamiliar or threaten valued professional standards. Test whether training, role changes and professional disagreement alter adoption.
2. **Incentive structures:** budgets, career rewards and liabilities may favour established tasks. Compare actual reward rules and behaviour before and after a change; distinguish legitimate quality incentives from resistance to correction.
3. **Observation channels:** established categories and reporting systems may leave relevant distinctions inaccessible at some decision points. Trace data availability, access and use, including alternative sources.
4. **Cultural narratives:** shared justifications may legitimate established arrangements. Examine variation across actors and settings, changes over time and the relation of discourse to decisions; do not infer national psychology from a label.

A reinforcing loop is one possible interaction: role expectations support existing rewards, rewards support existing reporting, reporting makes some alternatives harder to assess, and narratives defend the arrangement. Demonstrating the loop requires evidence of each directional link and a comparison against simpler explanations. Simultaneous presence is insufficient. A counterexample may be a highly specialised organisation with effective independent review and low-cost mechanisms for changing its practices.

### 2.4 Ontological capture: when categories shape behaviour

An observation channel is distinct from an objective and from the behaviour that a reporting rule elicits. Ontological capture names a hypothesis that categories used for recording or evaluation can become incentives that reshape the activities they describe. For instance, a funding rule tied to a recorded category can create incentives to classify cases differently or change actual service provision. This differs from passive information loss: the representation participates in a behavioural feedback loop.

A test would distinguish underlying state changes, recording changes, incentives, alternative information and downstream outcomes. A rise in a recorded metric may mean actual improvement, changed composition, changed coding or manipulation. Proxy–target divergence requires an appropriate target and comparator; a strict reversal is not entailed by a narrow objective. Conversely, a reporting change can improve accountability. Evidence of a metric's use is not by itself evidence of harmful capture.

### 2.5 Relationship to the variety gap

Lock-in and observational inadequacy are distinct. A task-relative hidden gap can exist in a new architecture without any history of reinforcement. Lock-in can preserve a harmful decision rule even when the relevant facts are accessible. Their conjunction is an interesting hypothesis: a decision architecture might reduce incentives to investigate omitted conditions and thereby preserve an observational mismatch. To distinguish this from a static resource constraint, examine whether changes in authority or incentives alter access and use of evidence, compared with changes in sensing alone. Neither persistence nor irreversible deterioration is guaranteed.
