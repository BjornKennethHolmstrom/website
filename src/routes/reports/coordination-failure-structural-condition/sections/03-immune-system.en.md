## 3. The Immune System: The Adaptive Stabiliser

### 3.1 Why institutions may absorb reform without changing

Institutions have procedures that preserve continuity, accountability, lawful authority and professional integrity. Some also protect incumbent interests or reproduce arrangements that perform poorly at a specified task. The historical immune-system metaphor refers to this family of stabilisation and resistance mechanisms. It is not an assertion that every institution has an identical defence mechanism or that resistance to a proposal is pathological.

A reform can be rejected because it is bad, unlawful, costly, unsupported by evidence or inconsistent with legitimate competing goals. It can also be delayed because of capture, incentives or a lack of capacity. Investigators should identify the proposal, affected groups, decision rights, relevant constraints and observed actions before attributing a motive. A useful account distinguishes safeguards from obstruction and allows that a challenge to an incumbent arrangement may itself threaten rights or public value.

### 3.2 Symbolic adaptation as a candidate response

Symbolic adaptation describes the hypothesis that an institution adopts the language, procedures or visible outputs of reform without delivering its specified purpose. A university might announce an interdisciplinary centre without providing sustainable staffing or authority; an AI developer might announce safety procedures without specifying who can halt deployment. The announcements are not sufficient evidence of either success or deception. Some incremental measures legitimately precede structural change, and evaluation may not yet be possible.

Revised VIII supplies three candidate implementation criteria: instrument enacted; implementing body resourced; independent evaluation at least two years later. Code each separately as met, not met, unknown or not yet assessable. The criteria are a proposed protocol, not an exhaustive definition of meaningful reform. Where the purpose is emergency protection or short-term relief, an appropriate evaluation horizon may differ. The historical symbolic share and implementation fraction are complements only when they are defined as exhaustive binary categories. Partial and unknown outcomes should remain visible rather than be assigned a false complement.

### 3.3 The immune taxonomy

Appendix B records the historical taxonomy [HR/H]. The proposed forms include procedural constitutionalism, centralisation, consensus protection, capture coalitions, security imperatives, professional disciplinary boundaries, administrative burden and legitimacy signalling. They are not necessarily mutually exclusive or manifestations of a single universal process. Comparable cases might differ in who has veto power, how feedback is recorded, and whether an actor can enforce a decision. Compare actual legal rules and practice, including episodes of successful adaptation.

A prospective coding protocol should distinguish institutional function (e.g. rights protection), mechanism (e.g. a veto), claimed beneficiary, actual effects, and narrative justification. A veto can protect a minority or preserve an incumbent privilege. Coding it as resistance without examining purpose and effect would lose the distinction the framework aims to reveal.

### 3.4 Institutional auto-immunity

The auto-immunity metaphor suggests a possibility: processes intended to protect institutional performance may consume the resources or attention needed to perform the protected task. Reporting requirements may both detect harm and take time away from delivery. Compliance and review may reduce risks while slowing some useful decisions. The net effect, distribution of costs and benefits, and feasible alternatives are empirical questions.

This hypothesis can be examined using time allocation, decision latency, audit quality, errors prevented, harm, workload and task outcomes. Specify the counterfactual: removal of a requirement, better implementation, automation, a different control, or additional resources. A rise in administrative staffing or documentation is not sufficient evidence of a harmful feedback loop. The original proposed terminal phase and threshold are withdrawn; there is no established universal crossing or trajectory.

### 3.5 Power and the immune system

Structural limitations and power preservation are not mutually exclusive explanations. A rule may make a fact difficult to communicate and also benefit actors whose position would be threatened by its recognition. A decision-maker may receive accurate evidence and decide not to respond because incentives favour inaction. Political economy is therefore not reducible to observability. It concerns the distribution of authority, resources, exposure to harm and the ability to demand or enforce correction.

The historical reports suggest questions about repeat players in adjudication, prestige hierarchies in academia, the political legitimacy of central bank mandates, and incentives for AI deployment. These questions should not prejudge motives. Examine who can set metrics, control access, challenge decisions, bear the costs and contest the rules. For example, a legal predictability argument may be valid even where a separate systemic-effects registry is useful; an academic freedom argument may be legitimate even where a funding rule makes cross-disciplinary work difficult. The same outcome can have several explanations.

Reform that changes an information channel may fail if actors with enforceable authority continue to prefer the existing outcome. Mobilising countervailing power may also fail if a new authority inherits unsuitable information or implementation arrangements. But either intervention could be sufficient in a particular setting. The framework proposes investigating perceptual, political and material constraints together, not a theorem that all must be changed simultaneously.

### 3.6 Stored value and the persistence of authority

Accumulated assets, recorded claims and inherited resources can allow the material position of an actor to persist independently of current performance. This may increase influence over appointments, access or the enforcement of accountability, and can be studied as a political-economy mechanism. The original claim that stored value necessarily severs corrective feedback is withdrawn.

A counterexample is an official who retains personal wealth after dismissal but whose office is subject to an independently enforceable removal rule based on observed conduct. The asset stock persists while the authority remains conditional on feedback. To determine whether stored wealth actually weakens accountability, specify the channel through which wealth influences the rule, the incentives to enforce it, and the evidence of enforcement. A stock of assets, a flow of resources, an office and enforceable authority are different variables. Redistribution alone does not guarantee better observation; improved observation alone does not guarantee accountability. The relationship is a research question, not a consequence of storage by definition.
