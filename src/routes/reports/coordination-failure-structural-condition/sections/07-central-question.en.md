## 6. The Central Question: Adaptive Coherence

### 6.1 The question stated

How can institutions coordinate consequential actions across tasks and scales while preserving the local and specialised information needed to make those actions appropriate? This is the central question retained from the original synthesis. It connects distributed expertise, shared infrastructure, authority and correction without requiring that every decision be made locally or centrally. It also allows a task to be performed adequately within a relatively simple architecture.

### 6.2 Adaptive coherence defined

Adaptive Coherence is a proposed multidimensional design aim [IP]: maintain task-relevant distinctions, coordinate where necessary, and retain the ability to revise decisions and arrangements as evidence or legitimate objectives change. It is not a validated scalar quantity, not an assertion of full observational dimensionality at every scale, and not the unique mathematical solution to governance problems. The five diagnostic axes offer one way to record evidence. Coordination outcomes, externalities, rights, distributional effects, costs, legitimacy and accountability must be assessed separately.

Variety in available observations and responses can help match heterogeneous needs. Coordination can help manage shared resources, dependencies and conflicts. Either can also impose costs: more information may overwhelm processing capacity; more coordination may displace appropriate discretion. A centralised system can preserve local distinctions and act on them, and distributed systems can coordinate effectively. No universal ranking follows from a label or a layer count.

### 6.3 Contrasting candidate failure modes and institutional alternatives

The original reports juxtaposed concentrated decision authority with fragmented capacity. The Russia and China narratives proposed mechanisms through which some reporting and incentive arrangements might suppress corrective evidence; the United States and EU narratives proposed barriers to coordination among distributed actors. These are historical interpretations to be tested in particular episodes, not exhaustive categories of political systems. Legitimate disagreement and legal limits can be important explanations of non-agreement; lack of coordination is not always a failure.

The historical examples in Appendix G offer alternative arrangements worth comparing: Finland's foresight institutions, Kaiser Permanente's integration of payer and provider functions, the Basque fiscal arrangement, Irish deliberative bodies and two payment systems. Each concerns a bounded task or institutional domain. Their relevance depends on the outcomes, decision rights, evidence pathways and constraints in that domain. They are not independent demonstrations that the same design mechanism works everywhere. Nor do they constitute a ranking of political regimes.

### 6.4 Candidate structural features

Four features from the original synthesis remain useful design questions:

- **Multi-scale observation:** which distinctions need to be available at which decision points and timescales? What information can be summarised without losing task relevance?
- **Matched authority:** who has the legal and material ability to act on those distinctions? What dependencies and rights constrain that authority?
- **Integration with relevant distinctions preserved:** how can information and actions be coordinated without needlessly losing local context? What are the costs and privacy implications?
- **Designed revisability:** how can decisions, rules and assumptions be evaluated, contested or reversed? What happens when reversal itself has costs or when a protection must be durable?

The relevance of these features is a question for comparison. No claim is made that every selected example contains all four, or that all four are necessary or jointly sufficient. The original fractality arguments in Papers I–II are historical model leads, not independently audited engineering laws of actual institutions.

### 6.5 Honest limits

Adaptive coherence does not choose the objectives of a society. Coordination may be highly effective in pursuing an unjust goal. People legitimately disagree about the distribution of resources, risks, privacy, authority and obligations. The design aim must be subject to rights and democratic or otherwise appropriate forms of legitimacy, not substituted for them. An architecture cannot ensure good intentions, eliminate uncertainty, or solve every political conflict. Institutional designs have transition costs and may shift burdens to people whose experience is poorly represented. Evaluation must include these consequences and allow that no reform is warranted for a particular task.
