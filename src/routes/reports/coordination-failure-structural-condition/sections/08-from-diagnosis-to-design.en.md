## 7. From Diagnosis to Design

### 7.1 The diagnostic toolkit

The proposed toolkit combines the historical eight primitives, the lock-in and resistance hypotheses, and the corrected task-relative profile. The earlier reports are not a collection of independent case studies; the recurrence of vocabulary partly reflects the shared method. That is a useful discovery record but not a validation sample. To apply the toolkit anew, start with the task and relevant parties, not the expectation of finding a deficit.

For example, a service-delivery episode may involve a delay between complaint and repair. Identify the date and endpoint, what was known to whom, whether the causal model was adequate, whether a response was decided, and what implementation constraints remained. A missing record, an unacknowledged complaint, an unfunded decision and a legitimate refusal are different phenomena. Collect routine and unresponded episodes, not only failures. Record rights, resources, feasible responses, unknowns and alternative explanations. The original canonical diagram is replaced by the schema between Parts I and II and the prospective protocol in Appendix F.

### 7.2 Six candidate design families

The six families below retain the substance of the original principles but are **conditional proposals**, not necessary architectural requirements. Each must be assessed against a specified task, baseline, potential benefits and harms, and a review or stopping rule.

**1. Match observation to task requirements at relevant scales.** Trace which distinctions matter to acceptable actions, where they are lost or inaccessible, and whether additional evidence would change a decision. Preserve useful local context where needed, without indiscriminately collecting information. Test an improved channel against a simpler measurement or improved use of existing data. Consider privacy, workload, rights and the possibility that no new sensing is needed. Stop or revise if information has no decision value or creates disproportionate harm.

**2. Coordinate across scales without unnecessary loss of discretion.** Multi-scale governance, interoperable information and shared resources may help with dependencies. Compare nested arrangements, horizontal agreements, central provision and existing architectures. Specify the scope of coordination, conflict resolution, authority, resources and accountability. Subsidiarity can be treated as a design principle about appropriate allocation rather than a universal control-theoretic necessity. Test actual delivery, distributional consequences and coordination costs, including the performance of a simpler single-level alternative.

**3. Distinguish threats to institutional integrity from threats to incumbent interests.** Review procedures, independent scrutiny, grievance channels and conflict-of-interest rules can support correction while protecting rights. Compare the effects of strengthening a safeguard, simplifying it, or changing who can invoke it. A reform that weakens lawful review in the name of speed can produce harm. Test whether the intervention improves correction without undermining legitimate protections, including evidence from those who dissent.

**4. Explore alternative routes around blocked functions, with review of their relation to the core.** A municipal pilot, sandbox, shared protocol or alternative service channel might supply an otherwise unavailable function. Determine whether the blockage is real and whether a direct reform or resource change is feasible. Evaluate access, duplication, exclusion, displacement of political pressure and dependence on the original substrate. A bypass may need a sunset, integration or renewal rule; permanent alternatives may also be warranted. Preserve continuity for users and avoid treating sunset as a universal requirement. Establish conditions for continuation or termination in advance.

**5. Create legitimate mechanisms for revising objectives.** A value architecture is not an observation channel. Mechanisms such as deliberation, public review, independent evaluation and contestation may help institutions reassess goals and priorities. Record whose values are included, how disagreements are resolved and what is constrained by rights. More objectives are not necessarily better; trade-offs may be explicit and unavoidable. Compare changes in objective with improvements in sensing, modelling or delivery. Success requires a legitimate decision process, not agreement with the framework author's preferred values.

**6. Build anticipatory observation where it has demonstrated or plausible decision value.** Foresight, early monitoring, scenario exercises and protected inquiry can make evidence available before an urgent decision. They also consume resources and may generate false alarms, surveillance or speculative overreaction. Identify a feasible intervention, what evidence would change it, and whether action can be taken in time. Compare with better routine monitoring, preparedness and robust action without a new signal. Prespecify costs and validation of forecast performance. An early-warning score is not evidence of useful warning unless information is available at the relevant decision time.

### 7.3 Transition pathways

The original reports propose a range of first steps: protected municipal experiments, regional coordination, independent review, shared registries, revised fiscal arrangements and alternative service platforms. These should be treated as a portfolio of candidate interventions rather than a single required path from periphery to centre. A protected experiment may be appropriate when authority and resources are available, people are protected, and a meaningful comparison can be made. Some reforms instead require central action, legislation, direct enforcement or a change in resources. The legitimate decision-maker and affected population must be identified.

The historical distinction between parametric and architectural reform can be useful when it is made explicit. Holding an observation channel and action set fixed can reveal an impossibility under the stipulated task. Real interventions need not hold them fixed: additional staffing, changed procedures, enforcement or better models can change effective information and action capacity. No blanket failure of ordinary reform follows. Compare resource improvements, procedural changes, architectural alternatives and no-change baselines. If a small intervention achieves the task at lower cost, that is evidence in its favour, not a failure of ambition.

Experiments that attract voluntary adoption can provide useful evidence, but adoption is also influenced by resources, selection and incentives. Mandates can be legitimate and sometimes necessary for collective action; voluntary uptake is not a general epistemic test. Report who was able to participate, who could not opt out, the effects on nonparticipants, and whether apparent success transfers to another site. Specify an exit plan and safeguards before implementation.

### 7.4 The limits of design

The six families are not a blueprint, and no list can substitute for political legitimacy, local knowledge or empirical evaluation. The framework cannot guarantee an appropriate constitutional settlement, a successful coalition or the timing of change. It cannot derive distributional choices from control theory. Tests should record failures, resource costs, displaced harms and unanticipated effects. Where evidence supports a different intervention or no intervention, the proposal should be revised or dropped. The aim is a corrigible programme of institutional inquiry, not an architecture protected from criticism by its own vocabulary.
