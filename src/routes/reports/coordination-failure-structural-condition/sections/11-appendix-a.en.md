## Appendix A: The Primitive Catalogue

The following entries define candidate analytical categories for future use. Cross-references to earlier engineering papers identify historical sources, not newly certified results. Examples refer to the historical interpretations in Appendices B–D. A case may instantiate multiple categories, none, or remain unclassified. The task and evidence determine coding.

### A.1 Observation channel degradation

A consequential change in information along a specified observation pathway, relative to a reference and task. Possible mechanisms include aggregation, missingness, noise, suppression and delay. Compare information available at each stage and alternative channels, not merely the count of indicators. Historic leads: Papers I and III, the clinical and monetary reports. An aggregate can retain all relevant distinctions; an externally inaccessible record can still be available internally. Evidence of loss is distinct from evidence of a harmful decision consequence.

### A.2 Variety mismatch

A mismatch between task requirements and available distinctions or responses. Use VI's static hidden-gap result only under its stated assumptions (Appendix E.1). Distinguish observational mismatch from inadequate actions, objectives or causal models. Historical leads: Papers IV and VI and the commons-related reports. Count indicators and disturbances only with definitions and documented relevance; counts cannot establish the gap in general.

### A.3 Frequency mismatch

A proposed mismatch between time-dependent demands and feasible observation, decision or actuation times. Papers I–II supply earlier models, not audited universal limits. Measure onset, deadlines, stage-specific delays, duration and response quality, including censored episodes. A faster response can be inappropriate and a slow process can be adequate; not all disturbances are periodic. Examples to investigate include health emergencies, financial risk and long-term ecological trends.

### A.4 Feedback failure

A break or inadequacy in a specified correction pathway: information about outcomes is unavailable, misinterpreted, ignored, or cannot lead to an effective response. These are different axes. Identify the authority, rules, information and actual correction, and compare episodes where the pathway succeeds. The historical Russia, China, Sweden and university narratives suggest possible cases, not proof of common feedback dynamics.

### A.5 Immune systems

Candidate stabilisation and resistance mechanisms that preserve an arrangement or its function. Distinguish legitimate procedural protections, incentives, resource constraints, capture and deliberate deception. A mechanism's function for one group can differ from its effects on another. The original Paper V/VII and capstone vocabulary remains historical pending source audit. Appendix B supplies candidate forms for investigation.

### A.6 Oscillation dynamics

Repeated temporal patterns of changes in state, decision or outcome. Do not infer a feedback loop or inevitable deterioration from recurrence alone. Identify the variables, time sequence, possible external shocks, comparison baseline and evidence of coupling. The breakthrough–capture, campaign–overshoot, centralise–fail–centralise, reform–retreat and alignment–deployment labels are historical hypotheses in the reports. Delay and gain models in I–II may motivate specific tests but are not a proof of these narratives.

### A.7 Bypass architectures

An alternative institutional route for a specified function. Examine existing bottlenecks, whether the route is actually new, how it depends on the original architecture, and the consequences for users and others. The bypass-trap hypothesis proposes that an alternative can relieve pressure for reform of a core while remaining constrained by it. It is not inevitable. Payment rails, cross-state compacts, independent research institutions and proposed shared AI evaluation protocols are candidate cases or proposals. Paper VII's formal and political claims remain pending audit.

### A.8 Performative adaptation

A proposed mechanism by which visible reform activity substitutes for change in the specified underlying function. Distinguish genuine gradual implementation, uncertain or delayed outcomes, and deliberate appearance management. Code enactment, resourcing and independent evaluation separately, using the VIII proposal; preserve partial and unknown outcomes. Examine incentives and actual results rather than inferring motive from the label. The historical university, AI, healthcare, monetary and courts examples provide leads. Proxy–target ordering (XX) is a different, precisely defined mathematical question.
