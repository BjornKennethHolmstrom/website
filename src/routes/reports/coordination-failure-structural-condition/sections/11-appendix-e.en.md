## Appendix E: The Governance as Engineering Connection

The distinction between a formal result and an empirical transfer is central to this revised appendix. Corrected VI, VIII and XX are the authorities for the repairs below. Papers I–V and VII have not been audited here; descriptions of their models are historical leads, not new acceptance of their claims. Standard mathematical background should not be mistaken for a theorem of institutional behaviour.

### E.1 Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety

Requisite variety concerns the ability to achieve adequate outcomes given disturbances, available distinctions and responses. In the deterministic action-cover setting of corrected XX, let A(w) be the set of adequate actions for world w. The minimum size of a set of actions intersecting every A(w) is a lower bound on the number of deterministic action alternatives required; worlds sharing adequate actions need not be distinguished. If any A(w) is empty, no such cover exists. This is a necessary counting condition, not sufficiency of a fixed observation map.

Corrected VI uses a separate static additive linear task: disturbance d ∈ D, observed y = Cd, outcome d + u required to lie in tolerated subspace L. A deterministic rule u(y) implies that two disturbances with the same observation receive the same action. Their difference must lie in L, hence K = D ∩ ker C ⊆ L. If all corrections in −D are available, choosing u = −d_y for a representative disturbance with the observed y establishes sufficiency. The hidden consequential dimension is dim K − dim(K ∩ L). By rank–nullity, dim D − dim(D ∩ L) − rank(C restricted to D) is a lower bound, not an exact gap in general. The same-rank example in §1.4 separates these quantities.

No universal difference of environmental and value dimensionalities, no inevitable failure and no national score follows. Paper IV is a historical commons model awaiting its own audit.

### E.2 The Legibility Compression Principle

A fixed many-to-one mapping may not distinguish two states. If they require incompatible actions, no deterministic decision based only on that observation can satisfy both. If both admit a common adequate action, that loss of distinction does not prevent adequacy. Additional observations, history, feasible probing or other sources can change the effective information set. A channel capacity result under specified encoding and noise assumptions is not a claim that every compressed representation is irreversibly inadequate for every future task. Shannon and Conant–Ashby remain bibliographic and mathematical background, not empirical evidence of an inevitable externality trajectory.

### E.3 Latency, gain and frequency mismatch

Papers I and II historically motivate the delay–gain and frequency-gap discussion. The original capstone's approximations K_max ≈ 1/(τ |A|) and f_max ≈ 1/(2τ) are reported historical model expressions [HR], not general control bounds. Stability depends on plant dynamics, controller, feedback, delays and other assumptions; actual disturbances need not be periodic. Neither a universal topological gap nor necessary nesting is established here. Compare response windows, sensing and delivery in the task concerned. A timely response may be achievable through changed authority, different control, preparation or resources rather than a change in the number of governance levels.

### E.4 Signal-to-noise ratio and representation

Paper III's historical representation-chain model combines attenuation and noise. Its reported crossing at two to three layers depends on assumptions and parameter choices that are not audited here. SNR = 1 is not a universal threshold of unobservability: for independent scalar Gaussian signal X and noise ε of equal positive variance, Y = X + ε carries I(X;Y) = 0.5 log₂(1 + SNR) = 0.5 bit. A signal below unit SNR may still carry task-relevant information. Exact recovery, estimation accuracy and decision adequacy are different conditions. Real chains may have alternative information channels, correlated errors and different aggregation rules. Layer count alone cannot establish loss of legitimacy or task failure.

### E.5 The Coordination Failure Tax

Use Q for retained capacity (not G, the historical gap symbol). Under the stipulated model of four sequential stages each retaining a fraction rᵢ = 1 − fᵢ of its input, with fᵢ ∈ [0,1], Q = Q₀ × ∏ rᵢ. If every retention is 0.5, Q/Q₀ = 0.5⁴ = 0.0625. Improving each to 0.6 gives 0.6⁴ = 0.1296, a factor of 2.0736 relative to the first outcome. These are mathematical implications of the retention model, not evidence that real institutional constraints have this structure.

The combined fractional loss is 1 − ∏(1 − fᵢ) ≤ ∑ fᵢ. This follows inductively because combining two loss fractions x and y yields x + y − xy ≤ x + y. Four half-losses yield 93.75% total loss, not a loss larger than the additive sum of 200%. The prior superadditivity interpretation is withdrawn.

A conditional breadth-versus-depth result survives. For a fixed sum of positive retention factors, their product is maximised by equality (concavity of log, or the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality). Starting from four factors of 0.5, an equal-cost budget allowing a total retention increment of 0.4 gives 0.6⁴ = 0.1296 if spread equally, versus 0.9 × 0.5³ = 0.1125 if spent on one factor. Even spending 0.5 on one factor to raise it to 1 gives 0.125, below the spread case, but uses a larger budget. The equal-budget comparison is the appropriate demonstration. This presumes equal cost per unit retention, independent feasible increments and the stipulated sequential product. Different starting points, costs, interactions, minimum requirements and rights can alter the preferred intervention. Paper V itself remains unaudited.

### E.6 Proxy–target ordering and revisability

Corrected XX separates Ashby's counting bound from the proxy–target ordering question. Relative to a feasible set, strict reward gaming involves a strict proxy improvement with target deterioration. Weak monotonic safety excludes such reversals; tie-robust safety also requires that equal proxy values cannot conceal differing target values. The finite example in §1.6 shows the former without the latter. Changing the feasible set (including randomised mixtures) can change these properties. An omitted target-relevant dimension does not by itself imply a strict reversal. Evaluation objectives are not sensors. Incentives and actions may affect exposure and learning, but that is a conditional mechanism requiring a model or evidence. Revisability of objectives is a design proposal, not a mathematically inevitable trajectory of expanding values.

### E.7 Multi-scale arrangements

Nested or polycentric control can be an appropriate design when relevant interactions, observations and action timescales warrant it. Papers I–II historically propose these arrangements; this revision does not assess their complete derivations. Real governance adds rights, jurisdiction, resource costs, coordination, legal constraints and legitimate disagreement. Compare competing architectures relative to the task rather than claiming fractality is necessarily optimal.

### E.8 Relationship to the empirical programme

Exact results constrain conclusions under stated assumptions and can suggest useful tests. They neither establish the model's empirical premises nor imply that the historical report labels are causal findings. The capstone makes no claim to unify the five axes, the eight historical primitives and the formal results into one theorem. Revised VIII proposes task-relative profiles and a prospective validation protocol; the historical scalar index, critical threshold, intervals and trajectory classifications remain withdrawn. Use the report narratives as hypothesis-generation material and Appendix F for evaluation.
