## 2. Structural Mechanisms: Evidence, Procedure and Systemic Effects

### 2.1 What "Judicial Observability" Means

Judicial observability, as used here, is an analogy for access to reliable, relevant evidence concerning a defined legal or governance task. It does not mean that the court must investigate every consequence of its decision. Observability in the formal static model of Appendix C has a narrower definition; neither number of documents nor number of social outcomes is its rank. Access, admissibility, interpretation, authority and implementation are distinct.

The original gives three high-observability examples. The contract case may be linked to evidence of repeated differences between repeat players and one-shot litigants under the same doctrine. The administrative-law case may be linked to evidence of the accumulated effects of review standards on agency capacity. The criminal case may be linked to evidence of disparities in the administration of justice across comparable cases. These are candidate questions, not findings that the disparities or adverse effects exist or that the court can lawfully correct them. The evidence needed to establish an individual breach, an arbitrary decision or a violation of defence rights is not the same as the evidence needed to identify a population pattern. Either kind may be relevant to an institution's lawful task. Where an apparent gap instead belongs to the legislature or an authorized agency, test coordination and response there.

The structural hypothesis is compatible with many intentions and does not imply deliberate suppression. A court's exclusion of material may be appropriate because it is irrelevant, unreliable, unfairly prejudicial or protected. The converse hypothesis is that relevant material may be accessible but systematically neglected or that parties cannot effectively introduce it. H1 asks which stage is the bottleneck.

### 2.2 Courts as Interrupt‑Driven Governance Architecture

The original contrasted scheduled legislative sessions, budgets and committee hearings, and periodic or ongoing regulatory review, with litigation that begins in response to a triggering event. These are suggestive modes of activation; actual legislatures, agencies and courts may combine them. A court may be activated at filing, make interlocutory orders, hear evidence, give judgment and later participate in enforcement or appeal. A judgment published as precedent is a much later event than initiation. Distinguish initiation, procedural access, disposition and publication, rather than identifying them as one trigger.

In the event-triggered analogy, a reliable instantaneous signal of every relevant change can be sufficient for a piecewise-constant state (W3). Delayed or missed triggers can instead leave intervals in which an estimate is stale. The original suggested that harmful regulatory effects or gradual market concentration could persist before a suitable challenge arises. Whether that happens depends on the legal task, interim remedies, agency monitoring, filing decisions and other authorized actors. A sequence of incremental transactions may be relevant to a particular competition-law inquiry; whether the court can consider it is a factual and legal question, not a deduction from discrete activation.

Filing and appeal are endogenous. Resources, standing, deadlines, perceived remedies and expected outcomes influence whether parties proceed; the population of disputes reaching judgment may differ from that of all eligible disputes. Constitutional questions selected for review need not coincide with every question the polity considers important, but legitimate screening and referral mechanisms may still serve a legally defined task. Test selection and delay jointly (W4/W6), and do not infer that a dormant court is failing when another institution is effectively monitoring the domain. Continuous monitoring by every court is neither a formal requirement nor necessarily compatible with its lawful role.

### 2.3 Rules of Evidence as Observation Channel

Evidence rules and procedures determine what information can lawfully reach and be relied on by a decision-maker. The original praised their role in filtering unreliable, speculative or prejudicial claims and allowing direct examination and cross-examination. Those functions may protect the accuracy and fairness of the record; verify actual rules, exceptions and practices rather than assuming perfect filtering or one uniform system. Legal relevance is not equivalent to systemic importance, and relevance does not by itself establish admissibility.

The original's pharmaceutical liability example is useful. Evidence may address whether a drug caused this plaintiff's injury: clinical studies, adverse-event reports, pharmacokinetics and expert testimony. A separate research question concerns the effects of a liability regime on drug development, physician prescribing and the allocation of risk across patients. The second question may call for population-level research and causal methods quite different from the first. Some of that evidence could be relevant and admissible; some may belong in legislation or regulatory evaluation. The original asserted it could not be heard because no party had standing to present it. Standing to sue and permission to offer evidence are not the same. Verify relevance, expert-evidence rules, amici and intervention, privilege and procedural rights for the jurisdiction and type of proceeding.

The form of evidence may shape professional habits. Familiar case-grounded argument may be easier to evaluate than longitudinal studies or complex models. That is a conditional processing and institutional-attention hypothesis, not a theorem of lossy compression. Appropriate safeguards must allow parties to examine methods and assumptions where an assessment is relied upon; where protected data cannot be disclosed for fair challenge, do not rely on it. An exclusion can be entirely justified. The relevant research question is whether the procedure adequately serves the legal task and whether other lawful channels address any remaining systemic question.

### 2.4 Standing Requirements as Sensor Activation Thresholds

The threshold metaphor captures a possible access issue, not a literal sensor threshold. The original described the US-style concrete particularised injury, traceability and redressability pathway; verify applicability by forum and date rather than applying it to all courts. Such requirements can protect separation of powers and ensure an appropriately concrete dispute. Standing is not the evidentiary strength of a claim, nor does standing establish its merits.

Climate harms, algorithmic discrimination and regulatory capture were the original examples of potentially diffuse injuries and complex causation. Collective or public-interest litigation, statutory rights of action and agency enforcement may provide alternatives; the availability, scope and effectiveness of those mechanisms are verification questions. Claude supplied *Massachusetts v. EPA*, *Juliana v. United States*, *Urgenda*, *Neubauer* and *KlimaSeniorinnen v. Switzerland* as unverified, potentially confirming or disconfirming leads. Their holdings, procedural routes, remedies and subsequent effects must be distinguished. None is a legal conclusion of this report.

The original hypothesized an interaction with resources: corporations and trade associations may more readily identify litigable transactions, retain lawyers and develop causal evidence than diffuse groups of consumers, workers or communities. That may produce a selection gradient, but standing rules, funding, public-interest mechanisms and litigation strategy may vary. Verify comparable cases and access outcomes. Future generations may need a legally assigned representative; identifying their interests does not confer standing on any particular claimant. A justified limitation on one procedure is not necessarily a system-wide access failure.

### 2.5 The Adversarial Process as Signal‑Processing Architecture

Competing presentations can expose weaknesses that a single investigator misses, enable affected parties to defend their interests and protect the appearance and reality of impartial adjudication. Those are the original's favourable hypotheses. Comparative procedures may be adversarial, inquisitorial or mixed; no universal epistemic superiority is claimed. Resource asymmetry or aligned party interests may also limit what gets presented, even if the process tests presented claims well.

Consider the original examples: future generations affected by an environmental rule, aggregate effects of a liability regime, legal alternatives neither litigant has reason to advance, and long-run consequences of constitutional doctrine. A dispute may not contain a representative sample of these interests. Evidence of that omission, and of its relevance to the legal question, is required. Where lawful, independent experts, interveners or amici may present relevant information without displacing parties' rights. Where it is outside the judicial objective, public research or legislative deliberation may be the better owner.

Aggregate statistics, systems modelling and longitudinal studies are not intrinsically incapable of being challenged. Their legal relevance, reliability, accessible underlying methods, uncertainty and the means for meaningful rebuttal must be assessed. Introducing new evidence into an adversarial system can increase expert costs and delay; assistance, independent appointments and alternative public-research channels need evaluation against no new mechanism. No aggregate benefit compensates for failure of fair hearing or defence rights.

### 2.6 Precedent as Paradigm‑Preservation Feedback Loop

The original emphasized *stare decisis* as a stabilising device: continuity may permit planning, contracting and equal treatment and constrain arbitrary departures. Verify actual rules of binding and persuasive authority, court hierarchy, distinguishing, overruling and statutory change. Precedent varies by legal tradition and legal question. The analogy with university peer review is historical and conditional, not an established shared mechanism.

Path dependence is plausible: early holdings may constrain later arguments, and the order of cases may influence how exceptions and distinctions evolve. Antitrust doctrine applied to new technologies, administrative review of changing agencies and constitutional interpretation are research domains. A later decision may reconcile apparently inconsistent rules, or a statutory amendment may settle an ambiguity. A distinction may improve legal coherence rather than fragment it. Establish actual holdings and comparable legal facts before counting a conflict. The original's assertion that no institution evaluates accumulated doctrine is withdrawn as a universal claim; examine appellate synthesis and law reform, and reviewer-supplied leads on en banc review, referrals, certiorari and Restatements. H2 tests whether unresolved accumulation produces identified consequences, not whether a growing case count exists.

### 2.7 The Epistemic Black Hole: Selection and Missingness

The original reported a figure of over 90% of civil cases ending in settlement. It did not establish a suitable denominator, case mix, jurisdiction or date for a universal figure; treat it as an unverified lead. A dispute may never be filed, be filed and dismissed, settle before or during proceedings, result in a sealed or unreported decision, yield a publicly accessible judgment without binding precedential effect, or be appealed. These stages have different denominators. A settlement is not necessarily confidential and a public judgment is not a census of disputes. Access to data, publication and precedential effect are different.

Settlement can protect privacy, reduce cost and uncertainty, permit flexible remedies and allow vulnerable parties to obtain relief. These benefits need evidence just as the possible information losses do. A legal system may have a legitimate reason to withhold information, including victim identity, privilege and safety. No requirement to improve population inference overrides those protections. The Epistemic Black Hole is a hypothesis of selective missingness, not a device defined as intentionally destroying information. An outcome of secrecy cannot establish anyone's intent. The original's stronger claims of specific design or ability to "purchase and delete the system's feedback loops" are withdrawn unless independently substantiated as intentional claims; the selection hypothesis does not depend on them.

The original discussed opioid and tobacco litigation and platform liability disputes as examples of consequential evidence possibly becoming unavailable through confidentiality. Verify which settlements, agreements, documents, publication rules and actual archives are involved. In both directions, Claude's review supplied tobacco document depositories and the opioid litigation document archive as possible signal-creation counterexamples; Galanter on the vanishing trial and repeat players, variation across case types and the Priest–Klein selection hypothesis are further unverified research leads. Accessible archives may support systemic inquiry without identifying the population or counterfactual judgment. Do not infer the merits of a case or the precedent it would have produced from settlement.

W4 gives worst-case bounds when a defensible eligible population frame exists. If nonfiling makes that frame unknown, do not assert bounds for all disputes from a filed-case denominator. W5 illustrates the bilateral nature of settlement: a disclosure rule can change the range of acceptable transfers or remove the possibility of agreement altogether, with no determined price or equilibrium. Trial, abandonment, nonfiling and private ADR may be alternatives. Arbitration was not mentioned in the original; Claude supplied it and the *AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion* and *Epic Systems v. Lewis* leads in review v0.1. Their relevance and legal rules need checking. Reporting rules can alter the observed population and access to justice rather than simply add information to a fixed sample.

The Registry itself inherits case selection. Assessments must state their sampling and eligibility frames, unknown coverage and defensible bounds or sensitivity assumptions. Prefer lawful aggregated, sampled or federated sources, including a no-new-data option. No publication of protected material, no forced waiver of privilege, and no inference of wrongdoing from refusal. Test actual incremental benefit and chilling effects separately from H3.

### 2.8 The Remedial Fragmentation Problem

The original contrasted damages, injunctions, declarations and specific performance with design of comprehensive policy. Those remedies have varied scopes and legal bases. A court may order compensation for pollution without designing emissions trading; it may invalidate an electoral map or, where authorized, order redrawing without selecting an entire electoral policy; it may enjoin an unlawful surveillance programme without designing intelligence governance. These examples are candidate illustrations, not universal limits or verified holdings.

Four effects must be kept distinct: relief for the parties, precedent affecting later legal interpretation, binding or erga omnes effects of constitutional rulings, and legally authorized structural, collective or prospective relief. Nonparty rights and fair notice matter. Verify jurisdiction-specific competence and actual enforcement, including Claude's unverified lead *Trump v. CASA* on universal injunctions. Systemic effects do not by themselves turn adjudication into legislation. Even a perfectly informed court may lack authority to pursue a desired policy outcome, while a legally authorized legislature or regulator could act (W2).

Remedial options may also influence the arguments a party advances and a court's reasons. The original's proposed chain—available remedies shape legal reasoning, reasoning shapes precedent, precedent shapes the later framework—is a hypothesis requiring examination of actual records and rival interpretive explanations. It does not establish that every limited remedy distorts doctrine. A broad remedial power may create risks of its own. Compare lawful institutional ownership, implementation, accountability and the rights of affected nonparties.

### 2.9 Weaponised Latency: Procedural Duration as Competitive Moat

Complex litigation may take substantial time through filing, discovery, motions, trial, appeals and implementation. Time can be justified by the complexity of issues and the need for a fair hearing, or it may reflect congestion, resource shortages or strategic choices. The original hypothesized that superior resources permit some actors to sustain proceedings that exhaust their opponents. W6 illustrates heterogeneous budget constraints, not a measured distribution of litigation duration. Conversely, too short a deadline can exclude evidence and harm either party.

The original's platform antitrust example concerns market position potentially becoming entrenched while a challenge proceeds; its pharmaceutical example concerns continued sales while liability proceedings continue; its surveillance example concerns a programme continuing while legality is disputed. These are leads about two distinct effects: the benefit of maintaining the status quo during proceedings, and the cost of exhausting an opponent's resources. Neither by itself proves strategic intent or wrongdoing. Test actual motions, merits, timing, interim remedies, funding and comparable case populations, including cases in which a well-resourced party supports expeditious resolution. Legal aid, judicial case management, adequate capacity and urgent procedures are genuine rivals or remedies.

The original §2.9 (02:95) further proposes a political economy of procedural reform: actors with substantial influence over rule-making—repeat players, the legal profession and incumbent interests—may also benefit disproportionately from existing delays. This is a structural hypothesis about the distribution of influence and benefits (IC-w), not a claim of deliberate obstruction, shared motives or actual misconduct. Under H4 and H5, examine the composition, consultation and decision processes of rule-making bodies, the positions different actors take on case management, legal aid and deadline reforms, and the subsequent access, delay and fairness outcomes. Compare legitimate professional expertise, repeat-player support for reforms and effective reforms adopted despite incumbent opposition. Evidence that influential groups support timely, fair proceedings or that reforms work despite existing interests would weaken the proposed mechanism. A benefit from delay is not evidence of intent to preserve it.

Some delay is itself a serious or irreversible harm: detention or loss of liberty, custody and family separation, eviction, removal and continuing injury. Independently assess applicable rights constraints, timely access to relief, priority listing, proportionate deadlines and interim protection. The stylized two-period proceeding with an independently justified one-period maximum and no interim protection is infeasible even with unlimited funds. No monetary compensation or aggregate efficiency gain makes a rights violation acceptable in this model. Actual limits must be established by law and facts. The Registry's challenge procedures also need a net access and delay test: a new expert dispute may reproduce the resource asymmetry it diagnoses.

### 2.10 The Document Production Crisis: Processing Capacity and Relevance

Discovery can make information available that would otherwise be inaccessible, subject to jurisdiction-specific rules, privilege and proportionality. The original described a shift from scarce evidence to processing bottlenecks and cited Enron's alleged hundred million documents and tens of millions of pages in pharmaceutical cases. These quantities, definitions and typicality are unverified leads, not established estimates. Assess document counts, relevance, capacity, deadlines and the actual use of case management, rather than assuming a universal frequency gap.

W7 illustrates a queue in which the first two documents fill a fixed review budget; an increased volume of irrelevant items can hide relevant evidence under a given ordering, while an accurate filter can recover it. The number of pages is not a measure of independent information or of legal relevance. Artificial intelligence may assist review and synthesis or contribute to larger volumes and common-mode errors; claims about generation, review quality and unequal advantage require evidence. Appropriate independent checks, data security, access, contestability and rights remain necessary. An information system can become a new bottleneck. The Registry must evaluate its own demand on judicial and party capacity rather than presume more reports are better.

### 2.11 The Westphalian Boundary Gap: Jurisdictional Arbitrage

The original contrasted US and French courts with multinational platforms, corporations and financial institutions. Territorial location, personal and subject-matter jurisdiction, applicable law, extraterritorial reach, recognition and enforcement are separate issues. The original's claim that US and French authority simply ends at the border is not carried forward; the actual territorial example at original 02:109 requires dated primary-source legal verification. A court may know about foreign conduct without authority to adjudicate it, or have authority but face enforcement obstacles.

The source examples include locating data processing, intellectual property, manufacturing and transactions in different jurisdictions. These arrangements might facilitate avoidance, or they may reflect legitimate regulatory diversity, operational reasons or effective compliance across borders. Examine evidence of actual obligations, enforcement and conduct; do not infer intent from location. Coordination compacts, existing recognition mechanisms, cross-border insolvency and competition arrangements, and appropriate domestic enforcement are comparators. Claude's unverified *Schrems II* and GDPR Article 3(2) and Chapter V leads are relevant to the data example, not findings or a citizenship-based rule. References to the EU Coherence Deficit and US cross-state externalities are uncorrected country-report hypotheses (original 02:113), not validated evidence of this gap.

### 2.12 The Constitutional Court Problem

Constitutional adjudication can have far-reaching consequences for the allocation of governmental powers and the protection of fundamental rights. That significance is not by itself evidence that the court is acting as a legislature. Legal authority, effect of the ruling, procedure, accountability and empirical consequences require distinct evaluation. A constitutional safeguard may legitimately constrain popular policy preferences.

The original names the US Supreme Court, the Bundesverfassungsgericht and the Conseil constitutionnel, then describes a standing, ripeness and certiorari route as if it characterized them all (original 02:119–125). That transplant is withdrawn. The original claims about each institution remain verification leads, not legal conclusions. Claude's v0.1 review introduced German abstract and concrete norm control, constitutional complaints, binding effect of decisions, expert statements, election and terms of judges; and French a priori review, QPC since 2010, binding effect and appointments and terms of members. All are unverified leads, to be investigated separately by jurisdiction and date, with actual access to review and reasons distinguished from formal availability.

The original identified *Citizens United*, *Dobbs* and major questions doctrine as consequential US examples. The exact holdings and records, including submissions of systemic evidence, legally relevant objectives, scope of relief and subsequent effects must be verified. The timing and selection of cases may influence doctrinal trajectories; abstract review, referrals or other procedures may address different questions. Do not assume the absence of democratic deliberation or impact evidence from case initiation alone. Legislatures and courts have different forms of accountability, neither substitutable for the other. The original §2.12 contrasts constitutional adjudication with legislative impact assessments, committee hearings, systematic evidence-gathering and electoral feedback. Treat these as candidate comparative mechanisms, not a universal description of legislative practice or a claim that courts lack accountability. Compare their actual availability and effectiveness with judicial reasons, fair process, review and applicable constitutional controls; the differences may protect independence rather than indicate a deficit. A popular assembly recommendation cannot determine an individual's rights or compel a judicial outcome.

### 2.13 The Cultural Operating System: Adversarial Epistemology

The original describes an institutional reliance on partisan contest before an impartial arbiter. The hypothesis is that a familiar form of evidence production may become professionally privileged even where other lawful forms could improve performance. It does not attribute a uniform belief or infer a mental state from a judge's ruling. Genuine procedural objections, lack of legal relevance, reliability concerns, resource limits and the constitutional division of responsibility are serious alternatives.

Adversarial challenge may reveal exaggeration, fabrication or error and help prevent a decision-maker from imposing untested assumptions. Conversely, aligned incentives, missing interests and unequal resources may limit what is challenged. Test both possibilities with actual proceedings. The Myth of the Neutral Arbiter names the possible conflation of impartiality with passivity, not a rejection of neutrality or the duty to decide on a lawful record. Compare independent expert mechanisms and mixed procedures without treating any as intrinsically better. H5 is weakened by meaningful challenge leading to revision or justified rejection; sincere belief, objections or nonadoption alone do not establish closure.

### 2.14 How the Mechanisms Interact — and May Fuel the Loop

The original connected activation, standing, evidence and adversarial presentation to precedent and missingness; it connected remedial limits, latency, document processing and cross-border jurisdiction to constitutional effects; and it proposed that professional norms stabilize those interactions. These are candidate connections. The same constraint may protect fair process, a new assessment may reproduce selection, a new tribunal may increase fragmentation, and integration may improve even as cases accumulate. Distinguish the stages and owners; test common-baseline joint effects rather than multiplying unmeasured losses. W8 supplies examples of opposite interaction signs and doctrinal integration, not a model fitted to courts.

The resulting loop is neither a demonstrated conspiracy nor an inevitable structural outcome. It can be tested without assuming anyone intends to suppress evidence. The original's structural thesis and its assertions of deliberate settlement design are not interchangeable: an intentional claim requires independent evidence of intent. H0–H5 specify disconfirmers. An intervention's adoption, failure or rejection does not establish the diagnosis. The rest of the report compares alternative institutional designs under the same constraints.
