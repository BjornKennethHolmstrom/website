## 3. What Building Requisite Judicial Governance Would Look Like

### 3.1 The Principle: Multi‑Scale Judicial Architecture with Matched Timescales at Each Level

The design aim is to preserve ordinary courts' retrospective stabilising functions, including fair process and the rule of law, while assessing where evidence or response capacity is missing for a specified task. The original asserted that incremental improvements could not resolve the gap, yet proposed modest standing, intervention and evidence reforms. This necessity claim is withdrawn. If existing arrangements or modest reforms suffice, that weakens the redesign hypothesis under H0. Compare existing law reform, regulatory monitoring, appellate mechanisms, ordinary court research and no new institution.

The original's three-layer design distinguished ordinary courts addressing individual disputes, specialist systemic tribunals addressing broader or medium-frequency questions, and international coordination for borderless issues. The earlier fractality insight motivates comparison of timescales, not a theorem that all single-scale controllers are inadequate. W3 shows that one appropriately equipped controller can respond at the faster rate in a stipulated model. Separate levels can fail where a trigger or actuator is missing. Actual institutions need lawfully defined responsibilities, coordination, contestability and response times matched to their tasks. Deliberation can be valuable, but serious delay harms require independent protection. No new layer is justified merely by adding information or an abstract dimension. The relevant distinction is functional. A proceeding may need to decide whether an individual claimant has suffered legally cognizable injury; a separate review may need to ask whether a policy regime is delivering outcomes across a population; and cooperation may need to make lawful orders effective across borders. Even when the same evidence bears on these tasks, different bodies may have responsibility for them. It is not the scale of a case, as such, that determines the appropriate authority. The original design should therefore be read as a menu of interfaces rather than a hierarchy in which higher levels automatically acquire greater control.

The original points to specialist competition and environmental institutions, multidistrict litigation and arrangements for insolvency, antitrust and human rights. These are dated leads, not existence proofs of the entire proposed architecture or its legal transferability. Compare the legal status, task, procedural rules, independence, enforcement, costs and actual effects of each. The aim is not to transform all ordinary courts into regulatory agencies or to optimize throughput. It is to identify whether the wider system can protect individual rights and adequately address systemic tasks within legitimate divisions of responsibility.

### 3.2 Ordinary Courts: Preserving the Retrospective Stabiliser

Three modest reform families from the original remain candidate designs. Their feasibility depends on jurisdiction, case type and lawful authority. Existing mechanisms may already serve the task; any change needs evidence of incremental benefit. In particular, time for procedural rigour is not a universal justification for years of delay. Assess timely relief and fair hearing independently of cost savings.

**Expanded standing provisions.** The original proposed recognizing diffuse environmental, algorithmic and regulatory harms at the level of affected populations even where the injury of an individual does not meet a particular threshold. Compare collective actions, public-interest standing, legislatively created rights of action and existing enforcement mechanisms. The original expressly limited its proposed expanded standing to recognition of concrete injuries, rather than abolishing the concrete-injury requirement or turning courts into roving commissions of inquiry. That is a design limit, not an assertion that every jurisdiction has the same injury test. A class action is not automatically a route around standing requirements. Where a legal change is appropriate, identify the competent lawmaker or rulemaker, the precise injury and remedy requirements, the rights of members and nonparties, and the resources for effective representation. Do not assume a court can expand its own constitutional competence. This option may improve access, but may also increase burdens or introduce contested representation; assess both.

**Broader intervention rights.** The original suggested enabling regulators, civil society and academic institutions to present evidence and argument in cases raising systemic questions. Existing amici and intervention arrangements vary; verify whether and how they permit evidence or argument. Compare targeted expert submissions, lawful intervention, independent public research and existing party-led procedures. Require relevance, early notice, access to methods, time and resources for meaningful rebuttal, judicial discretion on weight and admissibility, conflict rules, reasons and review as applicable. No organization acquires a mandate to represent all affected people merely through expertise or participation.

**Institutionalised systemic evidence mechanisms.** The original proposed that a judge might commission or receive analyses of competitive effects of doctrinal alternatives, or regulatory consequences across agency decisions. Such research can inform policy and sometimes adjudication. Any case-specific reliance requires legal relevance and a lawful process for appointment, disclosure, challenge and review. Do not treat an assessment of aggregate effects as determining individual liability or as permission for a court to choose a new policy objective. No ex parte contact. Where protected data cannot be disclosed enough for fair challenge, exclude reliance. If the same evidence can be supplied through an existing mechanism, a new one may be unnecessary.

These three options can preserve procedural protections while changing particular information or access channels, but their actual legal consequences, costs and benefits are unverified. Legislative or procedural rule changes may be required; an apparently modest reform may be constitutionally impermissible. Assess proportionality, urgent relief and effective support for less-resourced parties. Compare no change and stop or revise if the intervention adds more burdens than benefits or breaches rights.

### 3.3 Specialised Systemic Tribunals: Governance Courts for Governance Questions

The original imagined a higher-scale institutional layer for competition policy, platform regulation, climate, financial stability and electoral integrity. Some tasks may require ongoing monitoring or prospective action, but this does not assign them to an adjudicator. A specialist court, an administrative tribunal, a regulator and a legislature have different legal roles. Compare a new tribunal with existing specialist institutions, ordinary courts with expert support, regulators and law reform. The original's five design features remain options:

1. **Expanded evidentiary standards:** aggregate data, behavioural analysis, forecasts and systems models alongside individual evidence, subject to lawful relevance, transparent uncertainty and meaningful challenge. Do not presume the truth of forecasts or replace fact-finding on individual claims.
2. **Broader standing:** specified affected populations, regulators or civil society might initiate proceedings if authorized. Ensure representation, access, notice, rights of nonparties and fair hearing. No automatic collective-standing shortcut.
3. **Explicit governance mandates:** define permissible objectives, the distinction between prospective policy and adjudication, and the scope of structural or collective remedies. A lawful legislative assignment and review are needed. Systemic effects do not authorize discretionary control beyond the mandate.
4. **Multi-disciplinary composition:** legal expertise complemented by economists, scientists, engineers or domain experts where warranted. Appointment, conflicts, competence, recusal, accountability and access to independent challenge matter; expertise does not confer representative legitimacy or override legal protections.
5. **Prospective orientation:** assess likely consequences where the task lawfully requires it, with uncertainty, counterfactuals and independent evaluation; not a substitute for determining past liability or protecting rights.

The original identified the UK Competition Appeal Tribunal, South African Competition Commission, European Commission competition directorate, NSW Land and Environment Court and Kenyan Environment and Land Court. Verify which are adjudicators, regulators or other bodies, the precise powers they have and their actual outcomes. Their existence does not establish the proposed design's feasibility elsewhere. A new tribunal might create duplicative competence, forum shopping, inconsistent rules, greater costs or concentration of unelected power—the very fragmentation the original diagnoses. Establish clear allocation, lawful appeals or review, coordination, enforcement and rights of nonparties. Compare net benefit against existing structures and modest reforms; legitimate nonadoption remains an outcome.

### 3.4 The Systemic Effects Registry: Making the Invisible Visible

The original proposed periodic public assessments of the effects of major doctrinal decisions and lawful use of aggregated settlement information. The objective is to learn about systemic patterns, not abolish private settlement or reconstruct every confidential dispute. The original's >90% settlement figure and claims of intentional signal deletion remain unverified leads. Any identified gap must be task-relevant and not adequately served by existing sources.

Two ownership models appear in the original. Here (§3.4) it describes independent research institutions—law reform bodies, academic centres and policy institutes—with statutory access. In §§3.8 and 5.2 it describes a single independent statutory body. Preserve both as historical options; compare owner and scope explicitly. Plural independent assessments may be valuable under either, and a central body is not necessarily the right data custodian. Existing research, an independent network and a bounded public research function are comparators.

Public output may support antitrust research into competition, innovation and consumer welfare, for instance. Description, causal identification and policy recommendation must be separated. If a court is legally permitted to receive an assessment, party notice, relevance, access to methods and lawful data, ability to examine authors and commission rebuttal, sufficient resources and time, and a version cutoff must precede reliance. There is no automatic judicial notice of disputed social facts. Where fair challenge would require protected material, exclude reliance rather than compel disclosure. Mandatory incorporation and reason-giving are separate design options needing specific legal authorization; nonbinding findings may still impose costs. Public research can be useful without any mandatory case-record role.

Two questions from the original §3.4 are worth retaining as candidate research tasks: whether an industry's settlement patterns vary with the risk of adverse precedent, and whether outcomes differ between settled and litigated disputes. Predefine the eligible population, measures of precedent risk and comparable case types; account for case selection and confounding. Differences do not establish suppressed precedent or causal effects of settlement. Use lawful aggregate evidence only where appropriate; report unknown coverage and respect protected confidentiality.

Tracking settled alongside litigated cases may narrow particular uncertainties. It cannot establish representativeness without a defensible frame or causal effects without counterfactual assumptions. The Registry must report its own selection frame and coverage limitations, W4 bounds only where justified, and uncertainties from nonfiling or private ADR. Compare aggregate, sampled, federated and no-new-data alternatives; protect privilege, safety and victim identity, minimize collection and linkage, establish lawful access, retention and redress. Reidentification, retaliation and chilling effects require explicit testing. No compulsory public disclosure of protected material. Assess whether reporting shifts disputes outside the visible population. A single widely used assessment could also synchronize errors; retain genuine plurality, independent challenge and correction history. The research-only option remains available if procedural incorporation is unjustified.

### 3.5 International Coordination for Borderless Actors

The original proposed coordination compacts among jurisdictions for common standards, mutual recognition and resolution of jurisdictional conflicts, while preserving domestic authority. The EU and US country-report analogies are historical hypotheses, not validated diagnoses. Verify whether a particular cross-border task is underserved, including jurisdiction, applicable law, rights, recognition and actual enforcement; do not infer arbitrage from territorial organization alone.

The original identified the Brussels jurisdiction regime, GDPR and ICC complementarity as existing analogues. Verify the actual scope of the Brussels rules, applicable territorial criteria and international transfers under data-protection law (not a citizenship shortcut), and the ICC's treaty jurisdiction and admissibility prerequisites as distinct questions. Cross-border insolvency, antitrust and human rights arrangements may provide further comparators. Neither a universal world court nor the proposed compact is established as necessary or infeasible in general. Compare limited agreements, current cooperation and no new compact, including lawful treaty powers, domestic constitutional requirements, rights of affected parties, appeals and accountability. Cross-border exchange of court or settlement information does not become lawful merely because coordination is desirable.

### 3.6 Precedent Review Mechanisms

The original proposed independent domain-specific review commissions for antitrust, administrative law, constitutional rights and criminal procedure, with fixed terms and a review cycle of five to seven years. The analogy is to law reform commissions: review accumulated doctrine, consult affected parties, evaluate coherence and consequences and offer public recommendations to competent legislative or regulatory bodies. A commission might help identify conflicting interpretations or unintended effects; existing law reform, court research and appellate integration might already do so. Review the actual legal competence of comparators.

A commission, as proposed here, cannot overrule or reopen judgments, direct judges to reach a result or create a substitute appellate hierarchy. Recommendations are not binding; ordinary lawful review routes remain. Define criteria for coherence, avoid confusing legal disagreement with inconsistency, and preserve finality, minority rights and independence. The original proposed judges, lawyers, academic experts and stakeholder representatives. The same conflict test as for the Registry applies: sitting judges may be inappropriate where participation risks prejudgment, conflicts or later recusal; consider independent appointments, former judges where suitable, transparent funding, term restrictions, independent oversight and exclusion from pending matters. Membership is not an authorization to speak for every affected person. No public or confidential case details outside lawful purpose and access. Compare cycle length, costs, alternatives and stopping rules.

### 3.7 Deliberative Infrastructure for Judicial Governance

The original's Digital Rights Assembly would examine privacy, surveillance and data protection. A group selected by sortition with a design aiming at demographic representativeness could deliberate in facilitated small groups and plenaries, hearing technologists, legal scholars, civil society and affected communities, with access to technical support. Selection method, representativeness, accessibility, information quality, disagreement and independent facilitation need evaluation. Participants should be able to question experts and submit minority reports; no assumption of a representative mandate from a sample. Protect affected participants and do not invite discussion that compromises pending proceedings.

Public recommendations may help inform legislative or constitutional deliberation. They neither bind courts nor supply missing legal authority; they cannot override individual or minority rights or determine the outcome of litigation. The original's Irish assemblies analogy is a verification lead: distinguish actual selection, recommendations and the separate referendum or legislative route by topic. Compare public consultation, representative institutions, existing rights bodies and no assembly. Whether the proposal improves public understanding or trust without politicizing adjudication is a testable outcome, not a consequence of the design label.

The original §3.7 additionally hypothesizes that combining informed public deliberation with independent adjudication may yield greater legitimacy than either process alone. Compare the combined arrangement with each separate process and existing institutions using independently defined indicators of understanding, inclusion, reason-giving and trust. The rival is politicisation, misleading claims of representativeness or pressure on judicial independence. Deliberative input does not confer adjudicative authority; lawful responsibilities, rights and the protection of pending cases remain constraints. An assembly that adds no benefit, or undermines fair adjudication, should not be adopted.

### 3.8 The Concrete First Step: The Systemic Effects Registry

The original's bounded first step was an independent statutory body with public appropriation and philanthropic support, a board, multidisciplinary research, statutory data access subject to safeguards, and a defined publication schedule. Its other ownership model appears in §3.4. It suggested three initial domains here (digital-platform antitrust, environmental administrative law and campaign finance), and four in Chapter 5 (adding plea bargaining). Preserve both pilot scopes as options to compare, with the criminal-specific default of aggregate research and no use in individual cases. Do not assume statutory establishment or mandatory record incorporation is constitutional in any jurisdiction. The full design and procedural alternatives appear in Chapter 5.

The original's precedent argument at §3.8 was that a legal institution required to perceive one excluded dimension could be required to perceive others. Read as a conditional institutional-learning hypothesis: a bounded, lawful intervention might create reusable research methods, reason-giving and accountability interfaces. Scope creep is a rival, including additional collection, expanded discretionary power, more delay and displacement of tasks. Further use requires a demonstrated gap, incremental benefit, lawful task ownership and independent rights protection. The ability to build a Registry does not establish that courts should rely on it, much less that new tribunals are needed. Legitimate stopping or nonadoption is explicit. The central banks DIA analogy is a corrected historical proposal, not proven equivalent or empirical authority; other series proposals are uncorrected leads.
