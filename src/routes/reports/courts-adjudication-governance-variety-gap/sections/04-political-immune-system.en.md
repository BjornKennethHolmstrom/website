## 4. The Political Immune System: Adversarial Epistemology

### 4.1 Adversarial Epistemology Defined

The original used an immune-system metaphor to describe institutions' possible resistance to challenges to established practices. Country-report labels—bureaucratic inertia in Germany, Stability Bias in Japan, Extraction Coalition in Nigeria, Security First Responder in Israel—and healthcare's Administrative Imperative and universities' Performative Reform Trap remain historical unverified leads. For AI and central banks, the accepted R4 and R5 corrections constrain the diagnosis: the Deployment Imperative and Pretence of Knowledge are conditional institutional hypotheses, not unavoidable modes of behaviour. No cross-series validation follows.

Adversarial Epistemology names the possibility that professional routines favour information produced through contest between parties, and that some objections to alternative methods may preserve those routines without adequate reasons. A different account is that the procedures protect genuine values: independent adjudication, legal relevance, impartiality and a fair opportunity to challenge. Test whether proposed alternatives actually preserve those values before interpreting objections as resistance. Evidence can be offered and tested in various forms. The lawfulness and reliability of evidence are not defined solely by partisan contest.

The original's three vignettes show how the distinction might matter. A bar association could object to an impact assessment presented alongside the record on grounds of impartiality, lack of cross-examination or policy-making beyond the judicial role. Government legal advisers could warn a parliamentary committee that expanding environmental standing might increase litigation burdens and undermine administrative finality. Parties could object to a court-appointed expert's assessment because it falls outside the proper record or cannot be adequately tested. These are attributed illustrative scenarios, not verified events or descriptions of anyone's motives. In each case the objection may be substantively correct, or may be deflected without a satisfactory account of evidence. A credible response requires analysis of the actual rule, safeguards, costs and alternatives, not an attempt to evade the objection.

The three vignettes also identify different potential bottlenecks. In the first, the question may be whether the report can be contested effectively, not whether lawyers are willing to admit systemic evidence. In the second, the issue may be the actual administrative and remedial capacity of the court and whether an alternative forum already exists. In the third, an expert's independence, appointment process and access to underlying materials may determine whether the evidence can legitimately be used. These are not excuses to presume in advance, nor objections to dismiss by definition. A reform body that answers all three with an appeal to its own superior systemic perspective would reproduce the very institutional closure it criticizes. Establish how objections are assessed and what would lead their authors or the reform proponents to change their positions.

Professional training may reproduce routines as lawyers become judges, teachers, legislators or law reform commissioners. It may also support internal critique, new forms of expertise and correction. H5 asks whether relevant challenge is systematically deflected without adequate reasons. Compare actual objections, subsequent action, reasoned rejection and revisions. Nonadoption, disagreement and sincere commitment do not themselves confirm the hypothesis.

### 4.2 Who Benefits—Named Honestly

The original identified four groups with potential material or professional interests in existing arrangements. A benefit is not evidence of intent, and actors within each group differ. Distinguish sincere commitment to procedural safeguards from material incentives that operate regardless of belief. Test both separately, including cases where beneficiaries support reform.

**The legal profession.** Expertise in pleadings, case law, witness examination and procedural navigation may be rewarded differently from economics, statistics or systems research. The original hypothesized that a shift in the information used in proceedings could alter professional advantage and identity, and that training in cases, holdings and dicta might privilege particular forms of reasoning. Conversely, legal skills in interpreting authority, testing assumptions and protecting rights remain necessary even when complex evidence is introduced. Lawyers may develop complementary expertise and support reforms that reduce delay, improve access and increase the quality of decisions. Test training, professional incentives and actual responses, not beliefs attributed to an entire profession.

**Repeat players.** Corporations, government agencies and other frequent litigants may have the resources to manage portfolios of claims, absorb individual losses, develop specialist knowledge and sustain proceedings. A one-shot litigant—an individual plaintiff, small business or community organization—may face existential stakes in one dispute. Equal formal procedural rights need not ensure equal effective access. The original hypothesized that case selection, settlement, delay and jurisdictional variation might allow repeat players to influence doctrine or retain advantages. Verify actual selection, terms, proceedings, resources, effects and legally justified explanations; do not infer concealment or intent from settlement. A repeat player may prefer clarity, consistent precedent and timely decisions, or support systemic research. The Registry itself may reproduce this asymmetry through expert challenges and board influence. Compare access, independent expert assistance, timing and net costs against existing arrangements.

**The judiciary.** The original suggested that the neutral-arbiter ideal might protect judges from demands to evaluate their wider governance effects. The legitimate reason for a judge to confine herself to a lawful record may instead be independence, procedural fairness, legal relevance or constitutional duty. A judge may value better research while rejecting inappropriate use in a pending case. Test actual decisions and reasons, not the mental states of judges. A Registry board or precedent commission including sitting judges could itself compromise perceived or actual impartiality, create recusal burdens or concentrate influence. Independent appointment and conflict safeguards apply symmetrically.

**The political class.** The original suggested that legislators may benefit from leaving controversial questions to courts and criticizing decisions afterwards rather than assuming explicit responsibility. Delegation can also be a lawful, deliberate allocation of authority, and constitutional adjudication may be a protection against abuses of political power rather than an avoidance device. Investigate specific laws, assignments, accountability, incentives and actual responses. A public research body may allow legislators to assess evidence and take responsibility; it may also create another means to diffuse accountability. The interests of legislators, parties, officials and affected populations are heterogeneous. Do not infer motives from an unpopular decision or legislative inaction.

The original's account of the judiciary also distinguished accountability for legal reasoning from accountability for wider effects. Its example was *Citizens United* and alleged consequences for political finance, electoral competition and legitimacy. These are unverified causal and legal leads, not a finding about the case or judges. An assessment of consequences may be useful to an authorized legislature even when not legally relevant to a court's holding. The distinction should neither immunize judicial conduct from appropriate legal review nor impose a responsibility for every social effect of a lawful judgment. Specify the duty, the competent oversight mechanism, the applicable standard and the actual evidence.

The original illustrated potential political responsibility gaps through two cases: a legislature might attribute weak competition policy to courts' interpretations, while courts point to the governing statute; an executive might criticize inadequate privacy protection, while a court identifies absent enforcement actions or a limitation of the claim before it. These are schematic rival accounts, not descriptions of actual actors. To investigate them, map the legally assigned responsibilities, when and by whom they were established, available remedies, reasons for inaction, and outcomes after referral or legislative clarification. A political actor may support a clear assignment even where it increases its own accountability. A judicial decision may properly invalidate legislation, or uphold it, without being evidence of a deliberate strategy by the political branches.

These four groups need not form a coalition. The original noted possible tensions between the profession and repeat players over procedural rules, between judicial independence and legislative control, and between the political branches and courts that constrain their power. Those interests may align on some issues and conflict on others. An observed stable arrangement can arise from lawful commitments, inertia, resource constraints or shared incentives; it is not evidence of agreement to suppress information. A similar test applies to a Registry's own researchers, funders, stakeholders and governing board.

The immune-system hypothesis is structural and does not require a conspiracy. Where a claim of deliberate manipulation is made, evidence of intent is independently required. A reform organization may develop its own professional interests and treat its preferred model as unquestionable. The same test of challenge and correction must apply to it.

### 4.3 The Narrative That Could Support Reform

The original's master narrative was that adversarial process is necessary but not sufficient. This expresses the aspiration to preserve procedural integrity while supplementing its information channels where needed; it is not a general theorem of necessity or insufficiency. Some systems use other procedures. Adequacy depends on legal task, case type and available institutions. A reform's aims must be disclosed openly rather than strategically framed to bypass legitimate scrutiny.

A possible positive argument is that better evidence can be an investment in the durability of lawful institutions. If inadequate knowledge of the effects of doctrine causes preventable harms, inconsistent outcomes or loss of public confidence, appropriate research and accountable institutional responses might improve trust. Lawyers' skills in testing claims, interpreting law and reason-giving could remain central, complemented by empirical expertise rather than displaced by it. Courts might benefit from understanding consequences relevant to the law; legislatures may benefit from knowing when clarification is needed. Peer learning between jurisdictions could identify transferable methods. None of these possibilities requires uniform adoption.

The original's strongest narrative argument is a conditional durability pathway: if the legal system repeatedly fails to protect rights within its assigned role, or if consequential governance effects go unexamined by any responsible institution, political frustration may undermine the legitimacy on which judicial independence depends. Investigate separately effects on rights, economic expectations and democratic accountability, and the subsequent relationship between those outcomes and institutional trust or attempted restrictions on independence. Durable trust despite a limited judicial role, successful correction through the legislature, or a decline in confidence following an expansion of court functions would weaken the proposed pathway. This is a question about the wider institutional system rather than a justification for judges to decide individual disputes in pursuit of popularity.

Legal professionals' comparative advantage in precise argument, testing evidence and articulating legal principles may be complemented by the ability to examine empirical methods, understand uncertainty and communicate with researchers. An analyst's ability to model a market does not give the analyst authority to determine legal liability, just as a lawyer's legal expertise does not establish the reliability of a causal estimate. The proposed supplement is attractive insofar as the roles can cooperate without losing their distinct standards. Training, existing expert procedures and independent legal research might achieve that aim without a Registry. The original's suggestion that a successful jurisdiction may influence professional peers is a hypothesis about learning, not a reason to reward diffusion regardless of results. Compare actual improvements, institutional fit and adaptation rather than count adoptions.

The rival is substantial. More expert disputes may favour affluent parties; public projections may politicize proceedings, weaken perceived independence, expose confidential information or divert resources from individual cases. Judicial restraint and reasoned rejection may preserve credibility. Compare net effects, including independent rights floors, time, cost, trust, decision quality and the actual legal owner of the response. Professional and public acceptance may be an intermediate indicator, not proof of improvement. The Registry's evaluation in §5.5 is separate from H5's diagnosis of closure. If existing mechanisms perform well or an additional research mechanism causes harm, legitimate nonadoption or stopping is justified.
