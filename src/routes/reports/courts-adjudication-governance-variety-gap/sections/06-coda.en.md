## 6. Coda: The Retrospective Stabiliser

### 6.1 What the Legal System Has Achieved

The original celebrates courts as institutions with accumulated wealth: procedural integrity, institutional memory, reasoned judgments, judicial independence and the protection of the rule of law. These assets matter because individuals may need protection against the state, majorities or powerful private actors. Deliberation and continuity may protect against impulsive exercises of power; a legal order whose decisions can be relied upon may enable planning and peaceful resolution of disputes. These are favourable historical propositions to verify, not universal or comparative rankings. The same procedure may perform differently across case types, populations and jurisdictions. Fairness, access, timely relief and independence must be assessed on the same standards as criticism.

The original's praise of courts' role in constraining executive power and protecting rights does not make every exercise of judicial power legitimate, and adverse systemic consequences do not demonstrate lack of legitimacy. Separate effectiveness, legal authority, procedural fairness, distributional consequences and accountability. A legal system's ability to correct an individual violation may be valuable even where other institutions must address a broader policy problem. A narrow judicial mandate can be a protection rather than a defect. Do not infer that a retrospective institution should take on prospective governance merely because its decisions have far-reaching effects.

The original asks what kind of institutional wealth will matter when legal decisions affect digital markets, climate policy, electoral integrity and surveillance. The candidate answer is not maximal systemic knowledge inside the court. It is a sufficient and lawfully distributed capacity to understand consequential effects where relevant, to decide claims fairly, to challenge unreliable knowledge and to implement remedies. Consider the wronged claimant seeking redress, an accused person seeking a fair trial and a citizen challenging state overreach. Their interests cannot be reduced to a population-average measure of institutional performance. The system may need different forms of evidence and review to protect each. It is possible that a court already performs its part adequately while an agency or legislature needs better evaluation; it is also possible that the court's own access or remedial procedures need lawful reform. Both possibilities matter for the original aspiration.

### 6.2 The Shift

The original envisioned a shift from treating adversarial procedure as sufficient toward supplementing it with systemic evidence where appropriate; from exclusive reliance on case-by-case accumulation toward comparing integration and multi-scale institutions; from missingness through settlement toward proportionate research; and from constitutional decisions without wider deliberative input toward separate deliberative mechanisms. Each is a possible design direction, not a necessary sequence. An assessment's public availability does not change admissibility, and a court's receipt of information does not authorize a new objective or remedy. A new tribunal can create fragmentation, a new Registry can create correlated error and resource burdens, and an assembly can politicize adjudication. These risks are part of the comparison, not obstacles to evade.

The original contrasted legal doctrines developed through railroads and oil companies with current digital platforms, privacy disputes about physical intrusion and wiretapping with contemporary surveillance, and administrative-law doctrines concerning New Deal agencies and environmental regulation with current regulatory challenges. These historical characterizations and the implied effects of doctrinal transfer require verification. The examples pose a useful question: do inherited categories remain adequate for a changed task? They do not establish that a change of technology invalidates an existing doctrine. Legal principles may be deliberately general, capable of application to new facts, and subject to clarifying interpretation or statutory change. An institution may legitimately distinguish cases rather than redesign the underlying framework. The consequences of the choice depend on its legal purpose and actual evidence.

The retrospective function must not be traded away for systemic optimization. Fairness, individual rights, impartiality, procedural protections, finality and lawful division of responsibility constrain the feasible architecture. Supplementation may be best delivered by a separate institution or may already exist. Test the gap, alternatives and the benefit of any authorized change.

### 6.3 The Global Significance

The original placed courts at the end of a cross-series argument about Resolution Lock-In. It attributed success to institutions at the tasks they were designed for and suggested that they are being asked to perform tasks their architectures were not designed for. Treat this as the author's historical favourable proposition and task-relative diagnosis, not an empirical conclusion. Following R5's accepted IC-t, assess dated performance on assigned tasks separately from adequacy for new tasks; establish who assigned them and under what legal authority. Neither success nor a newly identified economic or social problem confers a new judicial mandate.

The university and healthcare accounts remain unverified historical leads. The central banks correction distinguishes objectives from sensors, evidence from mandate and institutional from system-wide adequacy. The AI correction scopes the strategic trap conditionally and does not establish the Commons as necessary. Courts are not a demonstrated limiting case. A successful lawful supplement might offer a research example for another institution, but transfer requires testing each context, and may be unjustified.

The original suggested both successful reform and failure to build the proposed architecture would confirm the framework. This self-confirming framing is withdrawn (IC-o). A demonstrated adequately performing existing system, effective modest reforms, integration instead of fragmentation or justified exclusion of evidence can weaken H0–H5. A new intervention's adoption or failure by itself tests neither the diagnosis nor its universality. Cross-report significance depends on independently supported mechanisms, not a common vocabulary.

### 6.4 The Honest Conclusion

The obstacles identified in the original remain research possibilities: professional norms and material interests, selective settlement records, delay advantages and the difficulty of changing established procedures. Their actual strength and direction are unknown. Procedural objections may be well founded, and a legal system may adapt without the proposed changes. The default outcome is not known. The original's accumulating-crisis forecast is not an established consequence of the formal framework.

The original also identifies resources that might support a bounded experiment: academic and policy research expertise in economic, statistical and behavioural analysis; the institutional analogy of an independent statutory research body; peer learning among legal systems; and public interest in climate, technology, inequality and democratic accountability. These are unverified leads about capacities and opportunities, not a claim that an intervention is feasible or desired. Assess the available institutions and the practical legal conditions, including representation, funding, privacy and legal authority. Avoid using international competition or political pressure as a reason to adopt a mechanism irrespective of outcomes.

The original's wager names the UK with its law reform tradition, Australia with independent law reform institutions and Canada with Charter-era systemic evidence practice. Verify the particular claims and legal feasibility separately. No claim that any has agreed to a pilot. The first step could be research external to courts, a network or statutory body, or no new mechanism. Mandatory case incorporation requires its own justification and authority. The precedent argument appears both here and in §3.8: the institution that can be required to perceive one excluded dimension may develop methods useful for others. This is a conditional learning hypothesis. A rival is scope creep, through excessive collection, judicial obligations without authority, new expert costs or diversion from core duties. Require demonstrated incremental benefit, a specified further gap and lawful ownership; stopping is legitimate. The Registry is not a panacea and may not be necessary.

### 6.5 A Final Word

The original's rule-of-law aspiration remains important: adjudication's procedural forms embody commitments to fairness, individual rights and protection against the state and the majority. These values are not obstacles to be eliminated by a more comprehensive model. A judge who confines her decision to legally relevant and fairly challenged evidence may be fulfilling those commitments. Where a legitimate systemic evidence gap exists, a carefully bounded supplement could make more relevant knowledge available without overriding judicial independence.

Consider the original's judge vignette. A judge receives an assessment alongside a properly constituted record, has an opportunity to evaluate its methods and hear the parties' objections, and explains her decision where the law requires reasons. She might be better informed if the assessment is valid, relevant and fairly contestable. Conversely, an inaccurate or unchallengeable assessment might undermine fairness or accuracy. She must be free to reject it for legitimate reasons, and cannot rely on protected material that cannot be fairly challenged. Her duty is to apply the law, not to maximize an independently chosen aggregate objective. The rights of parties and affected nonparties are constraints, not compensable losses.

The original's concern was that a legal order could be individually fair in particular proceedings while leaving broader consequences unattended. That concern remains intelligible without the claim that every fair judgment must assess all wider effects. The relevant test is whether the necessary tasks are adequately owned and performed across the system, including the protection of individual rights. If a legislative review can identify and correct an adverse effect of doctrine without directing judges or opening protected case records, that may be a preferable institutional arrangement. If a fair legal proceeding requires additional evidence and existing mechanisms cannot provide it, a proportionate authorized supplement may be justified. A reform that creates a single unchallengeable evidentiary channel or puts vulnerable parties at a disadvantage would fail the very purpose of that supplement.

The durability hypothesis is that better task-relevant information and accountable responses may reduce preventable harms and strengthen the legitimacy of the institutions responsible. It is weakened by existing adequate performance, by adverse effects of additional evidence and delay, or by public trust improving without mandate expansion. Institutional learning and procedural humility apply to the Registry itself. The original's closing refrain—"The adversarial process is necessary. It is not sufficient."—is retained as its aspiration to protect valuable safeguards while investigating warranted supplements, not a universal necessity claim. The question is whether a lawful and proportionate supplement improves actual outcomes, and whether the system already has what it needs. An adequate response may be to retain the existing architecture.
