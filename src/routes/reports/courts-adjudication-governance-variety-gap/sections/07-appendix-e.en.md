## Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

### How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek) from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. Several models were prompted in parallel to analyse legal governance from different analytical angles, applying the Governance as Engineering framework that he had set out. He chose the topic, posed the questions and decided to publish the result, but wrote little of the text himself.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about specific courts, cases, legal doctrines, procedures, figures and sources may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. Case names and citations in particular should not be taken on trust. Like any work of synthesis, it is provisional.

### Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them write from within the legal profession. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not a judge, does not practise law and has no legal training. The framework applied here draws on control theory, information theory and developmental psychology.

That distance from the legal profession restricts access to the granular, day‑to‑day texture of judicial decision‑making. An outside view is free of professional orthodoxies, which can sometimes surface questions insiders no longer ask — but it is equally likely to miss what practitioners know, and why procedures that look slow or rigid from outside often exist for good reasons. Feedback, criticism and corrections are welcome.

### The Organizational Reports Series

This report is the fifth in the Organizational Reports Series, an extension of the governance‑as‑engineering framework from nation‑states to the complex adaptive coordination systems that shape our world. All reports in the series were produced in the same way. The first four reports examined frontier AI governance (the Coherence–Velocity Trap), healthcare systems (the Clinical Observability Gap), universities (the Integration Deficit), and central banks (the Monetary Policy Variety Gap). Across all five reports, the series proposes a single recurring mechanism — Resolution Lock‑In: institutions become structurally trapped by the resolution level they were optimised for.

The Organizational Reports Series builds on sixteen preceding reports in the Country Reports for Systemic Change series, which proposed governance deficits in nation‑states across a wide range of adaptive capacity challenges. Together, the reports propose a global diagnostic framework. The series does not claim to be complete. It is meant as a foundation on which further analysis, deeper testing, and better design can be built. The legal system — perhaps the oldest and most procedurally sophisticated governance architecture examined in the series, and the case where the series sees Resolution Lock‑In as most complete — may be the domain where the framework is most demanding to apply, and where it most needs scrutiny from people with legal expertise.
