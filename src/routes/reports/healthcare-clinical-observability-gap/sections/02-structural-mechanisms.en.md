## 2. Structural Mechanisms: Evidence, Authority and Response in Healthcare

### 2.1 What "Clinical Observability" Means

Clinical observability concerns information sufficient for a specified clinical or governance decision. It includes the reliability, relevance, accessibility and interpretation of evidence about the patient's condition and context. It does not mean that all aspects of a person's life must be recorded. Nor does it mean that anyone who receives relevant information acquires authority to act on it. The term is used descriptively, with Appendix C specifying a limited formal analogy rather than asserting a measured observability model of actual healthcare.

The original illustrates how information about diabetes, insecure housing, food access and interrupted continuity might be needed to understand an individual's trajectory. It contrasts a waiting list of 847 patients with the changing circumstances of patient 347. These are illustrative quantities and scenarios. A population manager need not hold identifiable information about every patient to allocate resources appropriately. The relevant question is whether the individual decision can access the needed information and whether the broader system can recognise and address unmet needs without unjustified collection or disclosure. Identify the specific owner of the task, evidence, interpretation, response, authority, constraints and outcome. Follow information through the chain; distinguish failure to record, failure to retrieve, justified exclusion, uncertainty, error, attention, lack of capacity and a patient's decision to decline.

Clinical observability is not the same as clinical autonomy. Independent oversight, clinical standards, budgets and aggregate performance measures can all support care. The hypotheses below concern possible failures in their design or implementation. Their existence or their use in combination does not establish a spiral. Compare the beneficial and adverse pathways symmetrically.

### 2.2 Payment Architecture as Dominant Observation Channel

The original describes payment as the dominant observation channel. This is an institutional analogy about incentives, not a mathematical identity. Payment rules determine certain rewards and obligations, and may influence what gets recorded, how time is allocated and which activities are undertaken. Measurement and objectives can vary independently. W2 illustrates the distinction. Actual payment and accountability arrangements are often mixed and must be verified in the relevant jurisdictions.

Fee-for-service may pay separately for consultations, tests and procedures. Recording a billable event is useful for accountability but does not by itself establish its clinical appropriateness. A volume incentive could increase low-value activity, reduce attention to coordination or improve availability of needed services. The net effects depend on prices, constraints, clinical norms, safeguards, the provider's response and patient access. Compare services and outcomes rather than assume that more volume is either inherently wasteful or inherently beneficial.

Capitation may pay for a defined population over a period rather than each encounter. It could create incentives for prevention and coordination, or for reducing care that expensive patients require. Costs per patient cannot alone distinguish efficient provision from unmet need. Examine risk selection, case mix, access, quality, outcomes and the adequacy of risk adjustment; also examine contracts, minimum service obligations and independent oversight. A provider may observe complex needs while being inadequately compensated for addressing them.

Diagnosis-related groups (DRGs) classify episodes into categories for specified payment or allocation purposes. Reimbursement and coding uses vary. Within a category, social circumstances, clinical complexity and the cognitive work of assessment may differ. Classification can support comparison and budgeting without being an adequate clinical record. Study incentives to change coding and actual effects on care, including possible benefits of standardisation. Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied Swedish DRG/KVÅ allocation and remote-monitoring billing leads; they remain unverified.

No count of three to five effective dimensions or comparison with an alleged patient dimensionality follows. The original identifies specific candidate mismatches between what is rewarded and what matters. A remedy should address those mismatches, not assume that paying for more measured activities improves observation. Reimbursing review of patient-generated information can make review feasible but may also recreate a volume incentive; examine time funding, bundles and existing arrangements against clinically necessary review, queues and actual responses (W5, IC-j). No reward system alone establishes observation or lawful care.

### 2.3 Electronic Health Records as Observation Infrastructure

EHRs mediate clinical communication, decision support, patient access, documentation, payment and reporting. Their design may affect what clinicians can record and retrieve, which matters in time-constrained work. The original's contrast between a narrative account and drop-down menus for pain or other symptoms is a useful design question. Structured fields can make medications, allergies or critical results easier to exchange and detect; they may also constrain context if poorly designed. Narrative can convey reasoning and uncertainty but be difficult to search, duplicate or misinterpret. Test how the forms work together rather than prioritise one universally.

The original asserts that housing insecurity has no billing code and attributes EHR design generally to billing. These blanket claims are not retained. Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied ICD-10-CM Z55–Z65 and Z59 as unverified verification leads. The existence of a code would not establish routine recording or effective use; its absence would not establish lack of information. Examine actual fields, coding practices, access and clinical decisions. EHR histories and purposes differ across jurisdictions and organisations.

Interoperability can matter when a person sees cardiology, endocrinology and psychiatry or moves between hospital, primary care and social services. Different systems, terminologies, access permissions or responsibilities may prevent useful information from reaching a clinician; the same technology may also support a coherent care plan. Identify whether the bottleneck is technical compatibility, permissions, data quality, organisational relationships, expertise or responsibility for synthesis. A unified screen is not a unified understanding. Minimum necessary exchange and privacy constraints matter. The same test applies to any local record format introduced by a Sandbox (§5.4): preservation of external handover and continuity is not optional.

Alerts illustrate a trade-off rather than a necessary signal destruction mechanism. Drug-interaction warnings, screening reminders and abnormal-result notifications can aid care. Poor calibration, repetition, inadequate staffing or ambiguous responsibility may increase missed signals or inappropriate action. The original's claim about dozens of mostly irrelevant alerts requires measurement of actual volumes and dispositions. Compare sensitivity, false alarms, review capacity, priority, time to response and downstream outcomes (W5/W9). Do not infer neglect from an outstanding queue or require a treatment response to every alert. Copy-forward documentation likewise can preserve relevant history or obscure current findings. Assess accuracy, versioning, retrieval and unintended omissions; retain useful records rather than equating length with quality.

### 2.4 The Documentation Burden as Signal Destruction

Clinical documentation supports continuity: what was observed, what was decided, the reasoning and uncertainty, what was done and what needs follow-up. It can also serve reimbursement, legal accountability, quality improvement and statutory reporting. The original asks whether administrative demands have displaced care-oriented communication. Some records serve multiple purposes, and the absence of a subsequent recorded view does not prove they were useless; a record may protect safe transitions or be needed only when a problem arises.

The original reports 30–50% of clinicians' time spent documenting, two hours of an eight-hour Swedish nursing shift, a full working day per week of NHS GP administration, and almost two EHR hours per hour of direct patient contact in the United States. All are unverified leads. The time-use denominator, role, country, tasks and date matter. Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied Sinsky et al. (2016) as a candidate source, not a verified finding. Separate direct care, necessary indirect clinical work, regulatory and financial work, avoidable duplication and after-hours burden. Observe what happens to work when a requirement is removed, rather than assuming that saved minutes become patient contact.

The original's concern about divided attention during consultations is important. A clinician mapping a patient's story into fields may miss cues; a well-designed record may support attention by making prior history accessible. Time spent recording clinical reasoning may be necessary to avoid harm. Measure interruptions, accuracy, patient experience, workload, clinical outcomes and transferred tasks, including work shifted to nurses or patients. W5 shows that new measurement and alert review can exceed the time saved. The proposed Audit must face this same test. Assess documentation requirements individually against their legitimate purposes and alternatives; retain necessary records, including accountability and safety functions. A time ratio is not a measure of empathy or clinical quality.

### 2.5 Specialisation Without Integration

Modern healthcare draws on cardiology, oncology, psychiatry, geriatrics, orthopaedics, endocrinology, nephrology and other specialised knowledge. Specialisation can improve competence and make responsibility clearer. It also creates a coordination problem if different professionals hold relevant evidence or plans that affect one another's tasks. The original's multimorbidity example concerns heart failure, kidney disease, depression and social isolation, with cardiology, nephrology, psychiatry, primary care and social care contributing different perspectives. Its specific suggested treatment interactions are unverified clinical examples requiring qualified review; this report does not recommend medication changes, fluid management or any particular care plan.

The institutional question is whether there is a shared understanding of the person's goals and relevant constraints; whether assessments reach those who need them; and whether there is an identified owner for resolving conflicts, escalation and follow-up. Multiple specialists need not hold identical knowledge or jointly decide every issue. Effective referral, consultation and team continuity could be sufficient. Missing information may be less important than time, access, responsibility or the patient's preference. The care-coordination hypothesis must be tested against existing integration and the added burden of new meetings and records.

The original highlights nurses who detect deterioration, pharmacists who identify a potential interaction, and social workers who know about domestic circumstances but cannot themselves make every clinical decision. Observational ability is not scope-of-practice authority. A limit on prescribing may protect safety and accountability; a failure to provide timely escalation may be a defect. Verify local powers, escalation and outcomes. Professional bodies that set boundaries may have interests in the arrangements, just as payers and managers do. Under IC-aa/H4/H5, test expertise, conflicts, decision rights, professional rule-making, patient voice and actual independent review. Neither the benefit of a professional group nor a disagreement establishes motive or misconduct.

### 2.6 The Legal/Liability Architecture as Observation-Shaping Force

The original proposes that malpractice liability and documentation requirements change what clinicians investigate and record. This is a plausible incentive mechanism, not a demonstration that defensive medicine is universal. Testing intended to avoid a missed serious condition may be clinically warranted; other testing may have little value or expose patients to harm. The appropriate comparison depends on the clinical indication, evidence, liability regime, alternative practice and outcome. No example in this report is guidance on which tests should be performed. Liability may deter negligent practice and support accountability as well as impose costs.

Incident investigations can use records, checklists and protocol compliance to reconstruct events. The original asks whether they also consider workflow, cognitive burden, communication and whether a protocol was appropriate to the circumstances. That question can be investigated by examining actual investigations, changes after incidents and patient outcomes. Do not infer that investigators never consider these factors. Protect confidential reporting where justified and the rights of patients and professionals; do not weaken necessary incident records to improve a time metric.

The original acknowledges potential safety benefits of standardised checklists, especially in surgery and anaesthesia, while worrying about checklists multiplied without regard to marginal value. Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied Haynes et al. (2009) as an unverified lead on surgical checklists. Evaluate benefits, applicability, fidelity, harms and burden with the same standards. A standard can make important signals visible and a local adaptation can introduce error. Independent safety requirements constrain all proposals, including the Information Sandbox. The evidence must justify a specific change; a broad critique of administration does not justify exemption from law, medication safety, consent or continuity records.

### 2.7 Waiting Lists and the Temporal Gap

A waiting list can represent demand that exceeds capacity. It can also support prioritisation and scheduling. A published total or mean wait necessarily aggregates cases; this does not establish how the underlying system assigns urgency. The original contrasts suspected cancer, a stable hernia and chronic knee pain, and claims that dashboard counts cannot distinguish them. These are unverified illustrations of a concern about priority, not clinical classifications. Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied the NHS urgent suspected-cancer pathway, Faster Diagnosis Standard and surgical prioritisation categories as verification leads. Establish actual referral and urgency pathways, availability, reprioritisation and outcomes by period and service.

A backlog initiative may increase total throughput while leaving urgent needs unmet, or may reduce harmful delays across groups. W3 gives an abstract scheduling counterexample: completing the same number of jobs can meet different deadlines. Clinically and legally justified urgency and safety limits cannot be offset by aggregate throughput gains. The actual thresholds and triage decisions belong to qualified professionals and legitimate authorities. Delay can itself cause serious or irreversible harm; compare interim support, reassessment and capacity, not merely the ranking of waiting-time targets. A report that omits individual urgency may be appropriate for population planning if separate clinical pathways work.

Section 1.4 discusses continuity of care and childhood-obesity prevention; the original repeats both arguments here. They are retained once rather than counted as two pieces of evidence. The unique concluding point of the second prevention passage is retained in scoped form: there may be a task-relative mismatch in the time dimension if relevant changes or future consequences do not inform timely decisions. No inherent temporal unobservability follows. Annual budgets and long-horizon investment can coexist. Test anticipation, uncertainty and available response separately.

### 2.8 Algorithmic Observation: Restoration or Intensification?

AI can alter the information and decisions available in healthcare. The original describes restorative possibilities: integration across records, extracting relevant information from narrative notes, ambient assistance with documentation, triage and prediction. These are distinct uses; validity in one does not establish validity in another. Compare systems with current workflows, non-AI alternatives and clinician-only approaches, assessing prospective performance, accuracy, omissions, equity, privacy, safety, actual workload and response. Any technology described here remains a candidate, not a recommendation for deployment.

A model trained on billing or cost proxies may perform well against those labels without identifying clinical need. Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied Obermeyer et al. (2019) as an unverified lead on a cost-trained care-management algorithm. An ambient note system may generate accurate records or reproduce billing incentives, omit relevant details, introduce errors and expand data collection. Underlying data, incentives and monitoring can shape these effects. No binary trajectory of restoration or destruction follows. Beneficial, adverse, mixed and null outcomes are all possible.

A clinician's reasoning may be imperfectly articulated and fallible; a model's explanation is not evidence of correctness. Require provenance, clinical validation, the ability to challenge outputs, clear responsibility, safe fallback and incident review. Human override should not trigger automatic punishment for disagreement, but neither clinicians nor software should be immune from justified independent safety review. Shared data and practices can produce correlated errors (W9). Monitoring should not become surveillance of patients or staff. Evaluate subgroup performance, consent, data rights, security and who actually has authority and capacity to respond. The corrected AI report's strategic feasibility hypothesis is conditional and does not establish a necessary Commons or any particular healthcare governance response.

### 2.9 The Training Environment Reproduces the Architecture

The original proposes a generational mechanism: curricula, assessment, clinical supervision and electronic work environments may train professionals to attend to what institutional systems reward. Standardised examinations and protocol practice may support competence, or may inadequately evaluate history-taking, contextual reasoning, communication and physical examination. Documentation assessment may support safe practice or disproportionately reward completion of fields. Clinical training conditions vary; no generational decline in skill is established here.

The concern about loss of tacit and relational knowledge when experienced clinicians retire is worth testing. Examine curricula, supervision, time use, observed competencies, workforce composition and patient outcomes across cohorts, accounting for changes in technologies, training and case mix. Older practices may themselves have been inconsistent, discriminatory or inadequately documented. New forms of training can improve reasoning and coordination. The aim is to preserve valuable skills and learn from failures, not to assume that one generation sees patients better or that modern tools inherently diminish clinical skill. Training reform should face independent assessment of competence and safety, not merely changes in compliance measures.

### 2.10 Social Determinants as the Most Extreme Variety Gap

Housing, food access, isolation, employment, economic insecurity, violence, pollution and education are proposed determinants or contextual influences in the original. Their causal effects and relative importance differ by population and outcome. The original compares isolation with smoking; Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010) as an unverified lead. Verify definitions, comparison groups, confounding, effect sizes and transferability. Neither the ranking of determinants nor their total exclusion from records is established.

Consider the original's illustrative person with diabetes who faces insecure housing, limited affordable food, work constraints and depression. A recorded biological measure alone may not explain the trajectory or the practical feasibility of a care plan. The term non-compliance could obscure barriers or legitimate preferences. This is not a diagnosis or a recommendation about medication or diet. Ask what relevant circumstances the patient wishes to share, what information clinicians already have, and what support is actually available. The existence of screening or a billing code does not prove meaningful response, and refusal to disclose must not be treated as a defect or trigger disadvantage.

A healthcare team may identify a housing need but lack legal authority and resources to address it. Appropriate ownership may lie with social services, housing authorities or other institutions, with voluntary referral and coordination. Compare adequate existing pathways, strengthened interfaces, targeted support and changes to clinical responsibilities. Do not infer that healthcare institutions should govern housing because housing may matter to health. The preventive benefits of social investment may be delayed and can be studied with appropriate identification assumptions; they are not inherently invisible and may not save money. Assess costs, opportunity costs, equity and outcomes across services without compelling additional disclosure. The task-relative hypothesis is whether needs, information and authorised responses are aligned, not whether the clinical record contains every social determinant.

### 2.11 The Cultural Operating System: The Clinician's Gaze, Administrative Rationality, and the Solidarity Principle

The original describes three normative orientations. The clinician's gaze is contextual attention to the patient: appearance, affect, unspoken concerns, experience and the reasoning that integrates observations. It is a valuable candidate source of knowledge, not an infallible channel. Patients' own knowledge and independent evidence may challenge it. Investigate how work design, time, training and team relationships support or impede it; avoid attributing a universal decline or motivation to any profession.

Administrative rationality emphasises standardisation, measurement, comparison and stewardship. These can support accountability, equitable allocation, consistent safe practice and learning. They may also induce proxy gaming, rigid decisions or additional work. The difference depends on the task, metric, authority, incentives and safeguards. A comparison of common practice against a standard need not disallow justified variation. Independent oversight may protect people when discretion fails. The reform body that assumes its own metrics are superior without testing them would reproduce the concern.

Solidarity concerns the equal worth of people and access to needed care. Equal treatment need not be identical treatment; individualisation can serve equity. Common standards can reduce arbitrary discrimination, while inflexible pathways may disadvantage people with atypical needs. Test both, including patient choice and barriers to access. The original proposes that norms adapt to institutional incentives and in turn reinforce them. This is a hypothesis about actual conduct and rules rather than a claim about shared psychological traits. The value-system lens in Appendix A is interpretive and unvalidated, not a method for categorising individuals or ranking cultures.

### 2.12 How the Mechanisms Reinforce Each Other—and Fuel the Spiral

The mechanisms need not operate independently. Payment may shape record requirements; record design may affect attention; liability may affect recording and testing; specialist organisation and professional boundaries may affect exchange and authority. A backlog target may alter scheduling while capacity constrains response. AI can amplify a proxy or reduce workload. Training and organisational culture may support correction or perpetuate ineffective practices. Social circumstances and long-term needs may interact with all of these. The original's systems contribution is to ask how the joint design affects care, not just how each instrument works in isolation.

W8 illustrates that interventions with similar isolated benefits can interact negatively or positively. The causal links and comparators in H2–H5 therefore need to be investigated rather than inferred from the existence of the feedback diagram. In one setting better records and a new payment arrangement may complement one another; elsewhere new reporting may cancel the saved time. Establish the sequence, actual decision rights, confounders, selection, and outcomes, including harms and successful adaptation. No inevitability follows from institutional complexity. If an existing mechanism or a modest improvement proves adequate, that weakens the original necessity-of-redesign claim (IC-p/H0). Chapter 3 compares candidate designs, not a required transition sequence.
