## 3. What Building Clinical Observability Would Look Like

### 3.1 The Principle: Multi-Scale Observation, Not Single-Scale Optimisation

The original asks for an architecture in which population stewardship and individual care coexist. Its proposal is a useful design family, not a formal consequence that only a multi-scale controller can stabilise healthcare. Fast clinical detection and response, medium-horizon coordination across care settings and long-horizon investment and prevention may call for different capacities. A reporting interval is not the same as the clinical response interval. An existing institution may already perform several tasks adequately, or complementary institutions may divide responsibilities effectively. W1–W3 identify conditional requirements for specified tasks; they do not assign tasks or authority to healthcare institutions.

The design question is which information needs to travel, to whom, in what form, and under what conditions. A clinical team may need identifiable information for care while a population manager needs appropriately aggregated information and an independent safety review may require carefully controlled access. A higher level need not replicate the lower level's entire record. Compare lawful and proportionate interfaces, including local synthesis, sampled and federated approaches, and retaining existing pathways. More observation is not a goal in itself. The individual patient's privacy, consent and rights, safety and adequate resources constrain candidate responses. Assess relevant outcomes separately; do not force them into a scalar target. If current arrangements or incremental improvements meet the task, architectural expansion is not necessary.

The original sees potential precedents in several healthcare systems (Appendix B). These are research leads; actual designs, results and transferability must be verified. A successful design in a particular community does not authorise transferring it elsewhere or collecting additional information. The evaluation protocol in Chapter 5 applies equally to existing and proposed arrangements. None of the following proposals is clinical guidance or has legal or ethical authorisation.

### 3.2 Payment Architecture Reform: From Volume and Cost to Complexity and Signal Fidelity

The original proposes three payment designs, which may complement rather than replace one another. Whether they improve care depends on the clinical task, incentives and safeguards. Reimbursement is not a measurement channel by definition. Test payment reforms separately from documentation or other changes; monitor selection, coding, access, quality, costs and harms across services.

**Risk-adjusted capitation with complexity supplements.** A payment for responsibility for a population over a period could support coordination and longer-term relationships. Adjustments for expected needs, including relevant clinical and social circumstances where lawful and appropriate, could reduce incentives to avoid complex patients. Conversely, adjustment formulas may misclassify need, induce coding changes, exclude people or create excessive collection. Verify existing contracts and risk adjustment, compare complexity supplements against other resource allocation approaches, and examine unmet need, referral, independent clinical outcomes and equity. An actuarial estimate is not an entitlement to collect private information or a substitute for professional assessment. Minimum safety and service obligations cannot be traded against cost.

**Bundled payments for integrated care pathways.** The original's hip-replacement illustration envisages an episode spanning preoperative assessment, surgery and rehabilitation. A bundle may encourage coordination and accountability for the whole episode, but it may create disputes about attribution, costs outside the bundle, eligibility, complex cases and discontinuities at boundaries. Compare existing coordination, separate or mixed payment and bundles with the same populations and follow-up. Specify the responsible providers and how exceptional needs are addressed without exclusion or penalty. The example is a payment design, not a clinical pathway recommendation.

**Pay-for-outcome.** Outcomes such as function, symptom relief, survival and quality of life may matter more to patients than activity counts. They are not interchangeable and may be influenced by social circumstances, patient preferences, baseline health and care from other institutions. Payment based on measured outcomes could encourage useful attention, but also selection, short-termism, distorted documentation or pressure on patients to report favourable results. Compare independent outcomes, patient experience, missingness, uncertainty and gaming; ensure nonparticipation or refusal does not worsen care. No unvalidated composite score or safety offset is permissible. Risk-adjusted payments and outcome measures do not automatically solve the causal attribution problem.

The appropriate combination may be a modest change within existing arrangements, or no change if evidence is insufficient. Assign payment responsibilities lawfully and assess budgetary and clinical trade-offs rather than inferring that reward expansion creates observation. The same incentives analysis applies to funding new review activities, including patient-generated data (§3.7).

### 3.3 EHR Redesign for Clinical Observability

The original's design specification gives priority to clinical communication: a note that conveys the patient's story, observations, reasoning, uncertainties and plan in a readable way. Structured fields can also be important for medication safety, terminology, searches and exchanges. A useful redesign would preserve complementary functions, compare retrieval and entry workflows, avoid unnecessary duplicate records and make relevant information visible to authorised members of a care team. Test clinical accuracy, omissions, provenance, continuity, readability, access and actual burden. A unified view need not require a single central database or universal access. Patients retain privacy and relevant data rights.

Ambient note assistance could reduce the need to divide attention between a conversation and a screen. It could also create a new recording burden, generate plausible but incorrect summaries, omit relevant uncertainties, optimise for billing, or disclose information inappropriately. Compare against nonrecording workflows and existing assistance. Require lawful consent where appropriate, clear alternatives without disadvantage, purpose limitation, security, minimum retention, clinician review and correction, and independent evaluation of errors and harms. No automatic recording, dissemination or clinical decision based solely on unvalidated output. The actual state of the technology and applicable rules must be verified before any use.

The original emphasises shared data formats, terminology and protocols as infrastructure for integration. Technical, organisational, legal and economic barriers are all testable; no blanket claim that standards already solve the technical problem or that vendors intentionally obstruct exchange is retained. Compare interoperability, human handover and local synthesis; evaluate vendor lock-in, maintenance and actual use. A record format local to one clinic may be appropriate only if cross-setting communication remains sufficient. Agree lawful minimum necessary exchange with receiving teams, including emergency departments, hospitals, pharmacies and out-of-hours services. Protected material cannot be compelled merely to simplify an interface. Test cross-setting continuity and safety independently, including missing information and duplication. This is binding for the Sandbox under IC-ab.

### 3.4 Restoring the Clinician's Gaze: Administrative Burden Reduction

The original suggests protecting time to listen, examine, think and coordinate. The historical proposed proportions are seventy percent of physicians' and eighty percent of nurses' working hours in direct patient care, embedded in employment contracts, independently audited and linked to institutional funding. These numbers are not clinical standards or established targets. Role, specialty, patient needs, staffing and necessary indirect care differ. A mandatory ratio could induce misclassification, reduce important work or transfer burdens to other workers. Compare locally justified time protection and removal of low-value requirements with current arrangements; track actual patient outcomes, access, workload, cognitive burden and safety, not a contact-time target alone (W4/W5). No pay or sanctions based on unvalidated metrics. Test who benefits from time quotas and who bears the costs, including interprofessional inequalities and professional interests (IC-aa).

The complementary design is to record for the next clinician while reducing redundant administrative entry. Background extraction of codes and indicators from narratives might save time, but requires validation, error correction, lawful access, oversight and an assessment of net workload. A record can support both care and legitimate accountability; it need not serve only one purpose. Identify the purpose and owner of each requirement, the risk of removal, potential alternative evidence, and actual time and cost saved. Compare simplified templates, shared records, targeted removal and no change. Preserve medication, diagnostic reasoning, consent, handover, safety and legally required documentation. Independent clinical and legal review is needed before a requirement is altered. The original (§3.4, 03:43) proposes clinical communication as the default and explicit justification for additional non-clinical documentation. Retain this as a candidate rule for reviewing discretionary requirements, with legitimate accountability, equity and resource stewardship among the possible justifications. The thirteen protected record categories in §5.4 and applicable legal requirements are outside the scope of this rule for discretionary relief; it cannot authorise their waiver. Test the rule against existing and less burdensome review procedures.

An audit of documentation should not create a new surveillance system. Use proportionate data sources, sampling and existing information when possible; avoid individual punitive rankings and unvalidated inference from EHR activity logs. Measure necessary indirect work and tasks displaced onto other professionals, patients and caregivers. A reform whose monitoring consumes more time than it saves may still have a justified safety benefit, but that benefit must be assessed independently rather than presumed. Do not compensate a failure of safety or rights with a direct-care time gain.

### 3.5 Integrative Care Coordination for Complex Patients

**Dedicated care coordinators** are one possible institutional response to multimorbidity. The original proposes a nurse or GP with additional training whose function is to maintain an overview and coordinate relevant assessments. The coordinator does not substitute for specialists or acquire authority over all treatment. A defined role should specify consent, access, clinical responsibility, escalation and follow-up. Compare team-based continuity, existing shared-care arrangements and targeted improvements, including effects on duplication, staffing and time. A coordinator may reduce fragmentation or become an additional referral bottleneck.

**Multidisciplinary meetings** could bring relevant clinical and social perspectives together, as in the original's cardiology, nephrology, psychiatry, primary care, social worker and district nurse example. This is an institutional illustration, not a recommended composition for particular cases. The value of a meeting depends on relevant participation, protected time, information quality, actionable decisions and patient voice. Meetings can duplicate work or delay decisions. Test whether the same benefit can be achieved by a smaller team or asynchronous exchange, with clear accountability and timely escalation. Ensure that people can decline disclosures not required for their care and that participation and accessibility are supported.

**Integrated care pathways** may specify responsibilities across primary, secondary, community and social care. The original's example of an older person with complex needs involves GP, hospital specialists, community nursing, social workers and care-home staff. Appropriate coordination does not mean that all organisations share the same objective or record. Agree minimum necessary handover, lawful access, responsibilities for clinical decisions and how conflicts are resolved. Maintain individual choice, warranted professional boundaries and independent safety oversight. Common pathways can support coordination while permitting justified variation. Social care and patient representatives should participate in evaluating any proposed changes.

### 3.6 Clinical Leadership with Genuine Authority

The original identifies a possible mismatch between responsibility for care quality and authority over resources, workflows and performance evaluation. It also notes that people responsible for financial stewardship may lack detailed clinical knowledge of the consequences of a decision. Establish actual mandates, budgets, consultation, accountability and response rather than assuming that a managerial–clinical divide is universal. Distributed authority can be appropriate if coordination and escalation work. Additional clinical authority may address a real gap, or create another concentration of discretionary power.

Integrated leadership teams combining clinical and administrative expertise are a candidate design. Physicians, nurses, allied professionals and other relevant workers may contribute to resource and workflow decisions with managers and those responsible for finance. Patient and caregiver voice, safety expertise, transparency, conflicts and lawful responsibility must be included. The original calls for a single set of metrics; a common discussion of evidence does not require a composite target or a single channel of authority. Preserve independent review, competing evidence and separate constraints on safety, rights, equity and stewardship. Assign decision rights clearly, with reasons, appeals and escalation. No proposal makes clinical discretion immune from oversight.

The original describes stereotypes of managers as bean-counters and clinicians as resource-insensitive. These are possible sources of mutual mistrust, not documented beliefs of the groups. Compare arrangements that build mutual competence, including distributed alternatives and genuine shared decision-making. Apply IC-aa to the professional and reform interests as rigorously as administrative interests: contracts, appointments, professional boundaries, allocation and conflicts can influence decisions. Observe independent challenge and correction, including cases where either group supports justified changes or legitimately opposes them. Joint leadership is valuable only if it improves relevant outcomes without weakening safety and patient agency.

### 3.7 The Patient as an Integrated Sensor Node

The historical phrase identifies the potential value of knowledge that patients possess between encounters. It must not reduce people to instruments of clinical or administrative control. Voluntary patient-reported outcomes and experiences can complement conversation and examination; they are different measures and neither replaces patient preference. The original contrasts delayed satisfaction surveys with information available during care, and gives heart-failure weight reporting, depression mood and activity reports, home glucose or blood-pressure devices, rehabilitation trackers and sleep monitoring as illustrative possibilities. These require qualified clinical verification and individual assessment; no monitoring schedule, threshold, diagnosis or intervention is recommended by this report.

Participation must be voluntary and accessible, with nondigital alternatives, refusal and withdrawal without disadvantage, clear purposes and retention limits, lawful consent, privacy and security. Never compel adherence tracking or repurpose clinical reports as punitive performance monitoring. Consider disability, language, device ownership, caregiver burden, trust and capacity to use the system. Missing reports may reflect preference, access, illness or burden; no report is not evidence of absence of need. Do not compel disclosure to narrow uncertainty. Compare the validity of instruments, device accuracy, patient-reported outcome and experience measures, clinical relevance and independent outcomes. Support shared decision-making and preferences without treating every preference as a measurement error.

The proposal needs an actual response architecture: responsibility for review, monitoring hours, clinically justified triage, escalation, backup, patient information and staffing. Do not imply continuous or 24/7 response unless it is provided. A flood of unreviewed information can worsen care. Assess review queues, false alerts, missed harms, response times and actual outcomes; W5 shows why monitoring can increase rather than reduce workload. Compare with existing reporting and care pathways. The original's proposal to pay for review could fund useful time but also create a reimbursable volume incentive (IC-j). Compare time-funded, bundled and usual arrangements, testing clinical necessity, coding, gaming, workload, equity and care. Legal responsibilities and liability need jurisdiction-specific review. Patient knowledge matters even where no new data stream is justified.

### 3.8 Multi-Scale Dashboards

Aggregate dashboards may serve population-level tasks, including waiting times, mortality, cost and throughput. They are not substitutes for individual clinical records or safety review. The original suggests presenting representative patient journeys alongside aggregate indicators: a sample of patients behind a waiting-time measure, or clinical narratives and audit findings behind a mortality measure. The aim is to maintain a connection between aggregate information and actual care, not to give administrators unrestricted access to identifiable stories. A sample can help reveal mechanisms but cannot alone establish their prevalence or causal effect.

Define purpose, sampling frame, eligibility, uncertainty, lawful access, deidentification and reidentification risks, and consent where appropriate. Use minimum necessary information and consider aggregated, sampled, federated or independent review alternatives. Do not display identifiable narratives by default; avoid selection of extreme cases to persuade or shame staff. A representative sample of those in a waiting-list register may omit people unable to access that register. W6 gives selection bounds only for a defined frame. Protect patient and professional rights, and separate quality learning from punitive targeting. The existence of an emotionally powerful narrative does not establish the accuracy of the associated aggregate measure.

The dashboard itself requires evaluation: who receives what information, what decisions it can lawfully inform, how much attention and work it requires, whether it changes access or safety, and whether it adds value compared with existing audit and incident review. Use separate measures and allow divergent directions rather than force a single ranking. The design should enable appropriate referral or correction without transferring decision authority simply because information is available.

### 3.9 Algorithmic Governance: AI in Service of Clinical Observability

The original proposes evaluating documentation systems by clinical communication, triage systems by urgency and predictive models by actionable clinical patterns rather than administrative proxies. These are candidate evaluation questions, not claims of effectiveness. Each application needs a defined task, comparator, patient population, prospective validation, uncertainty, calibration, subgroup effects, false alarms and missed harms, workflow and actual response. Compare human-only, AI-only where permissible, and combined designs. Shared data and training may produce common-mode errors (W9); human review does not guarantee independent errors. No clinical recommendation follows from the illustrative witnesses.

Transparency can support challenge, but an explanation may be inaccurate or irrelevant. Require provenance, clinically meaningful validation, a correction pathway, version control, privacy, consent, security, monitoring for drift, incident review and non-AI fallback. Clinicians and patients should know the system's role and limits. Protect reasoned override against automatic punitive sanctions and unjustified administrative pressure. An override is not immunity from independent safety review, and neither AI agreement nor disagreement establishes clinical quality. Document relevant reasoning in a proportionate form. Specify who is responsible for decisions, escalation, appeals, liability and review, under the applicable legal and professional rules.

The choice is not between inevitable restoration and inevitable destruction. AI may deliver different effects across tasks and settings; it can add workload or reduce it, help identify needs or reinforce inequity. Compare the effects against existing methods, including no AI, and retain legitimate stopping. The corrected R4 AI report's strategic feasibility hypothesis and conditional governance proposals do not establish clinical performance or necessity of an AI Commons. All clinical and legal claims require human review. No pilot, procurement or data collection is authorised by this design discussion.
