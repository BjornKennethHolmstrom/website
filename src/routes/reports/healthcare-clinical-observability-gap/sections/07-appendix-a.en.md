## Appendix A: Value Systems and Policy Mindsets — A Guide for the Healthcare Context

### A Note on This Appendix

The original draws on Spiral Dynamics integral theory as an optional interpretive lens. Its attribution, empirical validity and scope require separate verification. The following four portraits are simplified hypotheses about institutional orientations, not validated developmental classifications of individuals, professions or societies. No orientation is intrinsically superior or entitled to govern. The framework's use is to make potential tensions and countervailing strengths discussable, not to diagnose motives or assign ranks.

### A.1 The Basic Insight

Institutions can emphasise order, efficiency, inclusion or integration in different contexts. A single organisation or person may combine these concerns. Each can support legitimate purposes or produce problems if applied without attention to the task, evidence, rights and other responsibilities. The hypothesis is that the interaction of norms, incentives and rules can affect how evidence is considered and decisions are corrected. Test actual arrangements and outcomes, rather than infer a mindset from a particular decision.

### A.2 The Value Systems in the Healthcare Arena

**Order and Stability (sometimes called “Blue”) — the Regulatory and Bureaucratic State.** The original associates this orientation with licensure, legal and regulatory frameworks, evidence-based protocols and public accountability. Candidate strengths are safety, procedural fairness, reduction of unwarranted variation and protection against incompetent or abusive practice. Candidate blind spots are rigid protocol adherence, documentation compliance as an end in itself and liability-related low-value work. A common standard may improve clinical observation and a professional boundary may protect patients; neither is a deficit merely because it limits discretion. Compare actual requirements, review, outcomes and justified exceptions.

**Achievement and Efficiency (sometimes called “Orange”) — the Managerial and Technological Healthcare.** The original associates this orientation with innovation, performance measurement, throughput and resource stewardship. Candidate strengths include improved surgical and diagnostic capabilities, therapies, organisation at scale and removal of waste. Candidate blind spots include proxy gaming, time treated only as a cost, and investment incentives that favour immediately measured activities over continuity. Efficiency can be a means of expanding access, and technology may support contextual care. Examine incentive rules, independent outcomes, cost, safety and distribution rather than infer a universal destructive mechanism.

**Inclusion and Care (sometimes called “Green”) — the Solidarity Principle and the Clinician's Ethic.** Collective funding, access, patient-centred care, shared decision-making and relationships are the original's examples. Candidate strengths include dignity, equity, patient agency and recognition of the human encounter. Candidate blind spots include reducing participation to a checkbox or interpreting solidarity as identical treatment regardless of need. Shared decision-making is not merely a signed form and patient-reported measures do not replace preferences. Common standards can promote equity, while adaptation may be essential. Test how the arrangements actually work, including access for underrepresented people and the legitimacy of refusal.

**Integrative and Systemic (sometimes called “Yellow”) — the Adaptive Governance Perspective.** The original associates this orientation with multi-scale care, institutional learning and the integration of expertise. Potential strengths are attention to interactions, uncertainty and complementary responsibilities. Potential blind spots include detachment, overly elaborate models, unrealistic coordination demands and unwarranted confidence in the reformer's own perspective. The Audit, Index and Sandbox are proposed from this lens but must face the same scrutiny as existing institutions. No label establishes superior knowledge or authority. A simpler existing mechanism may be more adequate than a complex design.

### A.3 The Clinical Observability Gap as a Value-System Configuration Problem

The original depicts regulation, efficiency and solidarity as potentially interacting in ways that privilege administrative measures over contextual care. It proposes an integrative translation layer to retain the strengths of each. This is an interpretive hypothesis, not an established developmental deficiency. Regulations may protect care, efficiency can improve access, solidarity can justify appropriate variation, and an integrative reform may add burdens or concentrate authority. Investigate interactions, decision rights and outcomes under H2–H5, including rival explanations and justified nonadoption. Patient agency and independent safety constraints apply to every orientation. No hierarchy of values or institutions follows from the mathematical witnesses.
