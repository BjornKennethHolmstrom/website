## Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

### How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek) from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. Several models were prompted in parallel to analyze Israel's governance architecture from different analytical angles, applying the systems-thinking framework he had set out. He chose the topic, posed the questions and decided to publish the result, but wrote little of the text himself.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about Israeli institutions, laws, events, figures and sources may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. Like any work of synthesis, it is provisional. It makes no claim to finality. It claims only that the lens it offers merits testing against reality—and that the testing, in the end, is what matters most.

### Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them write from within Israel's institutional core. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not Israeli, does not live in Israel and has no lived experience of Israeli governance. The framework applied here draws on complexity science, developmental psychology (Spiral Dynamics), governance theory, and control-theoretic approaches to institutional design.

That distance limits access to the granular, day-to-day texture of Israeli policy-making—the unwritten norms, the informal power structures, the lived reality that no formal framework can capture. An outside lens can sometimes raise questions that insiders have learned not to hear, but it can just as easily miss what insiders know. The report does not claim insider knowledge. It offers a lens that may or may not prove useful to those who hold institutional positions and are searching for frameworks that make sense of what they are experiencing.

The report touches on some of the most contested political questions anywhere, where even the framing is disputed. A framework developed elsewhere and applied by AI models may carry assumptions that readers from different communities would reject. Feedback, criticism, and corrections are welcome.

The report refers to related material on this website—the Global Governance Frameworks, the Governance as Engineering working papers and the Country Reports for Systemic Change—which were produced in the same way.

### The Country Reports Series

This report is the thirteenth in a series of Country Reports for Systemic Change, all produced in the same way. The first examined Germany through the lens of an execution deficit. The second examined France through the lens of an integration deficit. The third examined Sweden through the lens of a feedback deficit. The fourth examined India through the lens of a synchronisation deficit. The fifth examined the European Union through the lens of a coherence deficit. The sixth examined the United Kingdom through the lens of a control-delivery deficit. The seventh examined Brazil through the lens of an accumulation deficit. The eighth examined Russia through the lens of a power-vertical deficit. The ninth examined the United States through the lens of an integration deficit. The tenth examined Finland through the lens of a throughput constraint. The eleventh examined Japan through the lens of a continuity trap. The twelfth examined Nigeria through the lens of a substrate deficit.

Together, the thirteen reports propose a global diagnostic framework covering a wide range of adaptive capacity challenges—from first-order deficits of execution and integration to second-order constraints of velocity and paradigm lock-in, to the foundational challenge of substrate construction, and now to the challenge of boundary resolution that the report sees in Israel. The series does not claim to be complete. It is meant as a foundation on which further analysis, deeper testing, and better design can be built. The series frames Israel as a state that mastered survival but has not yet completed its own architecture, and uses it to pose a different question from the earlier reports: not how to govern better, but how to finish building the governance architecture that the founders began.
