## Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

### How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek) from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. Several models were prompted to analyse Dutch governance from different angles, applying the Governance as Engineering framework that he had set out. He chose the topic, posed the questions and decided to publish the result, but wrote little of the text himself.

The models were taken through the staged process developed for the South Korea and Bhutan reports, with its first step adapted to the case. Instead of an objective baseline, they began with a **constraint baseline**: mapping what binds Dutch development, how each constraint became binding and who enforces it. A constraint-interaction matrix followed, then a falsification matrix and two evidence passes. Several early hypotheses did not survive. The claim that the Netherlands lacks mechanisms for allocating scarcity was dropped when the models found explicit allocation systems for freshwater, grid capacity and nitrogen. An early reading that stalled projects are typically trapped by several constraints at once was weakened by project histories showing single and sequential bottlenecks—and then partly restored, at programme level, when a deliberate search for stalled rather than resolved projects corrected the sample's bias. The feasibility-debt hypothesis began with a single case and became the report's core only after independent cases were found in energy and aviation. A final comparison of institutional features across five domains was used to explain why water differs. The surviving claims were recorded in a claim-and-evidence ledger with wording ceilings. All of these steps, the evidence they cite and the conclusions drawn were produced by the models; whether the final text stays within the ledger has not been independently checked.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about Dutch institutions, laws, projects, figures and sources may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. Where the report says "evidence shows", that is the models' characterisation of external sources, not a verified finding; where it uses the language of the framework, it refers to synthesis within Governance as Engineering. The report registers predictions that could weaken or overturn its diagnosis. Like any work of synthesis, it is provisional.

### Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them write from within Dutch institutional life. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not Dutch, does not live in the Netherlands and has no lived experience of Dutch politics, its farming communities or its planning institutions. He has studied engineering physics and has worked with mathematical optimisation at TOMLAB Optimization; the report's central concept, recursive feasibility, comes from constrained optimisation and control theory. The framework applied here also draws on complexity science, developmental psychology and governance theory. Much of the texture of Dutch governance is inaccessible from outside, and Dutch readers will see what this report misses. Feedback, criticism and corrections are welcome.

The report refers to related material on this website—the Global Governance Frameworks, the Governance as Engineering working papers and the Country Reports for Systemic Change—which were produced in the same way.

### The Country Reports Series

This report is the nineteenth in the series of Country Reports for Systemic Change, all produced in the same way. The earlier reports examined Germany through the lens of an execution deficit, France through an integration deficit, Sweden through a feedback deficit, India through a synchronisation deficit, the European Union through a coherence deficit, the United Kingdom through a control-delivery deficit, Brazil through an accumulation deficit, Russia through a power-vertical deficit, the United States through an integration deficit, Finland through a throughput constraint, China through a calibration deficit, Japan through a continuity trap, Nigeria through a substrate deficit, Israel through a boundary deficit, Spain through a transition trap, Switzerland through a boundary-expansion deficit, South Korea through a tournament trap, and Bhutan through an interface race.

Together, the reports propose diagnoses spanning first-order deficits of execution and integration, second-order constraints of velocity and paradigm lock-in, the foundational challenge of substrate construction, constitutional incompleteness, the compression of objectives and the coupling of objectives to institutions. In the Netherlands the report sees a temporal failure mode: a system whose individual decisions may each be feasible while its sequence of commitments is not. The series frames the Netherlands as the makers of land at the limit of promise, and asks whether a country that taught the world to plan for an uncertain future can apply that wisdom to its own.
