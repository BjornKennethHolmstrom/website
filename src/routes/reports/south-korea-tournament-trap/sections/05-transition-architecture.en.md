## 5. Working with the Grain: Transition Architecture for South Korea

---

### 5.1 The Principle: Start Where Coupling Is Already Loosening

The original report cites regional variation in degree premiums, a local-talent hiring rule and Meister-school employment outcomes as possible examples of weaker coupling. These are leads requiring verification of effects, comparators and transferability. If verified, they could help identify candidate sites for voluntary, authorised pilots; they do not by themselves establish a proof of concept or confer legitimacy for expansion.

The Japan report proposed incremental refinement and consensus as a historical interpretation. Korean developmental programmes suggest a different sequence worth investigating: designate a priority, support a limited demonstration and evaluate results before considering expansion. The relevance of past programmes to present opportunity reforms, including implementation capacity, costs and transferability, remains to be tested. Neither national superiority nor rapid scaling is assumed.

The immune system described in Section 4 sets one condition on the early phase. Because the tournament is defended in the name of fairness, nothing in the first wave should change how any individual student is ranked. The first wave should change what rank buys—through supply, coupling and reversibility—in places and institutions that volunteer for it. Selection itself comes last.

---

### 5.2 Transparent Contextual Adaptation: Fairness Infrastructure

Earlier reports used the Trojan Horse metaphor for reforms expressed in familiar institutional language. This edition retains transparent contextual adaptation, not a concealed payload. The intended functions, responsibilities, affected groups and possible adverse consequences must be disclosed before authorization; low salience is not evidence of legitimacy.

In Korea the proposed common language is fairness itself. A procedural understanding of *gongjeong* may support auditable rules. Framing decoupling, reversibility and effective supply as extensions of fairness is a hypothesis about communication, not a mandate. The original report cites blind recruitment as a possible example of weakening pedigree signals; its design and outcomes require separate factual verification.

A decoupled hiring practice is *fair recruitment*: judging applicants on what the job requires rather than on a ranking made years earlier. A re-entry pathway after caregiving is *fairness across the life course*: the principle that one interruption should not cost a lifetime. A regional university that genuinely competes is *fairness of place*: the principle that where one was born should not decide which futures are reachable. A later-entry route into higher education is *second-chance fairness*. None of these asks Koreans to value fairness less. Each asks them to apply the value they already hold to more of the architecture than the exam.

The framing is not a disguise. It is a transparent translation. Participants must know the actual proposal; pilots require applicable legal authorization, consent, independent review, complaint and remedy channels, and the ability to stop or revise them. Demonstrated value may support a request for further authority but does not grant it.

---

### 5.3 Safe-to-Fail Pilots: Opportunity Regions

What follows is one illustrative architecture consistent with the diagnosis, not the only institutional design capable of meeting it. Its centrepiece is a small network of **Opportunity Regions**: designated metropolitan areas outside the capital where a coalition of provincial government, regional universities and anchor employers commits to building a complete alternative to the Seoul pathway, and to measuring whether it works.

The designation should build on what exists rather than replace it. RISE is already moving university support toward provincial governments; Glocal University 30 is already concentrating investment in selected non-capital institutions; relocated public institutions already operate local-talent hiring. An Opportunity Region would bind these instruments together around a single objective—raising effective prize supply—and add the elements they currently lack.

The proposed package has four parts. **Decoupled recruitment**: job-relevant selection and locally accessible recruitment, subject to applicable equality safeguards. **A complete life offer**: housing, transport, childcare, schools and amenities planned together. **Reversibility routes**: mid-career entry, recognised skills credentials and structured re-entry after caregiving. **Protected continuity**: the original proposal envisages at least ten years of statutory designation and multi-year funding. Any such commitment must permit lawful periodic review, amendment, termination and remedy; continuity is not immunity from accountability.

The candidates should be diverse by design. A research-intensive city built around its universities and laboratories, an industrial region anchored by large manufacturers in the southeast, and a smaller region with weaker starting conditions would together test whether the model works only where effective supply is already relatively high. A pilot that succeeds only where success was likely proves little.

Evaluation is what makes the pilots safe to fail, and it should measure effective supply directly rather than inputs or satisfaction. The questions are behavioural. Do capable students who could enrol in the capital choose the region's universities instead? Do the region's graduates stay? Do their wages, employer size and career progression converge with those of comparable capital-region graduates? Does private-tutoring expenditure among the region's families fall relative to similar regions? If the answers are no, the designation should be revised or ended, and the evidence published. If the answers are yes, the region becomes the most persuasive argument for structural change that Korea could produce.

A second, smaller pilot track concerns selection, and it follows the sequencing rule of Section 5.1. Before any change to how students are ranked, the fairness of alternatives should be tested directly: a national survey experiment comparing how Koreans judge rank ordering, holistic selection and competence-threshold-plus-lottery designs. Only if that evidence shows a credible legitimacy base should a university volunteer a small share of places in an oversubscribed programme for a threshold-based pilot, announced several years in advance and evaluated for both academic outcomes and preparatory behaviour.

### 5.4 Scaling by Attraction—and Task-Relative Opportunity Profiles

Korea's institutions compete intensely, and that competitive energy can be redirected rather than suppressed. Universities, regions and employers already watch each other closely and respond quickly to rankings of every kind. The transition can use that instinct.

Institutional opportunity measures could include retention and attraction of graduates, breadth of recruitment, outcomes relative to intake, and mid-career entry and re-entry. Publish contextual profiles and uncertainty rather than an unvalidated overall ranking. Corrected XX gives no guarantee that optimizing a proxy improves the target: predefine independent outcomes, test for gaming, selection, exclusion and reporting burdens, and suspend measures whose harms outweigh usefulness. Institutional metrics may also change the pressures students face.

Successful pilots might attract employers and applicants or motivate peers, but diffusion, displacement and selection effects need testing. Compare voluntary adoption with other legitimate arrangements. Expansion requires evidence of transferability, costs, rights and harms, and separately obtained authority and consent; a successful demonstration alone confers neither.

---

### 5.5 Sequencing: Lower the Stakes Before Changing the Selector

The original proposal sequences supply and decoupling before selector reform. Treat that order as a candidate, not a necessity: compare it with simultaneous reforms and procedural reform alone, and assess distributional impacts at each stage.

**First, investigate the architecture.** The proposed Tournament Audit would test where prize gradients are steep, effective supply is limited and coupling is consequential. It requires a mandate, resources and lawful data access, and may itself impose costs.

**Second, test supply expansion and decoupling.** Opportunity Regions, decoupled recruitment and re-entry routes are candidate interventions. Voluntary participation does not remove possible losses or selection bias; evaluate effects on participants and nonparticipants, including displaced costs.

**Third, flatten the gradients that rank does not justify.** Labour-market and pay-system reforms are the hardest politically and the slowest to show effects, but they are also the most powerful upstream lever. By this phase they can be argued not only on productivity and fairness grounds but on the measured evidence of what flatter gradients do to competition a decade earlier.

**Fourth, consider selection reform.** Lower stakes might make richer or less rank-resolving selection easier to accept. Test that proposition rather than assume it, and give affected cohorts adequate notice and access to remedies.

The phases overlap; none needs to wait for the previous one to finish. But the direction of travel should be clear. The historical instinct of Korean reform has been to begin with the selector, because it is the lever within reach. The transition architecture reverses that order.

---

### 5.6 The Role of the Centre: Commit Beyond the Term

The centre's most important contribution is not a programme but a commitment that survives changes of government. Structural reform of the tournament works on the timescale of cohorts: an Opportunity Region designated today will show its clearest results in the careers of students who are now in primary school. Korea's political system changes presidents every five years and has shown, as recently as 2025, that it can change them faster still. A transition that depends on one administration's enthusiasm will be abandoned by the next, and the tournament's immune system knows it.

The centre could consider four functions. **Legislate a reviewable frame**: lawful statutory authority and transparent funding with periodic review. **Separate evaluation from implementation**: protect independent publication while maintaining accountability. **Enable voluntary, authorized experimentation**: provide resources and flexibility subject to rights and safeguards. **Consider expansion only after evaluation**: test transferability and obtain the additional mandate and consent required; preserve the ability to amend or stop.

A demonstrated regional model could support a case for wider adoption, but only if independent evaluation establishes benefits relative to alternatives, transferability, feasible resources and acceptable harms. Expansion requires additional lawful authority and consent where applicable, with review, remedy and the possibility of amendment or termination. No national speed advantage or ten-year scaling guarantee is inferred from historical examples.
