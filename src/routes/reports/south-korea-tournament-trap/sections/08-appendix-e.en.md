## Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

### How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek) from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. Several models were prompted to analyse Korea's governance architecture from different angles, applying the Governance as Engineering framework that he had set out. He chose the topic, posed the questions and decided to publish the result, but wrote little of the text himself.

The models were taken through a staged process. The diagnosis began as a hypothesis and was tested against an explicit falsification matrix before drafting. Three evidence passes revised it substantially: an early version centred on fertility was demoted when the models identified career–family incompatibility as the primary mechanism; a version claiming that Korea is more extreme than its neighbours on every gradient was retired when Taiwanese and Japanese comparisons contradicted it; and a claim that suppressed competition is conserved was withdrawn as unsupported. The surviving claims were recorded in a claim-and-evidence ledger, which assigned each one a status—*established*, *supported*, *provisional* or *internal to the series*—and a ceiling on the wording the report could use. After the report was rewritten into the narrative form used across the country-report series, a second claim audit identified factual material and stronger interpretations introduced during rewriting. Claims added at that stage were sourced separately or weakened; the resulting ledger (v1.1) supersedes the pre-draft version. All of these steps, the evidence they cite and the conclusions drawn were produced by the models; whether the final text stays within the ledger has not been independently checked.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about Korean institutions, laws, policies, events, figures and sources may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. Where the report says "evidence shows", that is the models' characterisation of external sources, not a verified finding; where it says "the report interprets", it refers to synthesis within the Governance as Engineering framework; and where it cites Paper VIII, it refers to an internal pilot estimate. The report registers predictions that could weaken or overturn its diagnosis, and it proposes an institution designed to test them. Like any work of synthesis, it is provisional. It claims only that its lens merits testing against reality.

### Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them write from within Korea's institutional life. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not Korean, does not live in Korea and has no lived experience of Korean society. He has studied engineering physics and has worked with mathematical optimisation at TOMLAB Optimization. The framework applied here draws on complexity science, developmental psychology, governance theory and control-theoretic approaches to institutional design.

An outside lens can sometimes raise questions that insiders have learned not to hear, but it can just as easily miss what insiders know. Much of the texture of Korean family life, school culture and policy-making is inaccessible from outside, and Korean readers will see what this report misses. Feedback, criticism and corrections are welcome.

The report refers to related material on this website—the Global Governance Frameworks, the Governance as Engineering working papers and the Country Reports for Systemic Change—which were produced in the same way.

### The Country Reports Series

This report is the seventeenth in the series of Country Reports for Systemic Change, all produced in the same way. The earlier reports examined Germany through the lens of an execution deficit, France through an integration deficit, Sweden through a feedback deficit, India through a synchronisation deficit, the European Union through a coherence deficit, the United Kingdom through a control-delivery deficit, Brazil through an accumulation deficit, Russia through a power-vertical deficit, the United States through an integration deficit, Finland through a throughput constraint, China through a calibration deficit, Japan through a continuity trap, Nigeria through a substrate deficit, Israel through a boundary deficit, Spain through a transition trap, and Switzerland through a boundary-expansion deficit.

Together, the reports propose a diagnostic framework spanning first-order deficits of execution and integration, second-order constraints of velocity and paradigm lock-in, the foundational challenge of substrate construction and the challenge of constitutional incompleteness. In South Korea the report sees a further category: a system that can correct its controllers with remarkable speed while the opportunity architecture beneath them changes slowly. The series does not claim to be complete. It frames South Korea as the champion of the tournament at the frontier of plurality, and asks how a society that learned to compete fairly for a future can learn to offer its people more than one.
