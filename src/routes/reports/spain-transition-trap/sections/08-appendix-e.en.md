## Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

### How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek) from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. Several models were prompted in parallel to analyze Spain's governance architecture from different analytical angles, applying the systems-thinking framework he had set out. He chose the topic, posed the questions and decided to publish the result, but wrote little of the text himself.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about Spanish institutions, laws, events, figures and sources may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. Like any work of synthesis, it is provisional. It makes no claim to finality. It claims only that the lens it offers merits testing against reality—and that the testing, in the end, is what matters most.

### Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them write from within Spain's institutional core. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not Spanish, does not live in Spain and has no lived experience of Spanish governance. The framework applied here draws on complexity science, developmental psychology (Spiral Dynamics), governance theory, and control‑theoretic approaches to institutional design.

That distance limits access to the granular, day‑to‑day texture of Spanish policy‑making—the unwritten norms, the informal power structures, the lived reality that no formal framework can capture. An outside lens can sometimes raise questions that insiders have learned not to hear, but it can just as easily miss what insiders know. The report does not claim insider knowledge. It offers a lens that may or may not prove useful to those who hold institutional positions and are searching for frameworks that make sense of what they are experiencing.

The report touches on deeply contested questions of territorial organisation and national identity, where even the framing is disputed. A framework developed outside Spain and applied by AI models may carry assumptions that readers from different communities would reject. Feedback, criticism, and corrections are welcome.

The report refers to related material on this website—the Global Governance Frameworks, the Governance as Engineering working papers and the Country Reports for Systemic Change—which were produced in the same way.

### The Country Reports Series

This report is the fourteenth in a series of Country Reports for Systemic Change, all produced in the same way. The first examined Germany through the lens of an execution deficit. The second examined France through the lens of an integration deficit. The third examined Sweden through the lens of a feedback deficit. The fourth examined India through the lens of a synchronisation deficit. The fifth examined the European Union through the lens of a coherence deficit. The sixth examined the United Kingdom through the lens of a control‑delivery deficit. The seventh examined Brazil through the lens of an accumulation deficit. The eighth examined Russia through the lens of a power‑vertical deficit. The ninth examined the United States through the lens of an integration deficit. The tenth examined Finland through the lens of a throughput constraint. The eleventh examined Japan through the lens of a continuity trap. The twelfth examined Nigeria through the lens of a substrate deficit. The thirteenth examined Israel through the lens of a boundary deficit.

Together, the fourteen reports propose a global diagnostic framework covering a wide range of adaptive capacity challenges—from first‑order deficits of execution and integration to second‑order constraints of velocity and paradigm lock‑in, to the foundational challenge of substrate construction, to the challenge of constitutional incompleteness that, in the series' view, Israel and Spain represent in different forms. The series does not claim to be complete. It is meant as a foundation on which further analysis, deeper testing, and better design can be built. The series frames Spain as a democracy that mastered transition but has not yet completed its own constitution, and uses it to pose a more hopeful question than the earlier reports: not how to prevent collapse, but how to finish what was brilliantly begun.
