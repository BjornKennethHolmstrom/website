## 3. What Building Requisite Standard-Setting Architecture Would Look Like

The following is an architecture to compare with existing and narrower alternatives, not a specification derived from a theorem. If H1–H5 fail, the case for some or all changes may fail. The design seeks to preserve financial rigor while investigating whether other evidence can improve specific decisions. Four advocated design values guide the comparison: multi-scale observation (local, sectoral and wider effects where relevant); authority matched to the scope of decisions; discrimination between legitimate institutional protection and procedural obstruction; and designed evolvability through review, experimentation and correction. These are preferences to test, not properties established by the formal models.

### 3.1 The Principle: Multi-Capital Observation, Integrated Reporting, and Expanded Constitutional Objective

Three interdependent but independently testable components are proposed: a broader stewardship objective, integrated presentation of relevant evidence, and mechanisms for measurement, distributed observation, temporal coverage and exchange. A broader objective without suitable information or action authority may achieve little; an integrated report without credible assurance may mislead. A separate report or independent channel may work better than integration. Test combinations and compare incremental effects against a common baseline.

**Cross-cutting authorization constraint (applies to all §§3.2–3.10 and Chapter 5).** The architecture itself risks reproducing §2.14's critique: a category or map that makes information visible to an investor may deprive a community of control over its meaning. Neither omission nor forced inclusion is presumptively beneficial. For Indigenous lands, knowledge or data, the proposed design requires collective free, prior and informed consent (FPIC) through the peoples' own legitimate institutions; stakeholder consultation and individual privacy consent are separate. Indigenous peoples and other local communities are not interchangeable; appropriate community authorization for the latter must be established. Representation and internal disagreements can be contested; the report does not adjudicate them. Communities may refuse measurement, categorization, valuation, disclosure and mapping without penalty in the research or pilot. No capital classification or monetisation of sacred, relational or cultural values without consent. Community control extends to categories, valuations, mappings, access, retention, downstream reuse and withdrawal. Negotiate benefit-sharing and independent remedies; prevent extraction, including indirect collection and inference. Corporate participation, statutory entity access or a public API do not establish collective permission. Where lawful obligations or competing rights exist, examine them separately; this is a proposed safeguard, not a universal legal exemption. If consent or safe evidence is unavailable, redesign, abstain or stop. Do not demand disclosure of protected knowledge to establish harm. UNDRIP, ILO Convention 169, CARE Principles and community-specific protocols are references for verification, not asserted as equivalent or legally binding everywhere. These safeguards have not been implemented or endorsed by affected communities.

### 3.2 Redefining the Objective: From Investor Primacy to Multi-Capital Stewardship

The original report proposed the following replacement objective, quoted verbatim as an advocacy proposal rather than an established requirement:

> *to provide information useful for the stewardship of all forms of capital—financial, manufactured, intellectual, human, social, relationship, and natural—that affect the entity's long-term viability and its impact on human and ecological wellbeing.*

This is an openly advocated candidate for deliberation, not a prerequisite for every useful disclosure. A more careful formulation would provide evidence relevant to authorized stewardship decisions, including impacts and longer horizons, without requiring every relation to be classified as a capital. The six-capital vocabulary may be useful to some organizations and inappropriate to others; §3.1 governs adoption and valuation.

Four design rationales motivated this formulation and should be assessed separately. First, stewardship offers a framing across investors, employees, regulators, communities and civil society, whose tasks differ. Second, naming forms of capital could counter drift back toward exclusive financial priorities, but might itself impose inappropriate categories; compare explicit non-capital alternatives and apply §3.1. Third, long-term viability makes the time horizon explicit: test whether longer-horizon evidence adds value to existing financial information, rather than assume the current framework excludes it. Fourth, impact on human and ecological wellbeing is proposed as a dimension of performance in its own right, not only insofar as it affects financial returns. Whether these changes improve specified decisions is open.

A multi-stakeholder framing recognizes that investors, employees, regulators and communities have different tasks and interests. It does not confer shared authority over each other's knowledge. Investors may benefit from longer-horizon information, while impact information may matter independently of financial returns. Present both purposes explicitly rather than justify one by concealing the other. Compare broadening the financial reporting objective with preserving it and establishing complementary reporting obligations through appropriate authorities.

Periodic independent assessment of the reporting objective could inform an amendment process. The IASB, FASB and jurisdictions have separate powers; a proposed review cannot amend a Framework by itself. Evidence could justify confirmation of the current objective, a narrower change, or a broader one. Chapter 5 specifies a candidate procedure.

### 3.3 Integrated Reporting as the Core Architecture, Not a Supplement

The International Integrated Reporting Framework identifies six capitals and provides a potential structure for relating strategy, operations and performance across them. The original describes its development by the IIRC and subsequent location within the IFRS Foundation; status, adoption and evidence of effects require verification. Financial statements can remain a rigorous account of financial position while other evidence explains material dependencies, impacts and uncertainties.

The rationale for inverting the hierarchy is that what occupies the primary reporting channel may shape what preparers and users attend to. Test this hypothesis against linked but separate reports; the inversion alone establishes no improvement. Assurance should match each component and its task, including independent financial audit where required, physical or qualitative evidence and disclosed uncertainty. Uncertainty is a parameter for designing assurance, not by itself a reason to exclude evidence.

The original proposal inverts the presentation hierarchy: an integrated report would provide a common overview and financial statements would be a detailed component. This may help users see relationships between decisions and effects, but may also complicate assurance, obscure distinct reporting purposes or duplicate information. Compare the inversion with linked but distinct disclosures, including current arrangements. The EU CSRD/ESRS experience is a relevant lead, not an existence proof of the proposed architecture. Do not conflate EU legal reporting obligations with IASB authority.

Integration is not a license to merge all knowledge into a single report. Where a community chooses independent records, a link or a scoped interface may be appropriate only with collective authorization; some knowledge may remain unrepresented. The choice of presentation is independent of the right to collect and use the evidence (§3.1).

### 3.4 Double Materiality as a Candidate Reporting Design

Double materiality distinguishes effects on the entity's financial prospects from the entity's effects on people and environment. A company's impact on water quality could matter to residents and public authorities even when it has no immediate financial effect. Some impacts can later become financial risks, but their significance need not be reduced to that possibility. These are distinct purposes, not two independent mathematical dimensions that double a channel's capacity.

The EU's double-materiality framework and the ISSB's financially oriented sustainability disclosures offer historical leads for comparison; actual scope, dates, revisions and implementation require verification. Divergence can increase compliance burdens or reflect legitimate different purposes. Compare decision accuracy, impact evidence, burden and interoperability. The report advocates considering both perspectives, but does not establish that every entity must use a single combined standard. Impact reporting must not convert affected communities into compulsory sources of data: apply §3.1, including refusal and authorization.

### 3.5 Natural Capital Accounting: From Voluntary Experiment to Possible Standard

The UN System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA) is a statistical framework that the original presents as a source of methods for ecosystem services, resource stocks and environmental degradation. Verify the status of SEEA Ecosystem Accounting, its intended scale and the validity of transfer to individual entities. A national or landscape-level account does not establish a corporate asset or the entity's authority over an ecosystem.

The original advocates moving beyond purely voluntary experiments towards standardized natural-capital accounting where justified. A pilot could compare physical measures (water use, land condition, emissions), qualitative and community-defined measures, ranges and monetary valuation where appropriate. Attribution of a shared ecosystem's benefits or losses to one entity is itself a methodological problem. Distinguish stocks from flows, ownership from dependence, and monetary estimates from rights or obligations. No monetary value should imply a right to purchase or destroy the underlying relationship.

Mandatory SEEA valuation is not a default recommendation. Affected peoples may refuse a classification or valuation; sacred and relational values are not to be converted into capital without consent. Authorized alternatives can leave categories unmapped or rely on independent community-controlled accounts. Only a competent authority could adopt a specific reporting rule after evaluating reliability, costs, rights and alternatives; §3.1 applies throughout.

### 3.6 Measurement Innovation Without the Measurement Trap

Graduated measurement is proposed to match evidence and assurance to the task, not to lower standards of rigor. Financial transactions can retain established audit procedures. Less directly verifiable phenomena may be reported through physical observations, intervals, qualitative evidence or explicitly uncertain estimates. In the original analogy, fair-value estimates for illiquid assets disclose methods, assumptions and sensitivities; verify actual requirements before transferring them to other domains.

A useful measurement record specifies what was measured, by whom, when, with what provenance, model and uncertainty, and how it may be used. A decision-maker should be able to distinguish an independently observed quantity from a model estimate or an assertion. Test whether the uncertain evidence improves decisions over the baseline; poorly supported metrics can be worse than none. Separate measurement, recognition, disclosure and assurance. An estimate is neither proof of ownership nor a warrant to monetize knowledge. No imputation of zero for missing or refused evidence; authorization and community-defined measures govern relevant cases under §3.1.

### 3.7 Distributed Sensing for Supply Chains and Ecosystems

An entity may depend on suppliers, platforms, watersheds, workers and infrastructure outside its legal reporting perimeter. Environmental or human-rights impacts can occur in supply chains, but their relative importance varies by entity and sector. The reporting entity is a node in a network; evidence of dependencies and impacts may complement rather than redefine its consolidated accounts.

A candidate distributed-sensing arrangement would allocate responsibility for particular material dependencies, using existing due-diligence records and independent sources where sufficient. The original cites UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights and supply-chain reporting as leads for legal and empirical verification. Map the evidence pathway, identify which actor has the authority and capacity to collect and respond, and compare targeted reporting with centralized collection. Confidentiality, burden, duplication, security and false attribution are evaluation criteria.

The entity has no presumed right to sense lands or communities it does not control. §3.1 requires collective authorization for relevant Indigenous knowledge, land and data, separate from supplier or corporate consent, and appropriate authority for other communities. No indirect inference to bypass protected access, no forced disclosure for evaluation, and benefit-sharing and remedies where authorized collection occurs. A community-controlled channel or no additional sensing may be the appropriate option.

### 3.8 Temporal Expansion: Forward-Looking and Long-Horizon Disclosure

Historical statements provide a stable basis for assessment, while forecasts and scenario analysis may help users understand long-horizon risks. Verify the precise going-concern assessment requirements, including the applicable twelve-month reference period, and existing forward-looking disclosures; they are not assumed to exclude all longer-term information. Different risks have different relevant horizons.

The original proposes extending disclosure of transition plans, ecosystem dependencies, technological and demographic changes over periods commensurate with the risk. TCFD and CSRD transition-plan practices are leads for comparison. A scenario report could specify assumptions, alternative pathways, uncertainty, sensitivity and the response decisions available to each user. A long-horizon scenario is not an assurance of viability. Evaluate forecast calibration, usefulness and costs, and avoid implying that an uncertain projection is a recognized liability. Community data and scenario assumptions about affected lands remain subject to §3.1; protected evidence need not be disclosed to support a public forecast.

### 3.9 From PDF to API: The Medium Upgrade

Machine-readable exchange could complement the annual report rather than replace its stable, auditable record. XBRL provides an example of a structured format; verify which jurisdictions require filings and what their taxonomies cover. APIs may allow authorized users to retrieve current, versioned data, reducing manual transcription and enabling comparisons where semantics are equivalent. The appropriate update interval depends on the task: continuously updating data is not inherently superior to a reconciled periodic record.

An open specification could allow independent third parties to build analysis and assurance tools against authorized shared data, rather than rely on one vendor or reporting entity. Test whether event-driven updates for significant changes alongside periodic reconciled reports improve useful timeliness without imposing excessive assurance costs, false alarms or security exposure. Open specifications need not imply open access to protected records.

A pilot interface should specify data definitions, provenance, timestamps, versions, units, error handling, access roles, security and audit trails. Compare latency, decision performance, costs, assurance and risk with existing filings. Do not assume the standard-setter can impose an API or that a public interface authorizes reuse. Multiple ontologies may be supported through partial mappings rather than a single global list of capitals. Preserve authorized restrictions, unmapped categories, uncertainty and refusal; §3.1 governs collection and downstream use. An API must not become a means of circumventing community authority.

### 3.10 Networked Translation Instead of Single Global Standard

Networked translation is an alternative to requiring every jurisdiction or reporting tradition to use identical categories. A shared interface can define limited semantics for a particular purpose, while source frameworks retain distinctions needed locally. The original's internet-routing and open-source examples are analogues for coordination, not existence proofs for legitimate or effective accounting interoperability. The Stage Yellow framing expresses an interpretive preference for adaptive integration (Appendix A), not a design warrant.

A concrete candidate has two layers. A shared layer specifies limited disclosure purposes, relevant horizons and minimum assurance for authorized tasks, rather than a compulsory list of capitals. Recognised measurement frameworks provide the methods: the original names SEEA, GRI Standards, the Natural Capital Protocol and regionally developed approaches as leads whose scope and suitability must be verified. For each applicable disclosure an entity would state which framework and version it uses, why it suits its context, and how its authorized disclosures correspond to the shared interface; if no appropriate mapping exists, it may retain a separate record or decline the proposed mapping.

The translation layer would use open, machine-readable, versioned mapping protocols, independently assured where appropriate. For example, a GHG Protocol emissions record might map selected quantities into a common emissions field, with scope, units, boundaries, estimation methods and any lost distinctions recorded. A SEEA-derived ecosystem account might map authorized physical measures into a shared environmental interface, without equating national ecosystem accounts with corporate assets or requiring monetary valuation. Both examples are illustrative leads, not verified implementations. The source records and restrictions would remain intact; corrections and revisions would carry provenance. The protocol must not translate protected knowledge without collective authorization.

A map from source categories a and b to a common category c can discard their distinction if f(a)=f(b)=c. Whether this matters depends on the task (compare the conditional K ⊆ L criterion in Appendix C); multiple frameworks do not necessarily force non-injectivity, nor is a common taxonomy necessarily lossless. An independent authorized channel might retain the distinction. Even an injective mapping does not confer permission to use the information. Withdraw the former promise of “integration without compression” and the claim that translation resolves the power settlement problem by itself.

The monoculture-resilience hypothesis is that if a framework misses a relevant risk, alternative frameworks might retain that distinction, limiting synchronization to a shared blind spot. Compare error correlations, recovery from discovered omissions and independent decision outcomes with common-standard baselines. Correlated errors across frameworks, failure to contain omissions or comparability and assurance costs exceeding the benefits would weaken this case. The original biodiversity analogy is illustrative, not evidence of accounting resilience.

The boundary shell game and frontier dynamic might be addressed less completely: different observation boundaries could make some forms of migration to unobserved activity easier to detect, or might instead create new opportunities for arbitrage between frameworks. Test changes in the location of activities, actual exposure and residual omissions rather than infer a smaller periphery from the number of frameworks. Neither hypothesis entails more sensing of protected knowledge; an authorized narrower interface or no mapping remains a legitimate outcome.

Proposed interfaces should be optional and scoped, with versioned provenance, definitions, authority, mapping losses, collisions, uncertainty, unmapped states and permitted uses documented. Users and affected communities should be able to review, correct or decline mappings. Refusals are not zeros or observational defects; do not publish protected mappings or infer their contents through other data. §3.1 applies, including control over retention and downstream reuse. Compare plural interfaces, common standards and no mapping for specific tasks, including the costs of inconsistency and the benefits of shared definitions. Interoperability is a candidate design, not a claim of universal representability.
