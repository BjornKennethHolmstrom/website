## 4. The Political Immune System: Investor Primacy and the Pretence of Objectivity

The political immune system is a metaphor for possible feedback between interests, institutional procedures and narratives. It is not proof of capture, or a license to bypass democratic or professional scrutiny.

### 4.1 Investor Primacy Defined

The original uses investor primacy to describe a financial reporting purpose oriented towards investors, lenders and creditors. That reporting objective differs from corporate fiduciary duties, national policy goals and the mandate of other authorities. The public-interest argument is that reliable information enables efficient allocation of capital, which can support economic activity. This is a coherent rationale for a specialized reporting function.

The question is whether particular costs and impacts are missed by the decisions relying on that function and whether wider purposes should be served by the same or different institutions. The argument that all stakeholders benefit from efficient capital allocation is an empirical and normative claim, not a definition. Compare the benefits of specialization, additional impact reporting and alternative mandates. Professional rigor is a resource, not a problem to eliminate.

The original account of investor primacy also contains an important sequence of claims worth separating. Providers of finance support productive activity; comparable information may improve capital allocation; economic activity may contribute to employment, tax revenues and resources for public purposes. Even if these links hold in a given setting, the distribution of gains and losses and the handling of external effects require separate assessment. A capital allocation decision can be privately beneficial while imposing costs on someone not represented in that decision. This is a possibility to examine, not a finding that the accounting objective causes those costs.

The public-interest argument for specialization is strongest where other institutions actually have the evidence, authority and means to address impacts. Its limits may become important where those institutions depend on the same data and have no substitute. A court can take evidence from sources other than financial statements, a supervisor can commission a risk assessment, and a community may maintain its own records. H3 investigates the particular connections instead of presuming the entire governance ecosystem depends on one channel. The competing hypothesis is that a division of informational labour protects both financial rigor and legitimate plural purposes.

### 4.2 Who Benefits—Named Honestly

The original names five groups whose interests could be relevant to stability or reform. These are hypotheses to investigate, not allegations about individual motives.

**Investors and creditors.** Established valuation models depend on comparability, predictability and reliable financial data. Transition costs may be real; some long-horizon investors may favor new evidence. Test actual decision effects and expressed reasons.

**The Big Four firms (Deloitte, PwC, EY and KPMG).** Existing audit expertise and liability arrangements may favor familiar evidence, but new assurance services may create opportunities. Verify concentration, revenues, competence and actual positions rather than assume a monopoly or coordinated resistance.

**Corporate management.** Compensation and strategic discretion can be affected by new disclosure obligations. A manager might oppose a burdensome or unreliable measure, support evidence that reduces uncertainty, or resist disclosure that exposes a real harm. Distinguish these explanations with evidence.

**Governments.** Borrowing costs, public accounting, regulatory capacity and development priorities may affect choices about standards. Sovereign and corporate accounting differ; do not infer hidden sovereign liabilities from corporate IFRS rules.

**Financial services.** Asset managers, ratings agencies and data vendors invest in existing analytical systems. New standards can impose switching costs or enable products and better risk models. Examine who bears costs and receives benefits, including those outside financial markets. A coalition favoring continuity can arise without conspiracy; a legitimate objection can improve the proposal.

The mechanisms within these five groups are different. An investor's switching costs are not the same as an auditor's liability exposure or a preparer's cost of collecting information. For audit firms, assurance of a cash balance and assurance of a biodiversity estimate may involve different expertise, attribution methods and uncertainty. For preparers, disclosure may change the scope for shareholder questions, compensation measures and regulatory scrutiny; it may also expose commercially sensitive information or consume resources without changing decisions. Those effects should be compared for concrete proposals rather than inferred from group membership.

In public finance, infrastructure adaptation, environmental health costs and future fiscal exposure are potential information needs. They should be investigated using the relevant government accounting and fiscal-risk arrangements, rather than treating future expenditure as a recognized liability by definition. In financial intermediation, ratings models, portfolio rules and contracts referencing accounting metrics may be affected by a change to a standard; document transition effects and whether revised measures actually improve decisions. The distribution of transition costs, and whether they can be reduced without weakening accountability, is an important political question.

The immune-system metaphor is useful only if a pattern of stabilization can be observed rather than inferred from the existence of interests. A coalition that rejects a particular design may protect a shared rent, or it may identify a serious measurement, authorization or implementation defect. A comparison of positions, reasons, influence and actual outcomes is needed to distinguish those accounts.

### 4.3 The Narrative Strategy

Three narrative arguments from the original can be retained as transparent advocacy.

First, serving investors may require evidence of risks not adequately captured by their existing information. Test this rather than claim broader reporting necessarily serves investors better. Be explicit that some proposed impact disclosures also serve non-investor purposes.

Second, the Conceptual Framework is a revisable institutional design, not a sacred text. Respect its mandate and due process while asking whether changes in economic activity warrant reconsideration. The historical context and effects require verification; no constitutional change should be smuggled in as technical research.

Third, transparency about values need not threaten professional integrity. Clarifying who a report is for and what it does not measure can reinforce trust in technical work. Invite scrutiny of both the original architecture and proposed alternatives, including §2.14's critique of imposed categories. Do not use rhetorical framing to conceal intent or preempt legitimate rejection.

These arguments are not a communications strategy for making an otherwise unacceptable proposal palatable. They identify propositions that could be investigated openly. If long-horizon investors already obtain relevant information from other sources, the first argument may support maintaining the current reporting boundary. If the underlying mandate remains adequate, the second may favor ordinary standards revision rather than constitutional amendment. If disclosure of uncertainties produces confusion or misinterpretation, the third may favor more targeted methods of communicating evidence. The report should be willing to learn from all three possibilities. Professional pride in reliable standards is a genuine resource for this inquiry.

### 4.4 Standards as Power Settlements

Accounting conventions reflect historical settlements among institutions, jurisdictions and professions as well as technical reasoning. The original links IFRS development to the post-war Anglophone financial system and international capital-market access. Treat this as a historical hypothesis requiring evidence on agenda-setting, representation, adoption and alternatives. Verify any World Bank or IMF lending conditionality in specific programs and dates; do not assume it is universal or established here.

Adoption can reduce transaction costs or constrain local options; reporting changes can redistribute burden, authority and information. A technical reform is therefore also open to political and ethical assessment. Networked translation may offer some local flexibility, but a shared interface can itself impose dominant categories. The architecture in §3.10 must satisfy mapping-loss and authority checks, including collective authorization, refusal and benefit-sharing under §3.1. Technical interoperability does not settle the underlying distribution of power.

The historical power-settlement question also includes the position of jurisdictions with different accounting traditions, administrative capacity and development priorities. An internationally comparable standard may reduce financing and translation burdens for these jurisdictions. Conversely, a rule selected elsewhere can constrain their choices or require costly changes to professional and administrative infrastructure. Trace actual decisions and alternatives instead of equating adoption with coercion. The legitimacy of a common interface depends in part on who can define or contest the mappings, and whether participating institutions can preserve locally relevant distinctions. The risk that a translation layer merely recreates the dominance of a common standard is itself a test of the proposal.

### 4.5 The Dual-Reality Risk

The dual-reality risk concerns inconsistent or poorly connected financial and sustainability representations. A company might disclose an exposure in a sustainability report that appears inconsistent with assumptions in its financial statements. The inconsistency may be substantive or reflect different scopes, time horizons and materiality rules. Investigate reconciliation, independent evidence, assurance and decisions before calling it a contradiction.

Separate reporting can enable different purposes and professional competencies; integration could help readers see connections or could conceal important differences. The ISSB and EU frameworks are cases to compare, not a diagnosis of current universal fragmentation. Where users have enough reliable evidence and understand the relationship between reports, a complementary arrangement may be adequate. Test whether integration, cross-references or narrower targeted changes improve decision and impact outcomes. Legitimate non-integration is an option.

A practical diagnostic would choose particular estimates or claims common to the two reporting streams, specify the boundary and horizon of each, and ask whether any differences can be reconciled. Where the disagreement concerns a material assumption rather than the choice of scope, trace which users rely on it and whether their decisions change. If there is no decision-relevant inconsistency, the separation of streams is not a defect merely because it is visible. A growing ecosystem of services around either stream may increase the cost of integration; it may also enable useful specialization. These are competing predictions, not a conclusion that the risk necessarily increases over time.
