## 6. Coda: The Cost of Invisible Capital

### 6.1 The Wealth That Matters

Standard-setting bodies have built a remarkable infrastructure for common financial records and technical coordination. The authority of the IASB, FASB and ISO takes different forms and depends on adoption and enforcement; the original's jurisdiction counts and comparative claims require verification. What deserves preservation is the achievement of rigor, comparability and institutional continuity.

The wealth that matters is not exhausted by a financial account. Ecosystem conditions, knowledge, relationships and human capacities can be important in their own right and for the future of an enterprise. Not every such value is a capital or belongs in a common ledger. When relevant evidence is missing from a decision pathway, the cost may be substantial; the fact that a physical or social harm is unreported does not establish that reporting caused it or would prevent it.

Standard-setting helps curate one strand of civilization's economic memory. The courage to expand that memory, where it serves legitimate purposes, must be accompanied by the humility to recognize other memories, other authorities and the right not to translate. The future need not depend on making every relationship legible to a reporting entity.

### 6.2 The Shift

The shift advocated here is from assuming that existing reporting purposes are sufficient for every task to examining where different evidence and authority could improve particular outcomes. It could involve a broader stewardship objective, integrated reporting, double materiality, graduated measurement, distributed sensing and networked translation. It might instead involve a targeted disclosure, an independent evidence channel or preservation of the current division of labour. The choice is not established by a mathematical bound.

Investor primacy is a choice of reporting purpose, not a technical necessity established by the models; it may nevertheless be justified for particular tasks. False precision can mislead more than openly disclosed uncertainty, a risk to test rather than a reason to abandon rigorous assurance.

The cultural question is how to retain professional rigor while making the purposes and limitations of measurement explicit. A legitimate disagreement about a proposed measure deserves a reasoned hearing. An affected community's refusal deserves respect, not a more ingenious extraction mechanism. The architecture must not reproduce its own critique of ontological colonization (§§2.14 and 3.1).

Integrated reporting, SEEA, EU disclosure rules and machine-readable reporting offer concrete precedents to investigate. They do not certify that the combined proposal is feasible, desirable or lawful. Their differing purposes are part of what must be understood.

The original report found inspiration in the fragments already present in practice: the Integrated Reporting Framework's effort to relate different kinds of evidence; GRI and sustainability-reporting initiatives; environmental-economic statistics; climate and nature disclosure initiatives; and the XBRL infrastructure. Their relationships and effects remain verification leads. The presence of multiple approaches creates an opportunity for comparison, not an obligation to connect every fragment into one system. The possibility of an authorized, limited interface between different approaches is more modest than the promise of a universal accounting language, but may be more respectful of the variety of knowledge involved.

### 6.3 The Global Significance

The protocol-layer metaphor points to a possible source of leverage: a change to a reporting rule might change the evidence on which several institutions rely. Central banks, courts, investors and supervisors have different mandates and can use different sources. The importance of a common rule therefore depends on the particular dependency, available substitutes and response authority. There is no general ranking of reform leverage or proof that downstream reforms cannot compensate.

Debates about ISSB, double materiality and ecological accounts provide historical leads. A carefully chosen comparison might reveal a shared weakness, or demonstrate that distinct sources are sufficient. The question is whether deliberate evaluation can improve responses before avoidable harms occur, rather than merely responding after them.

The countervailing possibility is that a change to common reporting rules is less useful than action at the site of a particular decision. A bank's exposure to a water-dependent borrower might be understood with independent data and a supervisory rule even if corporate financial recognition does not change. A court may have access to evidence of damage that no accounting standard needs to encode. A worker may need a functioning inspection and remedy mechanism rather than another indicator in an investor report. Reform at the protocol layer and reform downstream are options to compare, not mutually exclusive necessities. The question of leverage is ultimately about what changes outcomes.

### 6.4 The Honest Conclusion

The Audit, Sandbox and Review Procedure are a wager on the possibility of learning from independent evidence and transparent, authorized experimentation. They may fail, produce limited benefits, expose new harms or justify keeping present arrangements. A successful complement need not become a replacement. No outcome is guaranteed and no institution is obliged to adopt a proposal merely because it has been tested.

Climate disruption, biodiversity loss and human and social harms deserve attention regardless of their treatment in financial reporting. Their existence does not prove this report's causal hypotheses. The question is whether our institutions can respond more adequately to specified challenges, while recognizing the limits of what they are entitled to know, measure and govern. Can we make that inquiry deliberate rather than reactive?
