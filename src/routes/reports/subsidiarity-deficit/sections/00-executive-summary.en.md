## Executive Summary

This synthesis asks whether mismatches between the scale of a governance task and the distribution of information, authority and delivery capacity help explain institutional failures. It revisits six exploratory reports: Germany, France, Sweden, India, the European Union and the United Kingdom. These are five states and a regional union with overlapping membership, not six independent observations. Their labels and proposed mechanisms are starting points for investigation, not established diagnoses.

The original edition argued that a common subsidiarity deficit followed necessarily from Westphalian architecture, and that eight architectural requirements and a particular transition strategy followed from that diagnosis. This revision withdraws those inferences. The evidence supplied by the reports does not establish a universal cause, unique institutional architecture or verified comparative outcomes. The reports and this synthesis were produced using AI-assisted analysis; the factual material has not been systematically checked against primary sources.

**A task-relative question.** A central decision-maker may lack crucial local information, but geographic centralisation is not identical to information aggregation. The same observation may be adequate for one task and inadequate for another. Conversely, local information does not itself confer legal authority, resources or the ability to coordinate across boundaries. The challenge is to allocate observation, decision and implementation functions in a way that meets specified outcomes and constraints, rather than to maximise decentralisation.

**Six historical hypotheses.** The earlier reports proposed execution difficulties in Germany, integration difficulties in France, feedback difficulties in Sweden, synchronisation difficulties in India, coherence difficulties in the EU and a control–delivery mismatch in the UK. These hypotheses can overlap, compete with other explanations or fail to apply to particular episodes. Appendix A preserves the original interpretive table as a historical record. Section 2 proposes more discriminating questions.

**Design considerations.** Distributed sensing, translation layers, fiscal autonomy matched to responsibility, deliberation, nested coordination, cross-border coherence, peripheral capacity and safeguards against capture remain useful candidate checks. They are neither jointly sufficient nor individually necessary in every setting. The trade-offs of subsidiarity include coordination costs, unequal capacity, rights protection and cross-boundary spillovers.

**GGF and transition.** The Global Governance Frameworks (GGF) offer one proposed institutional response. Their correspondence to the eight concerns is a conceptual design mapping, not evidence of independent convergence or operational success. The Genesis Protocol's emphasis on demonstrating value before seeking additional authority is a candidate strategy, not the only legitimate route. All experiments require appropriate prior authorization, transparency, consent and review. Success cannot itself confer constitutional authority.

**Research programme.** Start with a specified task, document actual information and authority pathways, distinguish alternative explanations, compare feasible architectures, and test interventions using prespecified outcomes, safeguards and credible comparators. The objective is to discover when subsidiarity helps, when other arrangements work better, and how each can be corrected.

The original title's implication that all six failures share a demonstrated cause is retired. The revised subtitle describes comparative hypotheses and a design inquiry. This is an AI-assisted correction candidate, not peer-reviewed research or an empirical re-audit.
