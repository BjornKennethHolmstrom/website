## 1. The Westphalian Achievement and Its Limits

### 1.1 Territorial authority as a historical question

The original report interpreted territorial sovereignty as a response to conflict over legitimate authority. In that account, a bounded state provided a locus for law, taxation and public administration. The history of state formation, however, is heterogeneous; territorial sovereignty is not identical to centralised administration. Federal systems, municipalities, courts, treaties and international organisations distribute authority in different ways. A historical assessment must distinguish legal sovereignty, practical control and the institutions through which people actually govern. The historical account is retained as an interpretive lead [HR], not a verified chronology or a claim about a single founding event.

### 1.2 The scale-mismatch hypothesis

A territorial boundary may fail to match the domain of a problem. Water, disease, finance, transport, migration and digital networks can involve several jurisdictions; context-sensitive services may vary substantially within one. Neither kind of mismatch is unique to the present era. Some tasks fit existing boundaries well; others require shared authority, information exchange or locally differentiated implementation.

The relevant research question is whether a particular allocation of decision rights and information channels prevents an adequate response, given the actual problem, resources and constraints. This is different from claiming that every territorial state has a single dominant scale or that the state form necessarily produces maladaptation. A national institution can coordinate local implementers and receive detailed information; a local institution can be internally centralised or lack useful information.

### 1.3 Multi-scale challenges

Climate adaptation, public health, cross-border finance, migration and digital infrastructure are candidate domains in which to investigate scale mismatch. In each, identify the concrete task: for example, a flood-warning decision, a shared financing arrangement, a rights-sensitive data exchange or the coordination of an electricity network. Trace who observes the relevant conditions, who may decide, who can execute and who is affected. Compare the existing arrangement with a feasible local, joint or higher-level alternative. A mismatch in geography alone does not establish a failure; evidence of a consequential limitation is required.

The original thesis that a once-adequate state architecture has become inadequate under accelerating complexity remains a historical hypothesis. Establishing it would require comparable measures of tasks and institutional capacities over time, not examples selected after observing failures. It is possible that improved technology, institutional learning or new coordination arrangements have reduced some mismatches even while others have grown.

### 1.4 Developmental perspectives

The original report used Spiral Dynamics to interpret the tension between order, performance, inclusion and adaptive institutional design. These remain useful questions to ask of a proposed arrangement. The developmental vocabulary is optional, not a validated causal explanation of governance outcomes, a classification of populations, or evidence that only one developmental stage can understand subsidiarity. Competing political and historical explanations must be considered on their merits. Do not infer what a population values from an institutional label.

### 1.5 Subsidiarity and task-relative adequacy

Subsidiarity is a normative and institutional design principle: consider allocating responsibility close to affected people where the relevant task can be carried out adequately, and coordinate at a wider scale when spillovers, shared resources, rights or capacity make that appropriate. This principle requires explicit judgments about the outcomes to protect, the distribution of burdens, legal mandates and feasible alternatives. It is not derived from a universal control-theoretic theorem.

The corrected engineering papers make a narrower point about information and feasible action. In the static noiseless linear model of VI, disturbances belong to a subspace D, observation is y = Cd, the residual d + u must belong to an acceptable subspace L, and u is the correction. Let K = D intersect ker C. Adequate deterministic regulation requires K to be contained in L; that condition is sufficient if every correction in −D is available. The condition concerns hidden distinctions that matter for the task, not the number of geographic levels. Appendix C spells out the corresponding finite-state example and action-cover condition.

A many-to-one observation cannot by itself distinguish states in its fibres. Other channels, histories or active tests may supply that distinction. Even without them, the same response may be adequate in every state consistent with the observation. Neither loss of information nor adequate information determines the location of authority. Authority is constrained by law, legitimacy and rights; action is constrained by resources, feasibility and coordination.
