## 2. Six Jurisdictions, Six Candidate Failure Modes

### 2.1 The three-layer diagnostic

The original sensing–decision–delivery distinction describes a coarse functional workflow: institutions receive information, decide on a response and implement it. It does not imply that the stages are exhaustive, occur in a single direction or correspond to distinct geographic levels. Feedback can connect them repeatedly. Corrected VI and VIII distinguish five diagnostic axes that cut across this workflow:

| Axis | Question |
|---|---|
| Evaluation | Which outcomes and interests are treated as relevant, and by whose authority? |
| Observation | Which task-relevant distinctions are accessible, at what time and with what uncertainty? |
| Modelling | Which explanations and causal relations can the institution distinguish? |
| Decision | Can information affect the selection of feasible and authorized actions? |
| Actuation | Can the chosen action be implemented, with necessary resources and safeguards? |

These are questions, not five independent measured variables. An institution may know what is happening but be unable or unwilling to act; it may implement a decision effectively that addresses the wrong objective. A task can have several deficits or none of the proposed ones.

Each of the following sections restates the historical report's hypothesis [HR/H] and offers a test strategy. No current national facts, laws, quantitative comparisons or conclusions have been independently verified here. In particular the older Sweden summary must not be treated as an authoritative description of the later revised report or companion analysis.

### 2.2 Sweden — feedback deficit

The earlier Competence Trap report proposed that strong professional competence and high trust may coexist with difficulty detecting, communicating and acting on disruptive evidence. Its drift-loop hypothesis connects delegation, filtering, delayed recognition, public controversy and corrective action. The Data Archipelago label draws attention to the possibility that information held by one institution may be unavailable or unusable to another.

Test the hypothesis in a defined pathway, such as a municipal service or interagency response. Document the evidence available at each step, the legal basis for access, what was shared, how it was interpreted, who was authorized to act and whether action followed. Compare cases where relevant signals did and did not reach the decision-maker; also test whether signals reached the decision-maker but were deprioritised. Alternative explanations include scarce capacity, uncertainty, conflicting priorities and deliberate rights protection.

Data protection and confidentiality are legitimate safeguards, not simply administrative obstacles. The original illustration that police cannot see health data is not retained as a categorical legal claim. Any proposed exchange needs a specific lawful purpose, necessity and proportionality, appropriate limits, access controls and remedy. A coordination cost does not by itself justify expanded surveillance. The latest Sweden report and current law must be examined before reuse of specific claims.

The proposed Framtidskommuner pilot can investigate whether authorized information pathways and local decision capacity improve defined outcomes without eroding rights or trust.

### 2.3 Germany — execution deficit

The Spending Mirage report proposed a mismatch between available investment resources and the ability to deliver projects. Its candidate mechanisms include interlocking responsibilities, approval sequences, staffing constraints and fragmentation across policy domains. These might interact, but funding without execution does not itself identify which mechanism is binding.

A study could follow a set of energy, housing or mobility projects from funding decision through permits, procurement and completion. Separate the dates of commitments and disbursements from operational delivery, compare comparable projects and record the reason for delay. Distinguish justified environmental or procedural review from avoidable duplication. A simpler administrative change or additional technical staff might outperform a transfer of formal authority. Conversely a joint mandate could help where responsibilities are genuinely interdependent.

Adaptive Governance Pilot Regions are a proposed setting to compare these possibilities, not a demonstrated remedy.

### 2.4 United Kingdom — control–delivery mismatch

The Control Mirage report proposed that a central institution can retain responsibility for outcomes while local implementers lack the discretion, revenue or stable planning horizon to deliver them. The centralise–fail–centralise loop hypothesises that poor delivery encourages additional central controls, which in turn can diminish local problem-solving capacity.

A useful test would trace a service mandate and its funding, staffing and legal discretion over time, distinguishing national standards from locally variable implementation. Compare delivery under alternative funding and accountability arrangements, accounting for needs and baseline capacity. Examine the possibility that central action protects minimum standards or supplies resources that local institutions cannot provide. The old report's illustrative Nottingham narrative is not verified here and is not used as evidence for this mechanism.

Trailblazer Regions 2.0 remain a proposal to investigate the effect of longer funding settlements and integrated mandates. Greater Manchester is a candidate comparator or case study, not an established proof of superiority in this synthesis.

### 2.5 France — integration deficit

The Decisiveness Mirage report described a possible reform–explosion–retreat loop: nationally formulated reforms may encounter resistance in delivery when their effects, legitimacy or implementation conditions have not been adequately addressed. This is not equivalent to an absence of local consultation or a claim that resistance is irrational. Substantive disagreement can persist even with excellent information and deliberation.

Examine the sequence of specific reforms, the affected groups, alternative proposals, consultation, implementation authority and subsequent changes. Compare similar interventions with different opportunities for participation and translation. Record both distributional effects and delivery results; distinguish failure to agree from failure to implement an agreed decision. Cultural and historical terms in Appendix A are interpretive labels, not explanations of all French institutions.

Territoires d'Intégration Adaptative are a proposed laboratory for context-sensitive design and deliberative processes under ordinary accountability.

### 2.6 India — synchronisation deficit

The Leap-Lag Cycle report proposed that institutional innovation and implementation capacity can be uneven across regions and sectors, so that achievements in one place fail to translate into comparable benefits elsewhere. Its scale-gradient hypothesis concerns heterogeneous constraints and learning opportunities, not a single national deficit inferred from population size or language diversity.

Track transfer of a particular administrative or service innovation between jurisdictions. Record staffing, infrastructure, funding, legal requirements, local languages and user needs; test whether differences in outcomes are explained by adaptation, resources or the intervention itself. Digital infrastructure may facilitate shared access while creating exclusion or privacy risks. Translation should be assessed by service outcomes, not simply by adoption counts.

The Synchronisation Sandbox is a proposed network for authorized experimentation and cross-state learning. The numerical claims about languages, caseloads and population in the original are not verified here and are not required for the hypothesis.

### 2.7 European Union — coherence deficit

The Coherence Mirage report proposed that agreement on broad goals can coexist with divergent implementation, timing and resources across member states. Its negotiation–dilution hypothesis concerns the difference between an agreed statement and coordinated delivery. The EU already embodies multilevel governance; it cannot be treated as simply a single-scale nation-state. Germany, France and Sweden also belong to this unit, so the cases overlap.

Trace a specific transboundary commitment, the decision rules and competences, national implementation and measured effects. Distinguish vetoes that protect legitimate interests from obstruction or bargaining that creates avoidable delay. Existing coordination mechanisms may be adequate in some domains and insufficient in others. Compare joint implementation, differentiated agreements, existing arrangements and stronger common authority, including their costs and safeguards.

Coherence Regions are a proposed test of shared responsibilities for specific cross-border tasks, subject to the actual legal powers and consent of participating jurisdictions.

### 2.8 A common question, not a common established cause

These six hypotheses ask whether institutional functions are poorly matched to a task. The shared vocabulary was used to develop the reports; recurrence is therefore not independent evidence of a single underlying mechanism. Test scale mismatch against rivals such as resource scarcity, conflict over objectives, causal model error, power, administrative capacity and legitimate limits on authority. Cases without the proposed mismatch, cases that perform well despite it, and failures corrected without decentralisation are informative. A good synthesis identifies the conditions under which a concept helps rather than redescribing every outcome in its terms.
