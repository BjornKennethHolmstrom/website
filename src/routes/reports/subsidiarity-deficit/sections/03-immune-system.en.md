## 3. Institutional Resistance and Stabilisation

### 3.1 The immune-system analogy

The original report used the political immune system as a metaphor for processes that maintain existing institutional arrangements. Interests, professional identities, legal requirements and organisational routines can resist a proposed change. Stability can be valuable: independent review, rights protection, predictable procedures and checks on authority may appropriately slow reforms. Other resistance can reflect concentrated benefits, deliberate exclusion, or administrative incentives unrelated to the public task.

The analogy is a vocabulary for possible mechanisms, not a claim that institutions have an identical or inevitable immune response. Specify the actor, authority, incentive, decision point and causal link. Test whether the resistance actually changes the outcome, compare reforms that succeeded and failed, and identify what the protected arrangement accomplishes. Do not treat disagreement as a pathology.

### 3.2 Germany: bureaucratic inertia and entangled coordination

The earlier hypothesis is that approvals distributed across multiple institutions can create mutually dependent decisions and delay. Such entanglement can also coordinate interdependent investments or prevent harm. A study should identify which approvals are required, which are duplicative, how information is shared and whether a change in sequencing or resources can improve delivery without weakening substantive safeguards.

### 3.3 France: the Jacobin spectacle

The historical label suggests that political incentives favour visible national announcements while the practical conditions of implementation receive less attention. A test could compare announced and implemented reforms, participation of affected groups, distribution of costs and the durability of changes. Visibility itself is not the problem: transparency can improve scrutiny and make accountability possible. The institutional and cultural interpretation requires evidence, not generalisation from a national stereotype.

### 3.4 Sweden: satisfied competence

The original hypothesis suggests that confidence in well-performing institutions could reduce attention to disconfirming signals. Confidence can also support learning and delegation. Examine evidence that adverse signals were available, how they were processed, and what authority or resources were available to respond. Distinguish suppression, justified confidentiality, selective influence and lack of capacity. Compare with episodes where institutions corrected themselves. The later Sweden report and critical companion should be consulted before empirical claims are made.

### 3.5 India: scale gradient and competing demands

The earlier report suggested that unequal administrative capacity and many competing political demands may make sustained coordination difficult. Diversity is not noise in itself; distinct needs and legitimate dissent may be information that a design should accommodate. Study the distribution of authority and resources, the ways local demands enter decisions and the ability to share successful adaptations. A national programme may assist regions with less capacity, while a uniform implementation rule may be poorly adapted in other cases.

### 3.6 European Union: sovereignty as veto

The historical hypothesis is that joint decisions can be delayed or weakened when individual members can block agreement. The decision rule can also protect members against unacceptable burdens. Study the relevant legal rule, the actual bargaining positions, externalities and possibilities for compensation or differentiated participation. Compare alternatives such as consensus, qualified voting or a limited joint mandate; do not infer that every veto is capture or that a voting-rule change necessarily improves outcomes.

### 3.7 United Kingdom: the visibility trap

The original label connects political accountability for highly visible outcomes to incentives for central control of implementation. A test should distinguish central monitoring that protects standards from controls that prevent context-sensitive action. Trace funding, reporting, discretion and consequences for delivery; consider whether different accountability arrangements would permit local adaptation without removing public oversight.

### 3.8 Reform resistance and political economy

The proposed mechanisms direct attention to who bears transition costs, who gains or loses discretion, and which actors have a credible route to contest a decision. Resistance need not increase monotonically with reform size. In an illustrative decision rule a coalition supports a reform when benefit b exceeds cost c: a small reform with b = 1 and c = 2 fails this rule, while a larger reform with b = 4 and c = 3 passes. This is a logical witness, not an estimated political response. Larger changes may sometimes offer compensating benefits or resolve interdependence that a small change cannot; small changes may sometimes be less disruptive.

Consider legitimate vetoes and consent requirements independently of strategic obstruction. A transition should address distributional effects openly rather than disguise a proposal or evade scrutiny. An effective reform may require changing incentives, improving resources, enforcing rights or creating a new information pathway; it need not entail transferring authority downward.
