## 5. The Trade-Offs of Subsidiarity

Subsidiarity is not costless. Distributing authority to lower levels of governance introduces coordination costs, risks uneven capacity between regions, and requires coherence mechanisms where fragmentation would be harmful. In domains such as pandemics, defence and macroeconomic stability, poorly aligned decentralisation can impede collective action. Equally, centralised decisions can overlook local needs or place implementation responsibility where resources are inadequate. Which cost dominates is an empirical and normative question, not a conclusion of this synthesis.

**Dynamic scale-matching** is a constrained design problem, not a scalar measure of geographical decentralisation. A local decision may be appropriate for a separable task; shared infrastructure, effects on outsiders, rights protection or urgent coordination may require a wider mandate. Translation layers can adapt common frameworks to local context, rather than abolish common frameworks. Fiscal equalisation can compensate for uneven regional capacity. Coordination can be binding or voluntary depending on the task and lawful authority. Observation, decision and delivery need not occur at the same level.

The GGF's Accountable Coordination Principle, Subsidiarity Protocol and multilevel councils are proposed responses to these trade-offs. Their inclusion in a design document does not establish that they manage the trade-offs in operation. Evaluate actual authority, incentives, costs, capacity, rights, accountability and outcomes, including failure of coordination or capture at the local level. Compare with existing arrangements and less costly alternatives. A system that acknowledges its own trade-offs is better positioned to investigate them; acknowledgement is not a guarantee of performance.
