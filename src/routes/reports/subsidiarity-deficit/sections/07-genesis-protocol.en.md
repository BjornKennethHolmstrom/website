## 7. The Genesis Protocol: Demonstrated Value Before Seeking Further Authority

### 7.1 The implementation problem

A proposed institutional redesign changes responsibilities, incentives and the distribution of power. Actors may resist when they expect losses or consider the change illegitimate; they may support larger changes when these address shared problems or offer credible protections. The original implementation paradox is a hypothesis about these incentives, not an inevitable increase in resistance with the magnitude of reform. Legal and ethical constraints apply at all scales. A pilot should not be selected because scrutiny can be avoided.

### 7.2 Build and learn within lawful authority

The strategic idea of demonstrating value before formal expansion is worth testing. Distinguish four steps:

1. Establish the applicable lawful mandate, affected groups, consent requirements, oversight, resources and boundaries before acting.
2. Conduct a limited experiment with publicly specified outcomes, comparison arrangements, data protection, costs, harm and stopping rules.
3. Evaluate implementation, outcomes, causal contribution and adverse effects. Publish negative and ambiguous results; enable independent challenge and review.
4. If expansion is justified, seek the additional authority, resources and consent needed for the next scale. Success in the pilot does not substitute for those requirements.

A voluntary municipal arrangement may be useful before a constitutional redesign, but it cannot acquire powers by demonstrating competence alone. Similarly, a constitutional change may be normatively justified or legally required independently of pilot evidence. The current Genesis Protocol and its institutional details should be evaluated against dated source editions before implementation.

### 7.3 Six proposed country-specific pilots

The original report identifies six proposals. They are not established operating GGF pilots, formal affiliations or demonstrations of efficacy. Participation, legal mandates, resources and agreements must be independently verified. The following descriptions preserve the original ideas as candidates with evaluation questions.

**Germany — Adaptive Governance Pilot Regions.** Integrated territorial mandates for energy, housing and mobility, with authorized flexibility to test coordinated approaches. Compare matched projects under existing and alternative delivery arrangements. Measure implementation times, costs, service outcomes and rights impacts, accounting for different project complexity and resources. Seek required federal and state coordination rather than operate beneath scrutiny. Stop or revise if safeguards cannot be maintained or delay merely moves elsewhere.

**France — Territoires d'Intégration Adaptative.** Regional laboratories for cross-sector design, deliberative bodies and stable funding arrangements. Compare participation procedures and implementation results, including dissent, distributional burdens and unsuccessful proposals. Demonstrated results can inform national discourse, but the work should be transparent from the beginning. Obtain the necessary mandates and consent, and review both effectiveness and fairness.

**Sweden — Framtidskommuner.** Volunteer municipalities exploring authorized information-sharing arrangements, deliberation with response obligations, and fiscal or legal flexibility for integrated services. Specify the service pathway, lawful purpose, limits on information access, evaluation and remedy. Compare with existing lawful coordination and non-data-sharing alternatives. Do not assume that broader access is necessary or desirable, and verify the latest Sweden analysis and current law before relying on its specifics.

**India — Synchronisation Sandbox.** A network of participating states testing structured experimentation, translation and cross-state learning, with proposed arrangements for timely resolution of disputes. Compare transfer of an innovation across different capacity and resource settings. Test accessibility, local adaptation, delivery and costs; assess exclusion and privacy risks. Judicial arrangements must respect due process and the actual independence and powers of courts. Dedicated experiments do not automatically establish general transferability.

**European Union — Coherence Regions.** Cross-border laboratories such as the historically proposed Alpine, Baltic, Danube and Rhine-Meuse areas, for specific jointly authorized tasks and shared budgets, execution or deliberation. Compare existing cross-border coordination and alternative arrangements. Define who can make binding decisions, who bears costs, how outsiders are affected and how disputes are resolved. A voluntary agreement among some participants cannot waive others' rights or relevant treaty requirements.

**United Kingdom — Trailblazer Regions 2.0.** Proposed integrated combined-authority mandates, longer-term funding settlements, fiscal discretion and capacity investment in health, housing, skills or social care. Greater Manchester's existing arrangements are a historical lead; their comparative performance is not established here. Specify baselines, outcome measures, funding and legal discretion; compare jurisdictions with comparable needs and capacity. Monitor differences in distribution, service standards and accountability.

All six proposals require transparent authorization, consent where applicable, oversight and review. Low controversy can reduce disruption but is not evidence of legitimacy. Choose scale for the task and safety of the experiment, not to evade political or legal scrutiny. If the requisite authority or protection cannot be secured, do not proceed.

### 7.4 Scaling by attraction

Voluntary adoption can be a way to learn and share useful practices. It may also select unusually well-resourced or motivated participants. The earlier report explicitly described participants as self-selected for readiness. Their performance relative to nonparticipants cannot by itself establish a causal effect.

For example, suppose high-readiness jurisdictions achieve 80 units with or without a pilot and low-readiness jurisdictions achieve 40 with or without it. If only high-readiness jurisdictions volunteer, the observed difference is 40 even though the treatment effect is zero in both types. This is an illustrative counterexample, not evidence about actual pilots. Use credible comparison designs, baseline measures, selection analysis and transparent reporting. Where possible pre-specify eligible sites, comparison groups and outcomes; investigate whether improvements transfer to sites with different constraints.

A demonstration can provide evidence in a form another institution can use, or fail to persuade because the result is not transferable, interests differ or the receiving institution cannot absorb the change. A bypass may integrate into existing institutions, remain a justified complementary arrangement, or become obsolete. Evaluate whether it relieves pressure without addressing an important underlying problem, or creates new dependency. Sunset, review, continuation and transfer conditions should be specified rather than assumed beneficial.

Scaling by attraction is a candidate strategy, not a proven law of durability. Some rights or shared dependencies require binding coordination beyond voluntary adoption. Compare voluntary diffusion, authorized statutory reform, shared service arrangements and other legitimate pathways. Avoid imposing participation while also recognizing that nonparticipants can be affected.

### 7.5 Honest limits

Pilots can fail, stall, be captured or produce benefits only under unusual initial conditions. Scaling can increase coordination costs, change incentives or expose unresolved conflicts. Strong local capacity can coexist with regional inequality; local accountability can fail as central accountability can. An intervention may require several years to assess, but the original 5–7-year horizon is a historical planning assumption, not a verified forecast or current protocol requirement. Publication of negative results, independent review and credible exit or transfer arrangements are essential. Design intentions do not guarantee performance.

### 7.6 Alternative legitimate pathways

The original claim that Genesis is the only path consistent with subsidiarity is withdrawn. Consider a hypothetical nationally enacted constitutional reform, locally ratified, which establishes legally protected local powers, provides independent review and prevents unilateral executive withdrawal. For tasks feasible locally with controlled spillovers, such an arrangement can satisfy a subsidiarity principle even though its initiation is central. Conversely, a bottom-up pilot can remain dependent on revocable central permission. The key distinctions concern enforceable powers, consent, accountability and the fit to the task, not origin alone.

Constitutional or statutory reform, federal deepening, intergovernmental agreements, networked arrangements and adjustments within existing institutions are alternative or complementary strategies. Their legitimacy and adequacy must be examined rather than inferred from their labels. Demonstrated value may inform persuasion and policy, but is neither a substitute for rights nor a necessary prerequisite for every legitimate reform.

### 7.7 A prospective research programme

Select specific episodes and record the task, legal mandate, affected people, time horizon, resources and rights constraints. Document existing observation, modelling, evaluation, decision and actuation pathways. Specify rival explanations and possible disconfirming evidence before examining outcomes. Compare feasible local, joint, higher-level and hybrid arrangements, including the option of improving existing institutions. Establish authorization and evaluation gates separately. Record selection, implementation, costs, outcome effects, distribution, harms and uncertainty; test transferability before expansion.

This programme has not been run. The synthesis offers a set of hypotheses and design questions, not a validated universal diagnosis or a certified architecture. Feedback from practitioners, affected communities and independent researchers is welcome, including evidence that the proposed lens adds little or that a different explanation performs better.
