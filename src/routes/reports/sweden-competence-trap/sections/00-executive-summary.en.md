## Executive Summary

*Reader’s note: indented historical quotations reproduce formulations from the earlier edition where the wording itself is being corrected. The accompanying prose states this edition’s position; the quoted formulations are not adopted claims or powers.*

Sweden’s accumulated institutional strengths are the starting point of this report: trust, transparency, welfare delivery and digital infrastructure are important resources to examine. The machine may hum and still miss consequential signals. Comparative performance requires indicator-specific evidence; high measured performance need not imply adequate detection of every emerging problem.

This report advances the **Competence Trap** as a hypothesis about institutional adaptation: *the better a system performs under known conditions, the harder it may become to recognize when those conditions have changed.* Sweden retains major governance strengths, but the report asks whether its ways of detecting, interpreting, and responding to change have kept pace with increasingly complex circumstances. The argument is a diagnostic framework to be tested, not an established general law.

The proposed **Swedish Drift Loop** links high trust to delegation, possible suppression or neglect of signals, quiet accumulation of problems, a triggering event and compressed reactive correction, potentially followed by erosion of trust and repetition. Housing, integration, energy and defence are candidate settings, not independent confirmations. The NATO accession illustrates a rapid pivot; the chronology in §1.4 and Appendix F.1(2) does not establish decades of suppressed warnings. Changed security conditions, competing priorities and legitimate deliberation are alternatives.

The proposed underlying condition is a **feedback deficit**: uneven capacity to detect signals early, share them when lawful and appropriate, interpret conflicting evidence, and act before problems compound. The degree to which this explanation accounts for Sweden’s challenges, relative to political incentives, resources, rights conflicts, and other causes, remains an empirical question.

The engineering models in Appendix C clarify task-dependent limits; neither they nor the examples certify the national diagnosis or a particular institutional design.

Three proposed structural mechanisms could contribute to the deficit:

- **The Data Archipelago.** Sweden combines advanced digital services with fragmented responsibilities, datasets, and analytical workflows. The Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act (2009:400) and the GDPR set safeguards, but information sharing is not categorically prohibited: legal possibilities expanded in 2025, especially for crime prevention and related purposes. The question is where lawful sharing, interoperable systems, and cross-agency learning still fall short—not whether agencies are wholly unable to exchange information.

- **Jantelagen as a Variance-Minimization Algorithm.** The original report interpreted consensus culture as historically reducing negative outliers—inequality, disorder, exclusion—while potentially filtering positive outliers, uncomfortable signals and productive conflicts today. This is a cultural metaphor, not a measured causal effect (Appendix F.2). The related **Mean-Reversion Fallacy** asks whether institutions sometimes assume disturbances return to equilibrium when they may instead be exponential or cascading phase shifts. Neither ordinary consensus nor appropriate screening of errors is evidence of suppression.

- **The Municipal Trap.** The proposed mechanism is *formal subsidiarity, functional centralisation*: municipalities carry responsibilities for schools, social services and crime prevention, and rely partly on local income taxes. Tax and cost equalisation are expressly designed to offset tax-base and structural-needs differences (§2.4; Appendix F.1(3)). Whether residual fiscal or experimental constraints leave particular municipalities without usable response capacity must be examined by task, after transfers, rather than inferred from local needs alone.

These mechanisms may reinforce one another and help explain delays between signal and response. Whether they do so consistently, and which other causes matter, requires empirical testing.

The report proposes a **feedback infrastructure upgrade** organised around four investments: privacy-preserving cross-agency learning, beginning with existing lawful exchange and disclosure-safe aggregates; structured disagreement through advisory deliberative bodies, bounded red teams and transparent futures literacy; fiscal and experimental flexibility only within specified lawful powers and common protections; and voluntary living testbeds—Framtidskommuner—to test the hypotheses. More information or more autonomy alone does not establish adequate response.

It examines a possible political immune system: satisfied competence, the consensus filter and the agency silo as institutional defence. The transition proposal works with Sweden’s strengths—trust, rationality and local self-government—through transparent municipal innovation (the historical “Trojan Horse” metaphor does not authorize a concealed purpose or power), lawful cross-silo data covenants, bounded pilots and voluntary learning across municipalities. Opposition and scrutiny remain legitimate.

**A proposed first step** is a network of 3–5 **Framtidskommuner** (Future Municipalities): voluntary pilots combining protected cross-agency learning, structured citizen deliberation, and bounded fiscal and legal flexibility. Each pilot would require a lawful basis for any data use, meaningful participation by affected groups, independent evaluation, and a way to identify harms as well as gains. Success must be judged by public outcomes and rights protection alongside feedback velocity and policy durability.

The 3–5 figure is the original summary’s planning illustration; §5.2 proposed five to seven and §6.2 seeks four types. No cohort size is adopted until coverage, selection, capacity and cost are assessed. Councils remain responsible, advisory participation grants no veto or new legal duty, and affected rights-holders retain their distinct rights and consultation channels. Pilots must stop or change on material harm; learning alone does not excuse it.

The European dimension completes the series as an **interpretive comparison**: Germany is examined through an execution-deficit hypothesis, France through an integration-deficit hypothesis, and Sweden through a feedback-deficit hypothesis. These are contrasting analytical lenses, not validated national rankings or exclusive explanations of each country.

The Swedish model need not become a museum piece. The image of the *folkhemmet* as pragmatic institutional craftsmanship is a historical interpretation open to scrutiny. The Competence Trap hypothesis is that the old model may need renewed sensing not because it failed, but because its very success may have made it stop looking. The question is whether that spirit can help Sweden become more permeable to relevant signals, realities its metrics may miss, and changing conditions. Which feedback mechanisms matter, whether this hypothesis holds, and which remedies help remain questions for evidence and legitimate decision.
