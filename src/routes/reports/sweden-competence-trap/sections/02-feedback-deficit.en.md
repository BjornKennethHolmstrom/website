## 2. The Feedback Deficit: A New Diagnosis

### 2.1 What "Feedback Capacity" Means

The term "feedback" has a colloquial ring. In everyday use, it suggests performance reviews, customer surveys, the annual conversation between manager and employee. That is not what is meant here.

Feedback capacity, in the sense that matters for Sweden's current situation, is the ability of a governance system to detect disturbing signals early, to share those signals across institutional boundaries without distortion or delay, to acknowledge their implications honestly — even when those implications challenge the prevailing consensus — and to act on them before they compound into crises.

Sensing and transmission are not themselves authority to act; timeliness must be judged against the legitimate task, evidence, feasible actions and rights, including justified restraint.

This is not a single capability. It is a composite of sensing, transmission, interpretation, and response, distributed across institutions, cultural habits, legal frameworks, and cognitive skills. When feedback capacity is high, a society can spot a housing market distortion, an integration challenge, or a security environment shift while it is still manageable — and can respond with measures calibrated to the scale of the emerging problem rather than the scale of the eventual crisis. When feedback capacity is low, the same signals travel slowly, if at all. They are filtered, softened, and sometimes actively suppressed. By the time they become undeniable, the window for calibrated response has closed, and the only remaining option is the kind of compressed, reactive correction the Swedish Drift Loop describes.

The architectural lens begins with the possibility that capable and dedicated professionals, trustworthy administration and deliberative processes can face limits in what their institutions can see. Claims about comparative corruption, independence or deliberative quality require separate evidence. Professional, political, financial and legal explanations remain alternatives to an architectural deficit.

This report investigates three interrelated mechanisms as possible structural properties: how signals arrive, which signals are filtered or suppressed, and where the capacity to respond is located. They may explain some delayed responses, but may also be absent or outweighed by attention, incentives, legal limits, changing conditions or implementation constraints. Evidence of timely consideration and adequate response would count against the proposed mechanism in the relevant episode.

### 2.2 The Data Archipelago: Where Might Task-Relevant Information Be Lost?

Sweden’s *personnummer* system and administrative registers can support high-quality longitudinal analysis under appropriate legal and governance arrangements. They do not, by themselves, create an all-purpose cross-agency data system or a general entitlement to link individual records. Any such processing requires specified purposes, a valid legal basis, and compliance with applicable secrecy and data-protection rules.

In practice, responsibilities, information systems, sector-specific rules, and analytical capacity remain distributed across agencies and levels of government. Yet sharing is not categorically blocked. Since 1 April 2025, Act (2025:170) has imposed specified disclosure duties to law-enforcement authorities; since 1 December 2025, a general secrecy-breaking provision in Chapter 10, Section 15a of the Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act (2009:400) has broadened inter-agency disclosure for defined purposes such as preventing crime and erroneous payments, subject to conditions and exceptions. The problem must now be examined purpose by purpose: what can lawfully be shared, what should be shared, and what still prevents usable collective analysis?

The boundaries are partly deliberate: secrecy, purpose limitation, and data protection protect people against misuse of sensitive information. Other obstacles may arise from incompatible systems, under-resourced collaboration, unclear responsibility, or uncertainty about how recently changed laws apply. Greater information sharing can improve coordination, but can also create disproportionate surveillance, profiling, and chilling effects. The task is to identify the narrowest lawful and effective response to a demonstrated information gap.

This is the **Data Archipelago** as an analytical metaphor: not sealed islands without bridges, but unevenly connected agencies whose legal powers, technical systems, and incentives do not necessarily produce shared learning. School attendance, municipal finance, and criminal recruitment may be studied using different datasets, levels of aggregation, and safeguards. The important test is whether the available evidence is actually brought together for legitimate decisions—not the unsupported assumption that no agency can ever connect the dots.

Transactional digitization is not the same as systemic visibility. BankID authentication across services, Swish payments and automated tax filing (including the earlier example of confirmation by text message) illustrate the transactional question, subject to verification. Point-to-point efficiency need not establish node-to-network sensemaking across agencies. The comparison does not show that Sweden lacks systemic learning or needs personal-data linkage; existing cross-domain evidence and powers matter.

The resulting tension still matters: highly efficient individual digital services do not guarantee timely cross-domain learning. Sweden’s policy challenge is to assess where existing legal gateways, interoperability, governance, and evaluation are adequate—and where targeted, privacy-preserving improvements are needed. The Data Archipelago is therefore a testable institutional hypothesis, not a factual claim that Swedish public data may be used only for transactions.

### 2.3 Jantelagen as a Variance-Minimization Algorithm

If the Data Archipelago is the outer architecture of the feedback deficit, the inner architecture is cultural. And the culture has a name, though it is more often invoked in self-deprecating jokes than in serious analysis.

Jantelagen — the Law of Jante — originates from a 1933 novel by Aksel Sandemose, in which a fictional Danish town enforces an unwritten social code: *You shall not believe that you are anything. You shall not believe that you are better than us.* The ten rules are a caricature of Scandinavian small-town conformity, but they capture something real. Over decades, Jantelagen has become shorthand for a cultural tendency to discourage individual exceptionalism, to value collective consensus over personal ambition, and to treat visible deviation from the norm with quiet suspicion.

As a metaphor, *Jantelagen* can illuminate how conformity and aversion to dissent might narrow an institution’s attention. It should not, however, be treated as a measured causal explanation for Sweden’s historical inequality, crime, or social cohesion: those outcomes also reflect policy, labour-market institutions, economics, and many other factors. The claim to test is whether particular decision environments systematically discount well-founded minority or dissenting signals.

A variance-minimization algorithm can suppress positive as well as negative outliers. By analogy, institutional screening could exclude the entrepreneur challenging a business model, the researcher questioning orthodoxy, the civil servant reporting policy failure or the citizen challenging consensus. Under stable conditions the benefits of screening error might outweigh missed innovation; under changing conditions early signals and unusual experiments might matter more. Social norms are not literal algorithms; whether valuable dissent is screened along with error requires evidence.

The innovation contrast — illustrated by Spotify, Skype and Minecraft — raises the question whether private-sector creativity finds comparable pathways into institutional adaptation. One hypothesis is that public-sector innovation can challenge established consensus in ways economic innovation does not. Actual pathways, including successful public experimentation, would test this rather than assuming suppression.

The **Mean-Reversion Fallacy** is likewise a proposed diagnostic metaphor rather than a demonstrated, Sweden-wide institutional assumption. In some settings, decision-makers may assume a disturbance is temporary when it signals persistent structural change. Distinguishing these cases calls for time-series evidence, explicit models, and records of how alternative interpretations were considered.

This assumption is not irrational. It has been correct for many disturbances over many decades. But it is structurally blind to exponential or cascading phase shifts — phenomena that do not revert to a mean because the mean itself has moved. When recruitment into criminal networks produces persistent self-reinforcing violence, it may indicate a new regime rather than a temporary deviation, requiring a qualitatively different response. Rapid population change and segregation dynamics may create persistent structural changes when housing and education systems cannot adapt. When climate-driven events accelerate beyond the pace of historical variation, the assumption of reversion becomes actively dangerous. The phase-shift argument concerns evolving processes, not a claim about the criminality of any demographic group or a demonstrated Swedish trajectory. Whether disturbances revert or compound depends on the actual dynamics and evidence.

The Mean-Reversion Fallacy connects the Jantelagen analogy to the Competence Trap hypothesis: an institution might assume disturbances are temporary because similar ones previously were, discount signals of regime change and eventually face a compressed response. Timely learning or well-founded rejection of inaccurate signals could count against this explanation. Not every successful institution assumes mean reversion.

The proposed mechanism need not involve malice. In a high-trust consensus-oriented setting, officials might regard an outlier as likely mistaken, agreement as informative and patience as reasonable, based on past experience. Those priors may become misleading as conditions change. Actual treatment of dissent and evidence, not assumptions about motives or all high-trust cultures, would test the mechanism.

### 2.4 The Municipal Trap: Responsibility Without Capacity

Sweden is often described as a decentralised state. The 290 *kommuner* (municipalities) and 21 *regioner* enjoy constitutional protection under the Instrument of Government, which guarantees local self-government. Municipalities levy their own income taxes, set their own budgets, and are responsible for some of the most essential public services: schools, elderly care, social services, spatial planning, and — increasingly — the frontline of integration and public safety.

This decentralisation is real, and it is one of the strengths of the Swedish model. It places decision-making authority close to the citizens affected by those decisions. It allows for variation in local priorities and approaches. It creates, in principle, a distributed network of governance laboratories that can learn from one another's successes and failures.

A possible limitation of decentralisation is **formal subsidiarity, functional centralisation** — responsibility devolved without matching authority or fiscal capacity. Residual constraints after equalisation must be examined separately for each policy field; formal responsibility alone establishes neither adequate nor inadequate discretion.

The fiscal dimension is important but more complex than a simple collapse of local tax capacity. Municipalities levy income tax, while Sweden’s income- and cost-equalisation system is expressly designed to offset differences in tax bases and structural service needs. It does not erase all pressures from demographic change, national mandates, administrative capacity, or timing; nor does it make municipal budgets independent of local decisions. The empirical question is which specific municipalities and responsibilities face a mismatch, after equalisation and grants are accounted for.

The municipalities on the frontline of integration, housing, crime prevention and social cohesion may face a mismatch between responsibility and their capacity to experiment or respond within national parameters. Actual resources after transfers, statutory powers, administrative capacity and outcomes for the specified task determine whether that mismatch exists.

A school principal noticing attendance or performance changes, a social worker observing exclusion or a police officer identifying criminal recruitment illustrates a possible local signal pathway. Information may need to travel across education, social services and public safety, while resources or authority may sit elsewhere. Whether signals were considered, by whom and with what lawful response requires contemporaneous evidence; neither subsequent harms nor silo boundaries alone establish a failure.

Where signals travel upward slowly or lose local context and responses return calibrated only to a national average, a municipality may receive an ill-fitting response. If only a national aggregate is observed, two municipalities with different local distress can be indistinguishable for a coordinate-specific response; an aggregate can be sufficient for a sum task. Existing registers (§2.2) mean aggregate-only observation cannot be presumed. Uniform responses, local knowledge and their effects are empirical questions; neither central nor local control is inherently adequate.

The question is not whether to abandon national standards or redistribution, but whether the sensing organs close to particular problems have the fiscal, legal and institutional capacity to respond appropriately. In the machine analogy, the sensors may work while the wiring, central processor or local actuators are mismatched to the task. Existing arrangements may suffice; additional powers require competent authorization and equal protections. A mismatch need not be deliberate.

### 2.5 How the Three Mechanisms Reinforce Each Other

The Data Archipelago, the proposed consensus filter, and the municipal capacity trap might interact. The following scenario illustrates one possible reinforcing dynamic; it is not a documented case or evidence that this sequence is typical across Sweden.

Imagine a local officer observing a change in youth recruitment into criminal networks. A responsible feedback process could combine the officer’s observations with suitably aggregated education, social-care, and municipal indicators, where necessary and lawful. It would also seek input from affected young people, families, and professionals. The goal would be to distinguish a real emerging pattern from misleading correlations and design a proportionate, rights-respecting response—rather than assuming that more individual-level data must always be connected.

In a weaker feedback setting, observations may fail to reach the appropriate decision-makers, or different actors may interpret them differently. Even where disclosure is legally possible, incompatible practices or capacity constraints may slow action. Other explanations—lack of consensus on effective interventions, competing priorities, or limited resources—should be examined alongside information fragmentation.

If delays persist, an initially manageable pattern could become more harmful before a coordinated response occurs. But this sequence is conditional: some Swedish institutions already share information, respond early, and adapt successfully. Studying both successes and failures is necessary to establish whether the proposed loop has explanatory power.

This is the kind of failure the feedback-deficit hypothesis aims to explain. Its relative importance compared with legal rights, material constraints, contested goals, and political choice remains to be tested. The next section develops proposals that would need independent legal, practical, and democratic evaluation.
