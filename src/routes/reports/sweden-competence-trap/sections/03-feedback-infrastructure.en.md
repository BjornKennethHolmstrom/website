## 3. What Building Feedback Infrastructure Looks Like

The feedback deficit diagnosis carries a practical implication: if Sweden's core problem is not a lack of competence but an architecture that suppresses, silos, and delays the signals needed for adaptation, then the central task is not to produce another round of policy reforms. It is to build the infrastructure that makes early detection, honest acknowledgment, and rapid response possible — and then to let that infrastructure reveal what needs doing.

This section describes four candidate investment domains: reconnecting the nervous system from transactional efficiency to systemic sensemaking, structured disagreement, matching municipal responsibilities with usable capacity within verified or specifically authorized powers and equal protection, and living testbeds to evaluate feedback architecture. They cross sectors and ministries; they are not universal prerequisites, and existing effective arrangements remain alternatives.

---

### 3.1 From Transactional to Sensemaking: Reconnecting the Nervous System

If the Data Archipelago hypothesis holds in particular settings, a promising investment is to improve cross-agency learning where justified—not simply to maximize data flows. The first step should be to audit existing powers, practices, and unmet needs in light of the 2025 reforms, and then choose the least intrusive approach capable of answering a specified public-interest question.

The goal is not a central surveillance database. A **federated sensemaking infrastructure** is one possible design, but technologies such as differential privacy or secure multi-party computation do not automatically establish legality, accuracy, safety, or anonymity. Each use case would require a defined controller, purpose, lawful basis, necessity and proportionality tests, security controls, and a data-protection impact assessment where required. When aggregate or non-personal data suffice, individual record linkage should not be the default.

A concrete proposal is a **National Signal Integration Framework** to document which lawful information exchanges already exist, identify unmet analytical needs, and develop carefully delimited pilot arrangements where authority is sufficient—or seek legislation where it is not. A useful starting case might assess aggregate school-to-work transitions without building individual risk scores. A public register of purposes, authorised participants, audits, retention rules, and avenues for complaint would help establish whether each arrangement creates more public value than privacy risk.

Alongside this, Sweden could test a **Societal Sensing Dashboard** using public or disclosure-safe aggregate indicators. It should show sources, update dates, uncertainty, missing populations, and relevant ecological outcomes; it should suppress or coarsen small cells to avoid identification. Such a dashboard can aid shared interpretation, but it cannot by itself establish causation, supply statutory powers, or substitute for deliberation with affected communities.

This would represent a shift from a solely transactional emphasis toward a **sensemaking state**: an administration able to study cross-domain patterns using suitable, lawful, and appropriately aggregated evidence. Understanding how neighbourhood conditions and service outcomes interact does not require displaying a named child’s school and health records on a common dashboard.

This investment would involve legal, organizational, technical, democratic, and financial choices. Even privacy-enhancing technology requires maintenance, specialist capacity, oversight, and public legitimacy. The binding constraints cannot be identified in advance without examining concrete use cases and the reforms already in force.

### 3.2 Structured Disagreement: Deliberative Infrastructure for Productive Conflict

If the Data Archipelago is one possible outer architecture of a feedback deficit, cultural and institutional filtering of variance is a possible inner mechanism. Where unjustified filtering is demonstrated, structured, protected disagreement may help beyond exhortations to speak up; existing channels and legitimate evidential screening must be distinguished.

*Earlier edition:*

> The first mechanism is a network of **standing citizen deliberative bodies** at municipal and regional levels. These are not one-off consultations or advisory panels that the executive can ignore. They are permanent assemblies of randomly selected citizens, given time, information, and professional facilitation, with a statutory right to a formal government response. If a citizens' assembly in a municipality recommends a different approach to housing integration, the municipal council must respond publicly, explain its reasoning, and — if it rejects the recommendation — justify the rejection in terms that the assembly can scrutinise. This is not a veto. It is an accountability mechanism that forces the system to engage seriously with signals from outside its own institutional filters.

A standing advisory body, with a publicly agreed voluntary reasoned-response procedure, is a candidate within existing powers only after those powers are confirmed. A statutory response duty is a distinct proposal requiring competent enactment; until then no enforceable duty is claimed. Elected councils retain decision authority. Lottery selection is not a representative mandate or veto. Affected residents, including noncitizens, should have accessible participation, safe refusal and withdrawal, compensation and complaints without retaliation; this cannot replace rights-holder consultation or collective authorization.

The earlier edition pointed to Swedish citizens’ assembly experiments, including a national climate assembly, as possible comparisons for a permanent multi-level arrangement. Their consultative status and influence, including energy development versus environmental protection and housing density versus community character, require verification. Ordinary consultation and existing channels may suffice; neither expert-led decision-making nor rejection of a recommendation alone demonstrates exclusion. Energy and housing decisions require actual authority and separate applicable rights processes.

An institutionalised **red team function** could give public administration an explicit remit to challenge prevailing assessments, including whether a cherished policy is failing or an emerging risk underestimated, or a comfortable assumption no longer valid. Official inquiries (*utredningar*) already offer a possible comparison: the earlier proposal draws on *tjänstemannansvar*, professional responsibility, and distinguishes episodic inquiry from standing challenge. Compare existing inquiry and review functions with optional bounded red-team exercises and, if justified, a standing function authorized by the responsible body. Its transparent remit is challenge and evidence, not a power to delay, veto or decide. Provide confidential reporting and independent complaints within verified employment and whistleblower protections; the earlier aspiration to speak without career damage is not a guarantee beyond actual safeguards. Evaluate useful corrections, false alarms, staff burden and retaliation; suspend the exercise if risks cannot be controlled.

**Futures literacy in education and public service training** is a possible way to explore alternative futures rather than predict them, following the earlier UNESCO framing of understanding the role of the future in what we see and do. Expanding the repertoire of plausible futures might widen present choices and help recognise emerging patterns before crises. The Finnish Committee for the Future and futures-literacy programmes are comparison leads requiring verification. Optional transparent planning and training exercises within existing mandates can be compared with ordinary professional development. Teacher-training or curriculum changes need approval by competent education authorities, evidence of educational value and regard for pupils, guardians and professional autonomy; no hidden curriculum, classification by developmental stage or compelled political agreement. Stop ineffective or harmful exercises.

These three mechanisms — deliberative bodies, red teams, futures literacy — share a common purpose. They are not designed to destroy consensus. They are designed to make consensus *earned* rather than assumed — the product of genuine deliberation and rigorous challenge rather than the suppression of dissent. In a high-trust society, this is a delicate cultural operation. The goal is not to import the adversarial political culture of other democracies. It is to give the existing Swedish commitment to *saklighet* — fact-based, rational deliberation — the institutional architecture it needs to function in a more complex world.

### 3.3 Territorial Capacity: Matching Authority and Responsibility for Kommuner

The Municipal Trap hypothesis raises the possibility that adjusting equalisation or adding earmarked grants may not address a structural mismatch. A structural shift is conditional on evidence of residual constraints after equalisation and existing cooperation; ordinary reallocation or improved implementation may suffice.

The first possible upgrade is **fiscal capacity matched to responsibility**. The fiscal proposal preserves solidarity and *kommunalekonomisk utjämning*, including compensation for structural differences without presuming dependency. For instance, a municipality might assess investment in preventive social work rather than acute interventions, with accountability for results and protection of mandatory services. Assess actual budgets after transfers, statutory service obligations, distribution and costs before proposing a targeted grant or lawful reallocation. No power to divert funds from mandatory services is implied.

*Earlier edition:*

> The second upgrade is **safe-to-fail experimentation authority**. Municipalities need the legal right to test alternative approaches to integration, housing, education, and service delivery without seeking permission from Stockholm. The mechanism already exists in embryonic form: *försöksverksamhet* — experimental activity — can be authorised by the government for specific purposes. But the process is slow, narrow, and politically contingent. A general Experimentation Act — a *försöksverksamhetslag* — would give municipalities a standing right to propose and implement bounded experiments, with transparent evaluation and a clear pathway for scaling what works. The central state's role shifts from gatekeeper to enabler: setting the parameters within which experimentation is safe, providing the evaluation infrastructure, and ensuring that successful experiments are visible to other municipalities.

The general standing right to implement without central permission is historical advocacy, not an adopted power. Municipalities may propose experiments and test within verified existing authority; departures require precisely enumerated permission from the competent authority or enacted legislation governing the sector. Publish purpose, permitted actions, duration, cost, common service floors, oversight, complaint and interim stop/reversal arrangements before authorization. A grant or pilot designation confers no exemption. Consider existing trial pathways (§6.4) or no additional power first. The design exclusion in §6.4 applies: no pilot exemption or experimentation arrangement may waive or narrow consultation duties owed to Sámi rights-holders, waive or narrow other legally protected rights, or decide concessions and permits held by other authorities.

*Earlier edition:*

> The third upgrade is **asymmetric capacity building**. Not all municipalities are equally positioned to take advantage of greater autonomy. Some have strong administrative capacity, dense civic networks, and a history of innovation; others are struggling with depopulation, skills shortages, and institutional fatigue. An autonomy framework that treats all municipalities identically risks widening the gap between the capable and the struggling. The solution is not to withhold autonomy from those who need it most — it is to pair autonomy with capacity-building support, so that municipalities at different starting points can build toward genuine self-governance over time. This is the principle of asymmetric devolution, applied at the sub-national level: different municipalities get different degrees of autonomy based on their demonstrated capacity to use it well, with a transparent pathway for increasing that capacity and the rights that accompany it.

Capacity-building is a candidate, not a basis for unequal rights. Tailored support and any additional powers require transparent task-specific criteria, competent authorization, independent evaluation and equal protection/service floors. Do not condition basic rights on administrative capacity. Compare shared services and support without new powers; stop or revise arrangements that widen unjustified disparities.

The northern regions—Norrbotten and Västerbotten—illustrate both the potential and the limits of territorial coordination. Industrial investment intersects with housing, labour markets, ecosystems, and Sámi land use and rights. Coordination is not a substitute for legal entitlements or the consultation obligations applicable to the Sámi people and affected Sámi reindeer-herding communities. Any pilot in such an area would need to identify affected rights-holders, consult them through the required processes, and distinguish issues a municipality may decide from those governed by national permits or other authorities.

Fiscal capacity, bounded experimentation and asymmetric support may continue the Swedish model where a demonstrated mismatch warrants them. The earlier edition interprets the *folkhemmet* as built partly through municipal welfare, housing and public-health experiments, and later centralisation as a response to industrial complexity; these are historical interpretations requiring historical-memory and affected-community review. The proposal is to test whether verified or specifically authorized flexibility, with equal protection and maintained solidarity, helps particular tasks. It is not a necessary extension or blanket transfer of powers.

### 3.4 Living Testbeds: Where Feedback Can Be Evaluated

Capacity may develop through application — trying to solve real problems in real places. A portfolio of integrated testbeds is a candidate for evaluating and refining the feedback infrastructure where a demonstrated gap warrants it; ordinary effective arrangements remain alternatives.

These testbeds are not "sectors" to be funded. They are spatial arenas — specific territories, at the municipal or sub-regional scale — where the three upgrades described above are combined and tested simultaneously. The goal is to create visible, documented examples of what a high-feedback governance system looks like in practice, and to let that evidence do the work of persuasion.

**Energy Transition, Rights, and Territorial Coherence.** Northern industrial projects create opportunities and significant strains on housing, services, ecosystems, and Sámi reindeer-herding lands. A pilot should map cumulative environmental effects and the distribution of benefits and burdens, not merely improve coordination. It must respect consultation duties, lawful land-use decisions, and the role of Sámi rights-holders; municipal deliberation cannot replace those obligations. The municipality may coordinate services and learning, but cannot unilaterally settle contested mining, environmental, or Indigenous-rights questions.

*Earlier edition:*

> **Housing Integration as a System Challenge.** Sweden's housing market is among the most dysfunctional in Europe, with waiting lists for rental apartments stretching for years in major cities and segregation hardening across urban areas. A feedback-oriented approach would select a few municipalities — perhaps one growing city, one stable mid-sized town, one declining rural community — and empower them to design integrated housing, transport, and education strategies. The data infrastructure would connect housing allocation data to school performance, labour market access, and health outcomes, making visible the systemic interactions that are currently invisible. A standing citizens' assembly would deliberate on the trade-offs — density versus sprawl, new construction versus renovation, social mixing versus community stability — and produce recommendations with a right to a formal municipal response. The experimentation authority would allow the municipality to test alternative approaches to land use, rent regulation, and social housing that are currently blocked by national frameworks.

The housing example remains a proposed joint learning and service task. Start with lawful aggregate evidence and existing coordination; connecting personal records of tenants, housing-queue applicants, pupils or other residents requires separate necessity, legal basis and safeguards under §3.1. An advisory assembly can recommend trade-offs with a voluntary reasoned response, not impose a council duty or replace tenants’ rights. The suggested rent, social-housing and land-use departures are historical advocacy until the competent sector authority has specifically authorized them; no pilot grants blanket permission. Protect service access and affected residents, assess alternatives and distribution, provide complaint and halt/remedy for harms.

*Earlier edition:*

> **School Reform as a Feedback Laboratory.** Sweden's school system has undergone multiple national reforms over the past three decades, each hotly debated, none producing clear and sustained improvement in equity or outcomes. A feedback-oriented approach would designate a few municipalities as *skolkommuner* with the authority to experiment with alternative governance models — teacher training, curriculum design, school organisation — within a framework of national learning goals. The data infrastructure would track outcomes not just as annual aggregate scores but as longitudinal trajectories, connected to housing, health, and social services data. The red team function within the municipal education department would systematically challenge the prevailing assumptions — that the current model is working, that the problems are external, that the solutions are known — and produce public reports that the municipal council must address.

The skolkommun idea is a conditional testbed, not a finding that past reforms failed. Compare authorized ordinary school improvements first. Teacher training, curriculum and organization changes require competent education authorization and protection of national learning and service obligations; no general school-law departure is adopted. Longitudinal learning starts with safe aggregates; any pupil-level linkage requires a separate lawful purpose, necessity, proportionality, independent review and safeguards for pupils and guardians. Red teams may issue evidence-based reports under a transparent authorized remit with verified protections, not binding instructions. A voluntary public council response differs from an enforceable duty, which requires competent enactment. Stop unsafe processing or harmful experiments and provide independent complaints and remediation.

These living testbeds are not an exhaustive list. They are exemplars of a different way of thinking about public investment: not as the allocation of resources to predetermined programmes, but as the creation of spaces where a new governance architecture can be built, tested, and refined. In each case, the primary output is not a bridge, a turbine, or a school. The primary output is the *capacity to produce such things intelligently in the future* — the feedback loops, the deliberative habits, and the institutional confidence that make continuous adaptation possible.

---

What unifies these four investment domains — sensemaking infrastructure, structured disagreement, territorial autonomy, and living testbeds — is that they are all investments in the system's ability to sense and respond. They do not compete with the urgent challenges Sweden faces. They may help in tasks where the hypothesised Drift Loop is observed; they are not universal prerequisites, and ordinary effective arrangements remain alternatives.

Even well-designed investments may encounter resistance through the institutional processes considered in the next section. Opposition, lawful refusal and evidence against a proposed remedy remain legitimate.
