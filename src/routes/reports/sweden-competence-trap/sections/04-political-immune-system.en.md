## 4. The Political Immune System: Why Feedback Fails

### 4.1 The Competence Trap as Immune Response

Political orders may develop reflexes that protect established power, assumptions and self-understanding. The Germany and France reports explore bureaucratic veto points and the political spectacle respectively; here the possible mechanism is **satisfied competence**. These are interpretive lenses, not established national types or rankings; existing safeguards and alternatives must be examined.

One possible reflex is not aggressive denunciation but a calm question grounded in perceived performance: *Our institutions are functioning. Our metrics are strong. What exactly needs fixing?* That question is legitimate, and the answer must be supported by evidence.

Confidence in institutions may be grounded in genuine achievement rather than cynicism. Rankings, aggregate statistics and everyday experience can be informative yet partial: metrics optimized for familiar conditions may obscure other task-relevant distinctions. Whether this occurs systematically in Sweden is an empirical hypothesis, not a structural property established for every successful system.

*Earlier edition:*

> A corollary of satisfied competence is that **failure must become visible and undeniable before it becomes discussable**. Until the evidence is overwhelming — until the gang violence makes international headlines, until the housing queues stretch beyond a generation, until the NATO pivot is forced by events rather than chosen by deliberation — the system defaults to the assumption that things are basically working and that deviations from the norm will correct themselves. This is not stubbornness. It is the rational inference from decades of experience in which deviations *did* correct themselves, in which the system *did* absorb shocks and return to equilibrium. The problem arises when the environment changes in ways that make that assumption obsolete, and the system's own success prevents it from noticing.

Housing and violence-related signals are cases to investigate, not proof of delayed recognition. The NATO wording in the original is too strong: §1.4 and F.1(2) document a rapid pivot but do not establish that earlier signals were suppressed or that deliberation was absent. Changed security conditions, attention and legitimate disagreement remain alternatives.

### 4.2 The Consensus Filter and Expert Dependence

A possible second mechanism is a consensus filter: uncomfortable signals might be discounted before reaching decision-makers. Agreement or rejection of a signal alone does not establish suppression.

*Saklighet* (fact-based deliberation) and *enighet* (broad agreement) are genuine democratic virtues that can support trust, reduce polarisation and make long-term policy possible. A potential vulnerability is that established agreement can be incomplete and dissent undervalued. Evidence would need to distinguish proper evidential screening, legitimate disagreement and competing priorities from unjustified filtering.

The hypothesized social dynamic need not involve censorship or conspiracy. A civil servant questioning an integration programme, a researcher presenting an outlier finding or a journalist reporting a disturbing trend may encounter demands for evidence or professional costs such as delayed promotion. Ordinary raised eyebrows or preferences for confirming work could be mechanisms, but do not establish retaliation. A test would compare contemporaneous treatment of dissent and accepted outlier findings and independently investigate career effects.

*Earlier edition:*

> Coupled with this is a deep **expert dependence**. The Swedish state relies heavily on agencies, expert authorities, and institutional trust. Citizens trust agencies. Politicians trust agencies. Agencies trust their own expertise. This trust is largely earned — Swedish agencies are, by global standards, competent and honest. But it creates a structural vulnerability: when expert consensus is wrong or outdated, the system has weak mechanisms for detecting that quickly. There is no institutionalised function whose job is to challenge the prevailing expert view. There are no permanent red teams within agencies tasked with arguing the opposite of the consensus. There is no standing deliberative body empowered to bring citizen experience to bear on expert judgment. The result is a system that can be honestly, diligently, and competently wrong for extended periods — not because anyone is acting in bad faith, but because the architecture assumes that expert consensus is reliable and provides no systematic way to test it.

The categorical absence claim is withdrawn: §3.2 itself recognizes existing inquiry capacity. A test would compare actual review and challenge channels, their independence, remit and effects, distinguishing episodic from standing functions. A standing red team is only a candidate where a gap is demonstrated and the competent body authorizes it; expert reliance and justified non-adoption alone do not show a failure.

The original report treated the immigration debate as a paradigmatic case of a possible consensus filter. That causal interpretation remains contested, not established. A testable element is the pathway from teachers, social workers and police officers through municipal and agency records to responsible decision-makers: what signals were available, when were they considered, and what actions were authorized and implemented? Fragmentation, selective influence, bounded attention, changed conditions, resource constraints, legitimate disagreement and evidential uncertainty are competing or interacting explanations. Later segregation, criminal recruitment or political polarisation does not establish that earlier warnings were suppressed, or that any side of the debate was correct. As with the NATO illustration (§1.4), the timing and causal mechanism require separate evidence; examine experiences of affected residents without attributing criminality or institutional failure to demographic groups.

### 4.3 The Agency Silo as Institutional Defence

An agency silo may in some cases function as a defence against inconvenient signals, but this is a hypothesis to distinguish from lawful secrecy, evidential standards, competing priorities and effective existing coordination.

Swedish agencies are, by design, autonomous. They are not subject to direct ministerial control in individual cases — a constitutional protection against political interference that is one of the strengths of the Swedish model. Each agency has its own mandate, its own budget, its own professional culture, and its own interpretation of what constitutes success. This autonomy creates islands of competence and integrity. It also creates islands of information, each self-contained and self-referential, each with its own metrics that confirm its own performance.

When a signal crosses agency boundaries—for example, possible interactions among school outcomes, housing, labour markets, and criminal recruitment—no single institution necessarily owns the entire explanation or response. Different actors may already cooperate, yet coordination can still fail because of competing mandates, scarce resources, disputed diagnoses, or governance incentives. The question is where collective responsibility is clearly assigned, exercised, and evaluated.

Moreover, agencies may have institutional incentives to retain control over their data and performance measures. Those incentives should be investigated rather than assumed to explain every restriction: legitimate confidentiality, statutory mandates, technical costs, and resource scarcity may be decisive in particular cases. Recent changes to Swedish disclosure rules mean that the operational question is increasingly whether existing possibilities are applied lawfully, consistently, and productively—not simply whether the law forbids cooperation.

One possible response to a cross-agency signal is a coordinating body, task force or national strategy that adds another layer without changing data access, authority or metrics. If it can only write reports, fragmentation may persist and the next signal may follow the same path. Actual mandates, outcomes, lawful limits and successful cooperation would distinguish this hypothesis from effective coordination.

### 4.4 The Narrative Strategy

The way the feedback agenda is explained may affect engagement. Framing the Data Archipelago as failure, Jantelagen as pathology or the Municipal Trap as betrayal might alienate people who value existing achievements. That is a hypothesis, not a prediction that objections prove an immune response. Transparent approaches and actual reasons for disagreement are the relevant comparison.

*Earlier edition:*

> The task, therefore, is to frame the agenda not as a rupture but as a **continuation** — the same pragmatic, evidence-based institutional improvement that built the *folkhemmet*, now applied to the architecture of governance itself. The argument is not that Sweden has failed. It is that Sweden succeeded so well at building institutions for a specific set of conditions that it now needs to upgrade those institutions to match the conditions that have since emerged. The Competence Trap is not an accusation. It is an observation that applies to all high-performing systems, and Sweden is simply far enough along the performance curve to encounter it first.

Continuity with pragmatic institutional improvement is a narrative option. The claim that all high-performing systems exhibit the trap, or Sweden encounters it first, is withdrawn as unsupported.

Trust, institutional capacity, rational deliberation and digital infrastructure, where present, may be foundations for reform rather than obstacles. The aim is to examine any task-relevant gaps while building on existing strengths, not to prescribe a different kind of society or presume a missing sensory architecture.

Different traditions bring legitimate concerns. For the social-democratic tradition, one question is whether the pragmatic adaptation associated with the *folkhemmet* can be renewed as conditions change. For liberal and green traditions concerned with innovation and sustainability, another is whether structures that filter uncomfortable signals may also inhibit the experimentation needed for the green transition. For conservative and moderate traditions concerned with stability and prudence, the original insight is that the greatest threat to stability may be not change itself, but the delayed recognition of change that has already occurred. These are perspectives and hypotheses each tradition may evaluate and legitimately reject, not claims about its supporters or reasons to presume agreement. None establishes the diagnosis or binds any tradition to support a remedy; all material aims and limits must be disclosed.

*Earlier edition:*

> The core message is deceptively simple: *Sweden is not broken. It is deprecating — like software that still runs but is no longer being actively maintained for the environment it is operating in. The upgrade is not a rejection of the original design. It is the continuation of the pragmatic tradition that produced it.* This is a message that can be spoken from any political platform without contradiction. And it carries the full feedback agenda inside it.

The software analogy is a metaphor, not a diagnosis established by evidence. Any concise framing must be accompanied before authorization by disclosure of all material purposes, powers, costs, data uses and evaluation conditions. No concealed agenda, mandate laundering or bypass of legitimate scrutiny is proposed.

The proposed political immune-system mechanism may matter in some settings, but resistance does not establish it. Trust, competence and rational deliberation can be resources for learning, and lawful opposition and evidence against reform remain legitimate.
