## 6. A Concrete First Step: Framtidskommuner

### 6.1 The Logic of the Pilot

A framework without a first step is a thought experiment. The proposed sensemaking, deliberation and territorial rebalancing may be tested through a locally bounded first step rather than a national programme; that does not establish its superiority. Existing arrangements and no new pilot must remain comparators.

A possible first step is a small number of voluntary municipalities where a task-specific responsibility–capacity gap is suspected, local civic capacity and political willingness exist, and the proposal can be tested against ordinary arrangements rather than assumed to demonstrate superiority.

This section proposes **Framtidskommuner** — Future Municipalities. The name evokes the earlier edition’s interpretation of the *folkhemmet* as a pragmatic, forward-looking tradition, a historical analogy open to scrutiny. The candidate tests whether a different architecture, or ordinary resources and arrangements, can better support task-relevant sensing, connection and appropriate timely response.

The Framtidskommuner are not an alternative to existing municipal structures. They are an enhancement — a set of additional authorities, capacities, and accountabilities that sit alongside the regular responsibilities of a Swedish *kommun*. They are voluntary. They are time-limited, with clear evaluation milestones and a pathway for extension or scaling. And they are designed to generate the evidence that will either validate the feedback upgrade or reveal its flaws — either outcome advances the learning.

Additional powers are not assumed: only existing lawful actions or specifically authorized departures are candidates, with affected-party protections, interim stop/remedy and a separately authorized extension. Learning does not certify outcomes or rights compliance.

### 6.2 Selection Criteria

The Framtidskommuner should not be chosen by political convenience or by a competition that rewards the most polished application. The goal is to create a credible proof of concept, and credibility depends on selecting municipalities where the challenges are real, the conditions are varied and their selection limitations explicitly documented, and the local capacity to engage seriously is present.

Five criteria should guide selection, administered through an open call managed by an independent foundation or research consortium, at arm's length from government:

The independent body would administer selection only within a transparent authorized remit; publish decisions, conflicts and complaints routes. Selection is not a grant of regulatory power.

**Problem density.** The municipality should face a meaningful cluster of interconnected challenges — for example, housing segregation combined with school inequality, or rapid demographic change combined with labour market mismatch, or industrial transition combined with social cohesion stress. The point is not to find the most distressed municipality, but to find municipalities where the interconnectedness of challenges is unmistakable and the limitations of siloed approaches are visibly costly.

**Existing civic infrastructure.** The municipality should have a baseline of functioning institutions, active civil society organisations, and some history of cross-sector collaboration — even if fragmented. The Framtidskommun is not a state-building mission from scratch. It is an upgrade to a system that already has some capacity to absorb it. A municipality with a dense network of engaged actors — strong *föreningsliv*, collaborative political culture, professional administrative capacity — would accelerate quickly.

**Political willingness.** The municipal leadership — both political and administrative — must be genuinely committed to the experiment, not merely tolerant of it. This commitment must include willingness to accept public scrutiny of mixed results, to protect the pilot through its inevitable difficult phases, and to invest staff time and political capital in the new deliberative and data-sharing arrangements. The safest sign of genuine willingness is a cross-bloc commitment, ideally spanning the traditional left-right divide.

Commitment cannot shield a pilot from stop, redress or lawful scrutiny. Staff and residents retain safe refusal and reporting routes.

**Scalability relevance.** The selected municipalities should collectively represent the diversity of Swedish conditions: at least one growing urban municipality, one stable mid-sized town, one rural or remote municipality facing demographic decline, and one municipality with a high proportion of foreign-born residents. If the pilots succeed only in uniquely favourable microclimates — a wealthy suburb with no integration challenges and a homogeneous, highly educated population — their lessons will be dismissed as irrelevant to the municipalities that face the hardest problems.

These are intended coverage types, not a probability sample; a three-municipality cohort cannot cover four distinct types unless overlap is explicitly designed and justified. The original 3–5 and five–seven planning figures remain unresolved pending selection, feasibility and author decision.

**Manageable scale.** The municipality should be large enough to contain meaningful systemic interactions — its own housing market, its own school system, its own social services — but small enough to be governable as a single learning entity. Most Swedish *kommuner* fall naturally within this range. The very largest — Stockholm, Göteborg, Malmö — would require sub-municipal pilot zones to be manageable; this is feasible but adds complexity for the first cohort.

A transparent selection process, with published criteria, an open call, and a publicly reasoned decision, would itself be a signal of the feedback architecture at work. It would create a constituency of municipalities that have chosen to participate rather than been conscripted — an essential foundation for scaling by attraction.

### 6.3 Core Design Features

Each Framtidskommun would be shaped by local conditions and priorities, but all would share a set of core design features that embody the feedback upgrade.

*Earlier edition:*

> **Integrated governance mandate.** The pilot municipality would receive a tailored package of regulatory flexibilities and coordination authorities that allow it to treat education, housing, social services, integration, and public safety as a single integrated design space. This does not mean abolishing existing institutional boundaries or creating a new super-department. It means giving the municipal leadership — political and administrative — the authority to coordinate across silos, to pool budgets where appropriate, and to be held accountable for cross-domain outcomes rather than silo-specific outputs. The national government sets the goals; the municipality decides how to meet them, with the flexibility to deviate from national programme specifications where it can demonstrate a reasoned alternative.

The integrated mandate is conditional on actual authority: coordinate existing services and pool budgets only where permitted, with councils and competent sector authorities retaining their respective duties. Any departure from a national programme requires the specific route described below; no general flexibility, implied permission or displacement of rights is adopted.

**Lawful cross-silo learning infrastructure.** Each Framtidskommun would begin with a legal-and-privacy assessment of specific learning needs, using existing aggregate data where possible. Any personal-data exchange would require its own legal basis, documented necessity and proportionality, security, access controls, retention limits, and independent review; a covenant cannot grant access by itself. Public dashboards would publish only disclosure-safe aggregates, with checks for small-group identification and misleading conclusions.

**Standing citizen deliberative body.** Each Framtidskommun would establish a recurring *medborgarråd*, selected by civic lottery with stratification and deliberate outreach to people whose experiences are usually underrepresented. Participation would include accessibility support, compensation, independent facilitation, multilingual access where needed, transparent agenda-setting, and a formal public response from the elected council. Deliberation would complement—not override—representative institutions, statutory rights, or the specific consultation duties owed to rights-holders such as affected Sámi communities.

**Fiscal and experimentation autonomy.** A Framtidskommun might receive a targeted grant and specified permission to test alternative processes, subject to the legislation that actually governs each policy area. It would not have a general right to disregard national education standards, planning law, social-care duties, or privacy rules. Exemptions, if necessary, would have to be enacted or authorised through the appropriate legal route, published, time-limited, independently evaluated, and reversible.

**Adaptive learning framework.** Independent evaluators would preregister the pilot’s hypotheses and compare it with credible non-pilot cases where feasible. Measures should include feedback velocity and policy durability, but also education, health, service access, well-being, distribution of benefits and burdens, trust among underrepresented groups, privacy incidents, rights compliance, and unintended harms. More meetings or faster decisions are not successes if outcomes deteriorate or rights are infringed.


For implementation, the advisory council response may be a voluntary reason-giving undertaking only unless a competent enactment establishes a duty. Participation is not limited to voters; safe refusal, complaints and nonretaliation are necessary. Separate rights-holder processes remain. Each dashboard has an accountable owner and withdrawal/correction route; each trial needs documented harm-stop, continuity and winding-down arrangements. The evaluator compares ordinary lawful arrangements and no new pilot.

### 6.4 Budget, Governance, and Legal Basis

**Budget.** A preliminary planning assumption of SEK 50–100 million per municipality per year over an initial five-year period could be examined, but this is not a costed estimate. The budget must be built bottom-up from the selected pilots, existing grants, legal and technical assessments, staff capacity, participation support, and independent evaluation. Costs, opportunity costs, and the case for continued funding should be published before commitments are made.

*Earlier edition:*

> **Governance.** Each Framtidskommun would be governed by its existing municipal council, which remains the legitimate democratic authority. The national government's role is to provide the legal and fiscal framework, the technical assistance, and the political cover. An independent **Framtidskommunernas Råd** — a small secretariat, perhaps housed within SKR or a university — would facilitate peer learning, manage the evaluation contract, and ensure that lessons flow between the pilots and outward to the wider municipal sector. The governance is deliberately light. The point is to empower municipalities, not to create a new layer of oversight.

The council remains responsible within its actual powers. National support is lawful resources and protection of honest reporting, not protection from scrutiny or intervention. A learning secretariat may convene and administer a contract but cannot substitute for competent oversight, independent evaluation, complaints or redress. Publish remit, conflicts, accountability and cost before establishing it.

**Legal basis.** Municipal self-government under the Instrument of Government and the Local Government Act provides an important starting point, not a blanket authority to depart from national law. A pilot might need targeted legislation, time-limited exemptions, or existing experimentation pathways, depending on its activities. The 2025 assignment to Statskontoret to examine municipal trials is a relevant policy route. Any dedicated **Försöksverksamhetslag för Framtidskommuner** would have to specify its scope and remain compatible with constitutional rights, sector-specific duties, EU law, and data-protection requirements. It could address:
- The scope of regulatory exemptions available to participating municipalities.
- The governance and accountability requirements for the data-sharing covenants and the citizens' assemblies.
- The evaluation framework and the role of the independent learning partner.
- The conditions under which the pilot status can be extended, modified, wound down, or scaled.

As a design constraint, no pilot exemption or experimentation arrangement may waive or narrow consultation duties owed to Sámi rights-holders, waive or narrow other legally protected rights, or decide concessions and permits held by other authorities (§3.4).

The proposed Act would include a sunset clause: after five years, pilots would be considered for renewal on the basis of demonstrated results or concluded in an orderly fashion. A time boundary may reassure sceptics and provide an occasion for national discussion of the results.

A sunset is not evidence of actual reversibility. Before any authorized experiment, specify an interim halt, service continuity, redress, data disposition, resources and a funded transition on closure; no renewal or exercise of disputed power until the applicable gate is satisfied.

### 6.5 How to Measure Success

The success of the Framtidskommuner must be measured in terms that connect directly to the feedback deficit diagnosis — and that speak to the practical concerns of municipalities, citizens, and the national government.

**Feedback velocity** is a proposed process metric, measured from a clearly defined, auditable first signal to a documented and appropriate response. The comparison should account for case mix, pre-existing capacity, selection effects, and simultaneous reforms. Faster is not always better: escalation without evidence, coercive overreaction, or privacy-violating shortcuts would count against the pilot rather than for it.

**Policy half-life** tracks how long local initiatives survive and remain effective. A Framtidskommun that launches an integrated housing-and-education intervention and is still running it effectively three years later, with adaptations informed by continuous sensing, has achieved a long half-life. A municipality that launches a similar intervention under conventional conditions and sees it diluted or abandoned after a year — when political attention shifts or a key official leaves — has a short half-life. The comparison reveals whether the feedback architecture produces durability.

Durability is not itself a success: a harmful or ineffective policy should be corrected or discontinued. The reasons for survival or withdrawal, not merely elapsed time, would inform evaluation.


**Cross-silo coordination frequency** is a secondary process indicator: joint analyses and operational cooperation can be counted, but meeting volume is not a proxy for quality. Evaluators should examine whether coordination changes decisions, improves outcomes, avoids duplication, and remains proportionate to administrative costs.

**Citizen trust trajectories** would be tracked through municipal surveys, comparing pilot municipalities to their own baselines and to matched non-pilot municipalities. The hypothesis is that municipalities with standing deliberative bodies and transparent sensing infrastructure will show slower erosion of trust — or even trust gains — relative to the national trend. Citizens who can see that their municipality is paying attention and who have a structured channel for their voice may become more trusting, or at least less distrustful, than those who encounter the state as a distant, opaque, and unresponsive machine.

**Adoption by other municipalities** is a measure of transferability, not the ultimate proof of success. Replication should follow independent evidence of benefits, rights compliance, manageable cost, and suitability for different local conditions; attractive but ineffective approaches should not be scaled simply because they spread.

The evaluation would be published in accessible, visual formats — public dashboards, annual learning reports, municipal peer workshops — designed to make the pilots' experience legible and usable. Transparency is the engine of scaling by attraction. The more visible the learning, the harder it becomes for the old architecture to dismiss it.

Public results must use disclosure-safe aggregates, with independent disclosure review, suppression/coarsening where necessary, correction and withdrawal routes, and uncertainty and small-group risk checks. No individual records or new decision powers follow from a dashboard.

---

The Framtidskommuner are a proposal, not a demand. They do not require every municipality to change, every agency to open its databases, or every citizen to join a deliberative assembly. They require only that a handful of municipalities — five, perhaps seven — be given permission to try something different, and that the rest of the country be given the opportunity to watch.

Five or seven is historical planning language, not a selected size. Participation is voluntary and not authorization for otherwise unlawful activity; stop or decline when material rights or feasibility conditions remain unresolved, and do not scale merely because others choose to participate.

The image of the *folkhemmet* as the outcome of municipalities experimenting, learning from one another and converging on what worked is an attributed historical interpretation of the earlier edition, not proof of pilot efficacy or legitimacy. Framtidskommuner are an invitation to examine that aspiration; historical-memory and affected-community review remain necessary.
