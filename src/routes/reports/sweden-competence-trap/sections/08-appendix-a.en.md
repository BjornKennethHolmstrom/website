## Appendix A: Value Systems and Policy Mindsets — A Guide for the Swedish Context

### A Note on This Appendix

The main body of this report avoids specialised terminology from developmental psychology or cultural theory. It speaks the language of state capacity, feedback loops, and institutional improvement. This appendix offers a complementary lens for readers who wish to understand the deeper value-system dynamics at play in Swedish governance. It is optional, but it makes the report’s underlying logic fully transparent.

### A.1 The Basic Insight

Different groups and institutions tend to operate from different centres of gravity in how they think about governance, resources, and change. These are not personality types or party affiliations, though they correlate loosely with both. They are underlying value systems—ways of constructing what feels real, legitimate, and important.

Each value system represents a coherent response to particular life conditions. None is “better” in any absolute sense. Each has characteristic strengths that emerge under certain conditions and characteristic blind spots that emerge under others. The challenge of governance in a complex society is to integrate the legitimate concerns of multiple value systems without being captured by any single one.

The framework used here draws on Spiral Dynamics integral theory. What follows is a simplified map of the systems most relevant to contemporary Swedish governance.

This is an interpretive vocabulary, not established science, a developmental ranking of persons or evidence that the cited mindsets cause Swedish outcomes. The following portraits are historical interpretations; actual institutions and people may express several concerns in different contexts.

### A.2 The Value Systems in the Swedish Arena

**Order and Stability (sometimes called “Blue”) — the Constitutional State.** In this lens, Sweden’s *rättsstaten* — the rule-of-law tradition, constitutional protection of rights and respect for due process — illustrates the concern for order. Its strengths are procedural integrity, institutional memory and equal treatment before the law. Possible blind spots include rigidity, elevating process over outcome and preserving structures whose function has degraded. This is not a claim about all institutions.

**Achievement and Efficiency (sometimes called “Orange”) — the Innovation Economy.** This lens emphasises competitiveness, entrepreneurship, scientific progress and measurable results, concerns associated in the earlier interpretation with Sweden’s transformation from an agricultural to a high-tech export economy. Strengths: innovation, global orientation and willingness to embrace technology. Blind spots: externalities outside market metrics, inequality efficiency does not capture, and undervaluing what cannot be quantified. Transactional digital efficiency illustrates the lens; whether insufficient integration explains its limits requires evidence.

**Inclusion and Care (sometimes called “Green”) — the *Folkhemmet* Ethos.** Protecting the vulnerable, including marginalised people and leaving no one behind are concerns associated with social insurance, integration aspirations and global justice and peace. Strengths: empathy, solidarity and human dignity. Possible blind spots: dependence on consensus, difficulty with hard trade-offs and treating expressions of inclusive values as substitutes for achieved outcomes. Whether these tensions affect the treatment of integration signals is a hypothesis, not documented suppression or an established causal effect of this value perspective.

**Integrative and Systemic (sometimes called “Yellow”) — the Adaptive State.** This lens prioritises functional fit, systemic awareness and integrating perspectives without capture by any one. Strengths: flexibility, whole-systems thinking, comfort with uncertainty and experimentation. Blind spots: detachment, excessive abstraction or political impracticality. Municipal innovation, *försöksverksamhet* and futures-oriented governance are possible examples for interpretation, not evidence of a developmental distribution or absence of institutionalization.

**Holistic and Planetary (sometimes called “Turquoise”) — the Ecological Consciousness.** This lens recognises ecological, social, economic and planetary interdependence, with long-term holistic awareness. *Lagom*, *allemansrätten* and awareness of Sweden’s embeddedness in larger systems are possible cultural resources. Strengths: long time horizon, comfort with complexity and grasp of interdependence. Blind spots: abstraction, lack of grounding or political impracticality. This does not rank Sweden or imply a developmental stage is blocked by an established trap.

### A.3 The Feedback Deficit as a Value-System Clash

The constitutional concern for procedure, entrepreneurial concern for efficiency and caring concern for solidarity offer complementary lenses on Swedish governance. Each may contribute to the model. Whether institutional arrangements allow them to sense collectively what none can see alone requires evidence, not an assumed dominance or measured distribution of mindsets.

As metaphors, the Data Archipelago contrasts transactional efficiency with solidarity and systemic sensing; the variance-suppression hypothesis contrasts consensus with adaptation; and the Municipal Trap contrasts constitutional form with changing economic and social demands. Actual mandates, evidence, incentives and constraints must be examined separately.

The proposed feedback infrastructure aims to integrate efficiency and innovation, legal coherence and privacy protection, citizen voice and equity, and systemic and long-term concerns. Framtidskommuner are possible spaces for testing integration without imposing a “higher” mindset. These outcomes must be independently evaluated; no pilot or perspective is presumed superior.
