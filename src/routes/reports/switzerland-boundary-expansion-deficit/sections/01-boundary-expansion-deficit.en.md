## 1. The Boundary Expansion Deficit

### 1.1 Governance within and across boundaries

Imagine a Sunday in late November. Swiss citizens go to the polls. On the ballot are three illustrative questions: an adjustment to climate policy, a revision of immigration quotas, and ratification of an international treaty. Rather than voting only for representatives who will later aggregate their preferences, the electorate can decide directly on particular questions with legally defined consequences. The scene is a composite, not a claim about an actual day's ballot. Regular voting provides a distinctive opportunity to examine how decisions are made and how authorities are held to account. A ballot records a choice among specified options, not preference intensity or the full range of preferences.

Yet the climate policy is affected by emissions on other continents; migration by conflicts, economic disparities and demographic changes; and the treaty by the priorities and bargaining positions of its partners. People in a supply chain or a neighbouring jurisdiction can experience consequences without belonging to the electorate. Their interests may be known, represented indirectly, disputed, or neglected. The contrast is between who decides and who experiences the consequences, not between an enlightened electorate and an ignorant one. This report asks when that contrast matters for a particular task, and whether existing arrangements can handle it.

### 1.2 The core diagnosis

A Boundary Expansion Deficit is a possible mismatch between the scope of responsibilities or coordination and the causal footprint of a task. A domestic aggregate objective may be achievable under current mandates even when consequences cross borders. A joint task requiring different responses to different locations may need disaggregated information or partner authority. Geography, information, objectives and feasible action are different variables. Distinguish the five axes defined in Appendix C.

In a commodity supply chain, firms may know prices and deliveries without knowing labour conditions; they may know those conditions but have no duty to disclose them; the public sector may already have the information but lack an authorised or affordable remedy. Each gives a different explanation of inadequate outcomes. Fiscal effects of corporate structures, financed emissions and supply-chain harms are possible topics, not quantities inferred from Swiss prosperity. Identify affected groups, including non-residents, and the distribution of benefits and costs. The Dutch, Singaporean and other comparisons in the original series motivate questions, not validated rankings or a universal law of externalization.

### 1.3 Institutional resources to investigate

The original account identifies 26 cantons, over two thousand municipalities, four national languages, local housing and drug-policy experiments, and the 1847 Sonderbund War followed by constitutional redesign in 1848. These historical leads need source verification. Federalism may support locally informed choices; direct democracy may make particular preferences visible; linguistic autonomy and consensus arrangements may accommodate pluralism. Their performance depends on the task, access and inclusiveness, the division of responsibilities, the quality of information and the costs of coordination. Cantonal differences can also create unequal capacity or externalities. No architecture is certified by these illustrations. The constitutional and institutional history is a research opportunity rather than an existence proof.

**Local learning and containment.** A housing experiment in Zurich or a drug-policy initiative in Bern may generate lessons for authorities elsewhere without requiring every canton to adopt the same approach. Education experiments in Ticino are another lead from the original account. Where jurisdictions have genuine discretion and resources, a local failure may remain limited while another jurisdiction tries an alternative. The relevant tests are whether information travels, whether the consequences really remain local, and whether less-resourced cantons can learn and act.

**Accommodation rather than uniformity.** The original report points to linguistic diversity, Catholic and Protestant cantons, urban financial centres and rural communities. Federal allocation of powers, the political convention of the *Zauberformel* and the double-majority rule offer different means of accommodating interests; they do not guarantee representation of all affected parties. The 1847–48 transition from the Sonderbund conflict to the federal constitution is an especially important historical lead: a redesign of the relationship between cantons, rather than a simple demand for uniformity. Examine the settlement's actual provisions, exclusions and subsequent changes. These are institutional resources to study, not evidence of a general optimality theorem.

### 1.4 The signature pattern

The original Internal Optimization–External Shock–Reactive Fudge Loop describes four possible phases: prioritisation of domestic arrangements, external pressure, incremental adjustment and continued domestic governance under changed conditions. Pragmatic compromise may relieve immediate pressure without addressing some longer-term causes. But an incremental agreement may also be the appropriate response, and a delayed agreement can expand the feasible options. Banking secrecy, commodity trading, tax alignment, climate finance and the EU relationship are candidate episodes or domains; none is a predicted next crisis.

Measure sovereignty in separate dimensions: formal competence, feasible options, bargaining influence, reciprocal access, democratic participation and obligations. Record who benefited or bore costs and what alternatives were available at the time. An earlier agreement is an unobserved counterfactual, not a baseline outcome that can be read from a later one.

### 1.5 A task-relative profile, not a split estimate

The previous internal, external and composite estimates, interval, band and trend are withdrawn; Appendix C explains why. There is no numerical Swiss ranking or critical boundary threshold. Compare tasks only with compatible evidence, deadlines and outcomes. For a domestic service and a cross-border service, record the five axes separately and allow unknowns; do not average unlike tasks. A wider mandate can entail coordination costs, rights burdens or reduced local autonomy. Better information, resources or enforcement within an existing mandate may suffice.

### 1.6 The real question and three prospective tests

Before testing, specify the task, affected people, rights and mandate, episode population and inclusion rules, comparator, horizon, independent outcomes and materiality criteria. Code unsuccessful and contrary episodes as well as apparently confirming ones; distinguish missing evidence from evidence against a claim. Lock the hypotheses before evaluating outcomes.

**H1 — Asymmetric Observation.** Trace actual information, its relevance and reliability, lawful access, interpretation and uptake at the same time and for the same task. Publicly available, usable information acted on in time disconfirms the proposed private/public disconnection for that episode. Information absent even privately defeats the existing-but-disconnected hypothesis. Adequate information without action directs attention instead to authority, resources, incentives or disagreement.

**H2 — Reactive Fudge Loop.** Compare proactive, delayed and no-change episodes where credible comparisons exist; record terms, options, timing, bargaining circumstances, rights, costs and participation. Reactive agreements that preserve or expand options contradict necessary erosion. Earlier agreements with worse comparable terms, or shocks without the proposed sequence, weaken the timing/recurrence hypothesis. One sequence cannot identify the earlier-agreement counterfactual.

**H3 — Boundary Expansion Deficit.** Determine whether the scope of mandates or coordination is a binding constraint on independent outcomes. Compare existing, narrower, jointly coordinated and wider arrangements where feasible and lawful. Adequate outcomes under existing mandates disconfirm the deficit for that task; improvements within them can contradict the need for expansion. A genuinely implemented wider mandate removing the hypothesised constraint but not improving outcomes, or producing disproportionate harms, weakens the diagnosis or remedy. Failure to implement is a separate finding.

These tests concern the diagnoses, not whether the three proposals in Chapter 5 are effective. No test has been executed. The historical four-stage Governance Problem Hierarchy is retained in the coda as a question set, not a necessary developmental progression.
