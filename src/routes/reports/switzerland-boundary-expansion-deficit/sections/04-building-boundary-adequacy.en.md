## 4. What Building Boundary Adequacy Would Look Like

### 4.1 Boundary recalibration without unnecessary disruption

Begin with the task and existing capabilities. Investigate whether a narrower, coordinated or wider mandate would improve outcomes and whether changes to information, incentives, staffing or enforcement suffice. Preserve useful local autonomy, direct-democratic accountability and procedural safeguards; do not assume every existing institution is adequate or that new institutions are unnecessary. Compare costs, rights and alternative designs. Cultural familiarity is a possible aid to communication, not a substitute for authorisation.

### 4.2 From asymmetric observation to better evidence

A Global Footprint Dashboard could combine existing lawful data with new measurements of the fiscal, environmental, financial and labour consequences of Swiss economic activity. The proposed Global Impact Assessments for major legislation would be mandatory to produce but nonbinding in conclusion, and would identify affected groups, relevant uncertainty, attribution limits, alternative scenarios and the expected costs of action and inaction. Neither more disclosure nor more citations demonstrates a better decision. The Dashboard would need a transparent purpose, independent governance, affected-party testimony, data minimisation, access and correction rules, protection of confidential and personal information, and a review of capture and gaming risks. Chapter 5 gives an operational proposal; existing assessment mechanisms are comparators.

**Target mechanism:** Asymmetric Observation (§2.1). The design changes the information on which decisions are made, not the authority by which they are made. It would establish a statutory, purpose-limited right of access for the independent Dashboard body, subject to applicable data-protection rules, confidentiality, proportionality, appeal and secure handling; this is a proposed power, not existing law. Where reliable data are missing, commission measurements or publish uncertainty instead of filling gaps with estimates presented as facts. The original draws analogies to regular reporting by the Swiss National Bank and Federal Statistical Office: a permanent information function, rather than a one-off study or a new regulator. Verify the precise statutory powers and independence of the comparators.

Global Impact Assessments would be mandatory to produce for major legislation, but their conclusions would be advisory, not legally binding on the legislative decision. This would require explicit attention to cross-border incidence without transferring legislative authority to the Dashboard. Publish the assumptions, alternatives and reasons for decisions, while protecting lawful confidentiality. An assessment may reveal a genuine trade-off or justify keeping the existing arrangement. Information does not guarantee an adequate response; assess whether it is used and what independent outcomes change, not merely whether a report is produced.

### 4.3 Strategic foresight expansion

A proposed Federal Foresight Unit could examine international regulatory, ecological, technological and economic changes and publish scenarios with uncertainties and explicit assumptions. The original comparison to other countries' foresight practices is a research lead (Appendix B). Inventory existing Swiss capabilities before creating a unit; identify the decision-makers able to use its analysis, the actual lead time required and how forecasts would be tested against outcomes. Retain scenarios that would justify no change or narrower action. Forecast accuracy and policy value are distinct.

The original proposal located the unit in the Federal Chancellery rather than the Department of Foreign Affairs, linking departmental foresight to the Federal Council's strategic planning. Compare both placements and existing functions; require an explicit mandate, independence, and review of actual use. Its suggested topics included geopolitical risk, energy transitions, supply-chain vulnerability, climate migration and technology governance.

**Target mechanism:** The neutrality-related information-pathway hypothesis (§2.2). Alternative pathways could provide relevant signals without membership in the institutions that generate them. The original cites Partnership for Peace and observer arrangements as leads, alongside diplomatic and research exchange. Verify actual participation, eligibility, information-sharing restrictions and obligations before treating any route as available. The proposal is to complement existing channels if a demonstrated gap remains, not to presume they are inadequate. Compare the costs of an independent foresight function with extending a current one, and protect legitimate confidentiality. A scenario that reaches the Federal Council early but leads to no justified change may still be useful; a new unit is not an outcome in itself.

### 4.4 Proactive interface mechanisms

Switzerland may be able to participate earlier in some international standard-setting processes, including financial disclosure, sustainable finance, tax transparency and commodity supply-chain standards. Compare membership, observer and consultation arrangements, diplomatic cooperation and unilateral action where lawful. Assess actual access, obligations, bargaining options, local competences, costs and rights. Earlier engagement need not produce better terms. Maintaining neutrality or declining an agreement may be legitimate. International organisations based in Geneva and the Bank for International Settlements are candidate institutional contacts, not automatic sources of public authority.

**Target mechanism:** The Reactive Fudge Loop (§1.4, §2.6). The original distinguishes *defensive participation*, aimed at protecting discretion and market access as external rules develop, from *constitutive participation*, aimed at contributing to the design of those rules while they are being negotiated. These are possible postures, not mutually exclusive legal categories. Defensive engagement may be justified by rights or costs; constitutive engagement can create influence but also obligations. Compare both, along with non-participation, for the relevant task.

Concrete domains include sustainable-finance disclosure and supervision, where the Swiss financial sector and the BIS in Basel are potential contacts, and commodity supply-chain standards, where trading expertise and evidence from producing communities could inform the rules. Participation could involve open consultations, technical working groups or proposals jointly developed with partner jurisdictions. A Swiss contribution would need transparent positions, affected-party participation and appropriate authorisation before undertaking obligations. The test is whether earlier involvement improves independent outcomes and preserves feasible choices, not simply whether Switzerland is present at the negotiating table.

### 4.5 Transparent contextual adaptation

Explain proposals using familiar Swiss practices — federalism, professional expertise, direct democracy and pragmatism — while disclosing the actual mandate, purpose, intended functions, possible future expansion and costs before approval. The historical Trojan Horse label is retired: communicating in familiar terms is not a licence to conceal a transformative purpose or to evade scrutiny. No regulatory harmonisation, training content, data sharing or irreversible commitment should be hidden in an unrelated mandate. Low salience is not legitimacy. Require applicable authorisation and consent, participation of affected people, independent review, accessible complaints and remedy, and stopping or revision rules.

**Target mechanism:** Political resistance associated with *Sonderfall* (§2.7). The design question is whether familiar Swiss strengths can be offered openly as contributions to cooperation, rather than presenting cooperation as a repudiation of Swiss institutions. Three possibilities from the original report deserve separate consideration.

**Federalism as a contribution to global governance.** Experience with distributing responsibilities among jurisdictions of different languages and interests could inform work on cross-border and multilevel governance. Swiss practitioners might exchange lessons with partner regions, including failures and limits, and co-design arrangements suited to local circumstances. Transfer is not assumed: constitutional authority, histories, resources and rights vary, and a domestic federal arrangement cannot simply be projected globally.

**Financial expertise as standards leadership.** Swiss experience in financial services and supervision could support work on transparent beneficial ownership, financial stability, sustainable finance and enforceable international standards. The aim would be to bring expertise to the design process while making conflicts of interest explicit. Include other jurisdictions, independent experts and affected communities rather than letting the industry define public outcomes. Regulatory differences may be legitimate; each proposal needs evaluation on its merits.

**Direct-democratic experience as deliberative infrastructure.** Swiss practice in voting on specific issues, preparing explanatory material and conducting multilingual campaigns could inform deliberative processes on shared problems. A voluntary international exchange or a jointly designed advisory assembly could test whether these methods help participants reason about trade-offs. This is not a proposal to confer Swiss voting authority over other populations or to replace their own institutions. Every participant's mandate and the nonbinding character of advice must be clear. None of these contributions requires accepting the report's diagnosis.

### 4.6 Internal–external coordination pilots

Basel, Geneva and Zug are proposed, not selected or authorised, for cross-border health and environment, multilateral perspectives and digital finance respectively. They may draw on cantonal experimentation, but must establish legal competence, partner consent, funding, appropriate information arrangements and independent evaluation. Existing cross-border initiatives and narrower alternatives are relevant comparators. A bypass may complement, be absorbed into, or leave an existing institution unchanged; a successful local pilot can produce evidence for federal learning but does not mandate scaling. Reports to Parliament, consultation of participants, a five-year sunset and biennial review could support accountability and learning. A finding that no further federal change is needed is admissible.

**Target mechanism:** Veto topology and temporal mismatch (§2.4–2.5). The original cites drug-policy and education experiments as precedents for cantonal learning; verify which measures were piloted, the actual federal uptake and the conditions of transfer. This proposal uses that possibility deliberately: a willing canton and consenting partners could test a bounded response without imposing it on the whole federation. The pilot must operate within lawful competence and cannot waive rights or approval rules merely by calling itself experimental.

**The bypass trap.** A pilot can relieve a problem locally while leaving a broader coordination constraint untouched. If its success attracts political attention away from the unresolved constraint, decision-makers may treat the exception as sufficient and never evaluate broader arrangements. Conversely, the pilot may demonstrate that local provision or a standing cross-border agreement is sufficient and that there is no reason to expand federal authority. Distinguish these outcomes using a baseline, a specified task, evidence of spillovers, and an assessment of what remains outside the pilot's scope. The design should include a route for communicating results to other cantons and the federal authorities, an explicit decision on continuation, transfer or termination at review, and an account of the interests and costs of affected non-participants. Neither automatic scale-up nor automatic integration is warranted. The point of the experiment is to discover whether the alleged structural obstacle binds, not to bypass democratic authorisation or manufacture an argument for expansion.

### 4.7 *Sonderfall* and *Vorbild*

The original report advocates moving from exceptionalism towards an exemplary role in addressing shared problems. This is an open political proposal, not a stage transition or inevitable cultural evolution. Citizens may prefer neutrality, existing bilateral arrangements, other reforms or no expansion. A citizens' assembly could make trade-offs explicit and hear people affected by Swiss decisions, including non-residents, but it has no independent constitutional mandate to change them. The aim is informed judgment rather than securing assent to a predetermined identity.

**Target mechanism:** The cultural triad (§2.8). The proposed *Vorbild* reinterpretation is not a rejection of Swiss distinctiveness. It asks whether the practices valued at home might also inspire cooperation with others, without pretending that such cooperation is costless or universally desired. Citizens could instead conclude that a different understanding of neutrality or existing arrangements better protects their values. The discussion should make those alternatives explicit.

This cannot legitimately be imposed from above. A change in collective identity cannot be ordered by a foresight body or engineered through hidden messaging. The assembly could explore the meaning of sovereignty, solidarity, neutrality and cross-border responsibility, and set out competing recommendations, uncertainties and minority views. Citizens and competent institutions would retain decision authority. The original is candid that cultural change, where it occurs, can take decades rather than legislative cycles. Institutional experiments might inform that conversation or be worth keeping for their own limited purposes even if the identity does not change. A legitimate result of deliberation is disagreement.
