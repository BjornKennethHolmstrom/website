## 6. Coda: The Boundary as a Design Question

### 6.1 The Governance Problem Hierarchy as questions

The earlier series grouped problems into four historical families: building observation capacity, closing feedback loops and allocating authority, accommodating heterogeneity, and coordinating across institutional boundaries. These are questions rather than stages a country must pass in order. Problems can coexist; solving one does not establish that the others are solved. The Finland, Israel, Spain, Russia and other comparisons remain historical interpretations pending their own reviews.

### 6.2 Swiss institutional resources

The 1848 constitutional settlement, local experimentation, federalism, linguistic diversity, consultation and direct democracy make Switzerland a valuable case for comparative study. They do not provide an existence proof of a formally optimal architecture. Study the circumstances under which these arrangements preserve local knowledge, handle conflicts and deliver outcomes, and the circumstances under which they do not. Cross-border effects invite a further question about coordination rather than a verdict on national development.

### 6.3 The shift

The original report proposes *Sonderfall* to *Vorbild*: from a special case towards an example of cooperation on shared problems. This is an invitation that can be debated, revised or rejected. No theorem requires an expanded boundary; a negotiated interface, a change within an existing mandate, or maintaining current arrangements may be preferable. The reactive sequence is a research hypothesis, not a prediction of progressive loss of sovereignty.

### 6.4 A final word

A useful report must make its own diagnosis correctable. If firms lack the supposedly available evidence, if reactive agreements expand options, or if existing mandates deliver adequate outcomes, revise or reject the relevant hypothesis. The Dashboard, cantonal pilots and assembly offer concrete questions for testing, not proof of the diagnosis. Their value depends on lawful authority, consent, costs, rights and independent outcomes. The three cases in Chapter 3 are starting points for verification. The wider invitation is to ask whose consequences matter, whose evidence is heard, and who can legitimately act, without assuming that all answers require the same institutional boundary.

Switzerland is not in crisis in the sense of the diagnosis proposed here. It is not failing simply because the consequences of its decisions cross borders. The report is not an assessment of every current Swiss problem. It is an invitation to examine a particular kind of institutional limit without disregarding the achievements worth preserving.

The original closes with the image of a mirror and its frame. Institutions can reflect some of the preferences, experiences and concerns of the people who participate in them; a frame defines which questions and voices enter that reflection. Widening a frame need not mean breaking the mirror, but it also need not improve what is reflected. The design task is to ask whether a particular additional perspective is needed, whether the existing mirror already reflects it, and how the arrangement would preserve its clarity, legitimacy and capacity to act. If external pressures repeatedly reveal binding limits, is it better to anticipate those pressures through legitimate cooperation, or respond when concrete problems arise? If the existing arrangements remain adequate, the case for expansion has not been made. The question is open to evidence and to Swiss democratic judgment.
