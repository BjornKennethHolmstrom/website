## Appendix B: International Analogues

These are historical leads from the original report, not verified precedents of effectiveness or proof of transferability. Check dates, actual institutional powers, participation, evidence of effects and alternative arrangements before drawing conclusions.

### B.1 Comparative cases

Norway's 1972 and 1994 EU referenda and EEA participation invite comparison of market access, rule adoption and influence over rule-making (the original mentions the criticism of “fax democracy”). The Netherlands raises questions about tax flows, EU-level regulation and domestic responsibilities. Singapore motivates comparison of regional participation through ASEAN, financial regulation and global standards leadership; no capacity ranking or equivalence of cases is implied. Irish deliberative processes concerning marriage and abortion motivate study of the relationship between advisory deliberation, legislative authority and referenda; verify which convention or assembly did what and when. None establishes that a Swiss assembly or wider mandate would work.

**Norway.** The original uses the two membership referenda and the EEA as a way to examine the difference between accepting rules and shaping them. The criticism of “fax democracy” refers to concerns about incorporation of rules developed elsewhere. Verify the actual EEA procedures, opportunities for consultation and adaptation, and the differences between Norwegian and Swiss arrangements. The comparison should consider market access and reciprocal benefits alongside restrictions on discretion. It does not establish that membership or alignment is preferable.

**The Netherlands.** The original draws attention to another trade-dependent economy and to tax arrangements with possible consequences abroad. EU membership may provide additional coordination mechanisms, but does not eliminate the possibility of external costs. Compare specific fiscal, regulatory and supply-chain tasks, not an aggregate national capacity ranking. Verify the legal and economic claims separately.

**Singapore.** The original contrasts a small, trade-connected city-state with Switzerland and points to regional participation through ASEAN and technical work in financial regulation, port governance and urban planning. Investigate whether and how selective engagement can contribute to shared rules without general political integration. Singapore and Switzerland differ in constitutional structure, geography, democratic arrangements and regional context. These differences limit transfer; verify the actual activities and outcomes rather than treating Singapore as a ready-made model.

**Ireland.** The original invokes deliberative processes on marriage and abortion, associating recommendations with subsequent referenda. Verify the distinct roles and dates of the Constitutional Convention and Citizens' Assembly, their selection procedures, how recommendations entered legislative decisions and the eventual votes. They provide leads for studying how advisory deliberation and direct voting may complement each other, not evidence that an assembly will break every impasse. Swiss multilingual participation, constitutional competence, minority concerns and access for affected non-residents need their own design.

### B.2 Institutional analogues

The UK Climate Change Committee (the original cites the Climate Change Act 2008) motivates a study of independence, reporting and actual influence without direct regulatory authority. INTERREG and the Upper Rhine cooperation arrangements motivate study of funding, cross-border mandate and partner consent for cantonal pilots. The OECD Global Forum on transparency and exchange of information motivates analysis of the costs and benefits of early participation in standard-setting. Verify also the precedents for strategic foresight invoked in the original report. For each analogue ask whether the task, resources, legal powers, legitimacy and relevant outcomes are comparable.

**The UK Climate Change Committee.** The original describes an independent statutory body established under the Climate Change Act 2008, with a reporting relationship to Parliament and without direct power to compel particular policies. Verify the statutory duties, access to data, actual reporting arrangements and evidence of influence. The analogy with the Dashboard concerns an ongoing independent information function, not the automatic effectiveness of its advice. The Swiss proposal has a different scope and needs a separate legal basis. The INTERREG and OECD examples above also need specific examination of partner consent, financing, obligations and the actual role of Swiss actors in designing standards, rather than an inference from their existence alone.

### B.3 Cross-references in the series

Finland's throughput constraint, Israel's boundary deficit and Spain's integrative closure are hypotheses in other reports, not independently verified diagnoses. They motivate distinct questions about response timing, contested authority and territorial heterogeneity. The Swiss institutional history may offer comparisons, but does not establish a sequence of national stages or validate the framework's predictions. The old quantitative comparisons with Finland, Japan and Spain are withdrawn. Comparative claims must be re-evaluated with task-level evidence; no other report is corrected by this appendix.
