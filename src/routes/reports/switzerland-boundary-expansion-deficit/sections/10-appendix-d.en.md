## Appendix D: Anticipated Objections

### D.1 Switzerland is not broken. Why diagnose it?

No national failure or optimality is established here. The question is whether particular arrangements work for particular tasks. A useful comparison must allow adequacy and learn from success as well as failure.

The objection is helpful because it distinguishes the object of inquiry from an assessment of a whole country. Federalism, direct democracy and the constitutional settlement may be resources for a particular task; a study should investigate when they work, including cases that contradict the proposed deficit. The report is concerned with the possibility of institutional boundaries becoming misaligned with particular cross-border consequences. It is not a verdict that Switzerland must pass through a new stage. If existing responsibilities and cooperative arrangements are sufficient, the proposed diagnosis fails for that task. The four historical families in the coda are questions that can coexist, not a developmental ladder.

### D.2 Externalization is widespread. Why Switzerland?

Cross-border incidence is not uniquely Swiss. The juxtaposition of federal institutions and internationally connected activity motivates comparison, not a best-case or worst-case selection. Verify harms, benefits and causation rather than inferring them from wealth or trade.

The original compares the Netherlands, Singapore, Ireland, Luxembourg and the United States. These are useful prompts for a comparative inquiry into whether an internationally connected economy can internalise particular effects through domestic rules, reciprocal agreements or joint institutions. They are not a set of countries shown to externalize the same costs. Switzerland's distinctive institutional arrangements make it an informative case, not a demonstration of exceptional virtue or culpability. Specify comparable tasks and independent measures before asking whether a mechanism found in one country occurs elsewhere. Benefits to partner populations and the effectiveness of existing international measures belong in the comparison as much as harms.

### D.3 Expansion could damage valuable arrangements

This is a substantive objection. Dynamic alignment, sharing information, common regulation or broader fiscal commitments may constrain local autonomy, create burdens or conflict with rights. Delayed agreement can also have costs. Compare actual alternatives and independent outcomes, including no expansion, and preserve legitimate safeguards. Earlier agreement is not automatically better.

This is perhaps the strongest objection. A new agreement may reduce cantonal fiscal discretion, a dynamic rule-adoption process may change legislative options, and common standards may increase compliance costs. There can also be gains in market access, predictability or protection for affected people. These are real design questions rather than costs to be dismissed in the name of integration. Examine which powers are transferred, which safeguards are retained, whether obligations are reciprocal and how decisions can be reviewed or changed.

The original uses banking secrecy and the EU relationship to suggest that waiting for external pressure can reduce bargaining options. That claim is H2, not an established historical counterfactual. FATCA and the OECD-related changes provide leads; compare actual terms, available alternatives at the time, distributional effects and circumstances that changed independently of Swiss policy. The earlier Framework Agreement and later Bilaterals III package cannot be treated as identical offers in a controlled experiment. Waiting can be costly, but it can also preserve discretion or produce better options. The comparison may favour no expansion.

The design response is to investigate arrangements that preserve valued features — local autonomy, direct-democratic authority, negotiated compromise and linguistic accommodation — while addressing a demonstrated problem. A narrow agreement, a pilot, existing enforcement or voluntary cooperation may be preferable to a wider mandate. If a design imposes disproportionate costs or rights burdens, revise or reject it. No principle in the report overrides a legitimate democratic decision against a particular proposal.

### D.4 Bilaterals III may show the existing arrangement works

That is a legitimate hypothesis. Verify ratification, obligations and outcomes; the mid-2026 account is not a current-status assessment. A package can yield reciprocal access and restrict discretion. Evaluate whether the specified tasks are adequately handled, rather than assuming that an incremental agreement is failure or that its adoption proves adequacy.

The objection points to a possible success: pragmatic negotiations may stabilise relationships and open areas of cooperation while retaining substantial domestic autonomy. That possibility should be recorded as potentially contrary evidence, not dismissed because the agreement followed a period of pressure. The original mid-2026 account identifies dynamic law adoption, dispute resolution, cohesion contributions and amendments to Swiss law as topics for comparison; verify legal status and scope, including the original claim of amendments to 36 laws, before factual reliance.

A later agreement can have both benefits and constraints. Evaluate distinct sovereignty dimensions and task outcomes, including access, participation, labour protections, compliance costs and practical decision options. Compare the circumstances of the negotiations, not just the texts or the number of concessions. We cannot observe what would have happened had the earlier Framework Agreement been accepted, nor assume that subsequent shocks would have been unchanged. A reactive response that preserves or widens feasible options contradicts necessary erosion. A series of such episodes may weaken H2 more broadly. Whether existing arrangements meet the task is the separate H3 question.

The practical question is whether the anticipated benefits of earlier engagement justify its costs compared with the incremental approach. A government could reasonably prefer responding to concrete problems rather than committing in advance. The report's proposal for constitutive participation is one alternative to evaluate, not the required next step for Switzerland.

### D.5 Is the framework too abstract?

Apply it to a specific episode and identify the information needed, evaluation criteria, causal model, competent decision-maker and feasible response. A data profile without authority is not a remedy. A failed vote does not prove all alternatives impossible. A proposed intervention needs independent evaluation and may fail. The framework is a question set, not a substitute for sector expertise.

For example, asymmetric observation becomes a comparison of specific records held by a bank and a relevant authority, their lawful access and whether they are used in a decision. The Faraday Cage metaphor becomes a comparison of actual diplomatic and regulatory information channels and associated duties. Veto topology becomes an inventory of legally binding approvals, consultation and political influence, together with feasible amendments. None can be inferred from a general impression of institutional complexity. These translations turn abstract concepts into evidence requests; they do not establish the diagnoses.

The proposals in Chapters 4 and 5 specify a Dashboard, willing cantons and an advisory assembly, with independent outcomes and review. A sector practitioner may find another remedy more appropriate. The task-relative framework should help distinguish why a specific remedy might work or fail and which authority can act. It is successful only insofar as those distinctions improve concrete inquiry, not because it supplies a vocabulary for every difficulty.

### D.6 Why change neutrality?

No abandonment or reinterpretation is required by the analysis. Compare actual information pathways, legal responsibilities, security and peace-related effects and costs within lawful neutrality and alternative arrangements. Neutrality is a legitimate choice; information access and alliance membership are distinct. Any change requires its own authorisation and public reasoning.

The original suggests uncoupling military non-alignment from information exchange and engagement in civilian standards. This is a possible reinterpretation, not a requirement to change the legal meaning of neutrality. Switzerland might remain outside an alliance while exchanging information under appropriate agreements, or contribute technical expertise to a standards process without accepting every resulting rule. Whether these options are lawful, accessible, useful and compatible with neutrality must be established separately. Some access can depend on obligations, and restrictions on sharing can be justified.

Geneva's international institutions provide a historical lead for considering neutrality as a platform for mediation, convening and cooperation. That possibility can be investigated alongside the benefits of existing neutrality. It is not a claim that Switzerland is uniquely able to perform these functions or that its current practices lack them. A more active civilian role might suit some tasks while a restrained posture suits others. Citizens can legitimately prefer the present interpretation; any departure requires transparent reasoning and the appropriate democratic and legal authority.
