## Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

### How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. Six models — Claude, DeepSeek, ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and Mistral — were each prompted to analyse Switzerland through the Governance as Engineering framework he had set out, drawing on their different training distributions and analytical tendencies. Their outputs were then combined into a single argument. He chose the topic, posed the questions and decided to publish the result, but wrote little of the text himself. The final voice is the models', not his.

Where the models converged (as they did on the core diagnosis of internal excellence combined with external boundary inadequacy), the convergence was given more weight. Where they diverged (as they did on the relative weight of moral critique versus architectural diagnosis), the divergence was used to sharpen the report's analytical distinctions. Convergence between language models is weaker evidence than it may appear, however: the models share much of their training data and can agree on the same mistakes.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about Swiss institutions, laws, referendums, events, figures and sources may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. It is offered in the spirit of collaborative sense-making, not authoritative pronouncement.

### Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them write from within Swiss governance. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not Swiss, does not live in Switzerland and has no lived experience of Swiss governance. He studied engineering physics at Uppsala University, in the systems engineering track (*systemteknik*), which included courses in control theory and signal processing, and has worked with mathematical optimisation at TOMLAB Optimization, his father's company; he has no formal training or professional experience in political science or public administration.

An outside lens can sometimes raise questions that insiders no longer ask, but it can just as easily miss what insiders know. The report does not claim to have the final word. It offers a lens that may or may not prove useful to those who hold positions within Swiss institutions and are searching for frameworks that make sense of what they are observing. Feedback, criticism and corrections are welcome.

### The Governance as Engineering series

This report is part of the Country Reports for Systemic Change, a component of the broader Governance as Engineering series, produced in 2024–2026. The whole series was produced in the same way, and none of it has been peer-reviewed. The formal papers in the series attempt to set out mathematical foundations, arguing that centralised governance destroys spatial information (Paper I), that fractal architectures are the stability-optimal solution (Paper II), that representation chains deeper than two or three layers become constitutionally unobservable (Paper III), that low-dimensional observation cannot stabilise high-variety resource systems (Paper IV), and that structural failure modes compound multiplicatively rather than additively (Paper V). Papers VI through VIII develop the variety gap framework, the capstone synthesis, and the parametric measurement methodology.

The country reports apply this framework to specific governance architectures: Germany (execution deficit), France (integration deficit), Sweden (feedback deficit), India (synchronisation deficit), the European Union (coherence deficit), the United Kingdom (control-delivery mismatch), Brazil (accumulation deficit), Russia (legibility deficit), the United States (integration deficit), Finland (throughput constraint), China (calibration deficit), Japan (continuity trap), Nigeria (substrate deficit), Israel (boundary deficit), and Spain (integrative closure deficit). Switzerland is the sixteenth country report. The series sees it as the first system examined that has largely solved the problems the other reports identify — and that encounters, as a consequence, a higher-order challenge that only near-optimal internal governance can reveal.

### Acknowledgment of Intellectual Debt

This report is indebted to the cybernetic tradition — W. Ross Ashby, Stafford Beer, Norbert Wiener — and to the contemporary scholars who have extended their work into governance design. The framework also draws on Elinor Ostrom's work on polycentric governance, Jay Forrester's systems dynamics tradition, Donella Meadows' leverage points analysis, and the developmental models of Clare Graves and Don Beck (Spiral Dynamics). The comparative governance diagnosis owes debts to scholars too numerous to list individually but whose work on Swiss federalism, direct democracy, consensus governance, and the EU-Switzerland relationship informs the argument throughout. The text was generated by AI models, which may have simplified or misrepresented this body of work; any such errors are not the fault of the scholars named.

The inspiration for this specific report came from a conversation with Björn Kenneth Holmström's father, Kenneth Holmström, whose recommendation to study control theory set the series in motion.
