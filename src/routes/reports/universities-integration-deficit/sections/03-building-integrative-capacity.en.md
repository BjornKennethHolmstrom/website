## 3. What Building Integrative Capacity Would Look Like

### 3.1 The Principle: Preserve Depth, Build Integration

Where existing arrangements demonstrably impede task-relevant integration, institutional changes may be useful. Marginal improvements, shared services, existing networks or additional resources may suffice; architectural redesign is not established as necessary. Compare them with the proposals below.

The central design principle is to preserve the disciplinary depth that makes integration valuable while enabling relevant knowledge exchange. Depth without integration may be insufficient for particular tasks; integration without depth may be superficial. Legitimate specialist work and valid disagreement need not be integrated. The aim is a complementary capacity, not a universal scholarly template.

The original invokes a multi-scale regulatory analogy: detailed disciplinary inquiry and slower synthesis across fields may be complementary. No universal single-scale impossibility follows. W3 shows that timely, valid exchange is task-dependent, and W8 that interventions can substitute or complement. Integration must preserve relevant evidence and uncertainty, including incompatible methods where no justified mapping exists.

The comparators in Appendix B provide historical leads about possible arrangements, not evidence of necessity or efficacy. Assess their design and outcomes in context before attempting transfer.

### 3.2 Interdisciplinary Institutes with Real Authority

Empowered interdisciplinary institutes are one possible way to create space for research across boundaries. Centres without tenure lines, independent budgets or durable funding may face constraints, but may also be effective at limited tasks. Compare existing centres, joint appointments and external networks.

The original proposes freestanding institutes with their own tenure lines, budgets, governance and agendas alongside departments, rather than centres dependent on departmental goodwill. This is a design option, requiring lawful delegation, independent oversight, clear interfaces and conflict handling. An institute may become a new silo or concentrate power; it must not be privileged merely because it is interdisciplinary.

The original cites the Santa Fe Institute as a model of institutional design for complex systems science: physicists, biologists, economists, computer scientists and anthropologists brought together through seminars, working groups and collaborative research programmes. Its organisation, evaluation criteria, resident and external faculty and resources are unverified historical leads. Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied the resident/external-faculty and evaluation questions. Apply the same institutional classification and evidence standards as to universities (§3.7, B.1).

The original emphasises that SFI is small, well-funded and unusual relative to a comprehensive university. Protected space and suitable assessment may support integrative research; the existence of a design does not establish benefit, necessity or transferability. Compare research quality, resources, missions, affiliations and legitimate alternatives. Do not infer superior speed or integration from the institutional label.

The original cites Olin College of Engineering as a founding-design model integrating design, entrepreneurship and humanities, team projects and faculty collaboration. It describes a small undergraduate institution, not a comprehensive research university. These particulars and outcomes are unverified; Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied leads on charter versus entering-class dates and whether Olin uses tenure. Compare it using the same criteria as §3.7 and B.2. Founding design may be helpful, but retrofit reforms may also work.

A university might establish three or four institutes on climate and sustainability, AI and society, health and inequality, or democracy and technology, each with its own tenure lines, budget, governance and curriculum. Joint appointments could link institutes to departments. Specify hiring, promotion, supervision, budget and appeal rights; dual-reporting conflicts, workload, shared infrastructure and research-integrity duties require independent scrutiny. Compare narrower mechanisms and protect foundational research, staff contracts, student progression and disciplinary resources. Measure actual coordination and quality, not mere existence of institutes.

### 3.3 Tenure Reform: From Disciplinary Publication to Broader Impact

Tenure and promotion reform is a candidate way to recognise otherwise undervalued work; its leverage depends on actual employment and incentive arrangements. Disciplinary standards and independent integrity remain binding. No claim that tenure change is universally necessary follows.

One candidate design would modify the tenure dossier to include multiple categories of scholarly contribution, each weighted according to the candidate's intellectual trajectory and the mission of their unit. Disciplinary publication would remain a category—the peer‑reviewed articles and monographs that demonstrate mastery of a specialised domain. But it would be joined by other categories: *integrative synthesis*—books, articles, or other works that assemble knowledge across disciplinary boundaries to illuminate complex phenomena; *collaborative research*—contributions to team‑based projects that span departments or institutions; *public engagement*—writing, speaking, or other activities that make scholarly knowledge accessible to non‑academic audiences; *teaching innovation*—the development of curricula, pedagogical methods, or educational materials that enhance the university's integrative capacity; and *institutional contribution*—service that strengthens the university's ability to pursue its mission, including leadership of interdisciplinary programmes, mentorship of junior faculty across disciplinary lines, and participation in governance that shapes the institution's intellectual direction.

Criteria may vary by unit and role. The six categories above are eligible contributions, not six mandatory requirements. A disciplinary scholar may legitimately meet applicable criteria without integrative work; a role explicitly concerned with integration can assess relevant competence. Avoid universal numerical weights and protect nonparticipation and academic freedom. New dossier categories add work for candidates and reviewers (IC-h/W5); measure net workload and transfers, compare simpler assessment routes and recognise invisible labour.

External referees can be drawn from the relevant discipline and, where the work requires it, interdisciplinary domains, with appropriately qualified and independent panel members. The original would make integrative contribution part of every positive tenure decision; that conflicts with its own rejection of a single template and is withdrawn. Predeclare task-relevant criteria, protect existing expectations and fair transitions, require recusal, reasons, accessibility, non-discrimination and appeal. Research validity is not compensable by public engagement or collaboration. Integrative contribution is eligible and unit-specific, never a universal condition of tenure or hiring.

Concerns about diluted standards may be legitimate. Evaluate proposed criteria and the effects of disciplinary and integrative panels symmetrically; negative findings, rigorous specialist contributions and disagreement remain valuable. Criticism is not proof of the Disciplinary Imperative. Assess administrative costs and the possible reverse selection effects under IC-af.

### 3.4 Transdisciplinary Funding Streams

Funding may constrain institutional reforms where relevant cross-field work is not adequately supported. Existing programmes, ordinary grants and external networks may already provide sufficient resources; compare them rather than presume disciplinary funding is deficient.

Candidate mechanisms include appropriately qualified interdisciplinary panels, criteria for valid synthesis and support for coordination. The original names ERC Synergy Grants, Wellcome Collaborative Awards and NSF Growing Convergence Research as historical leads. Verify actual scheme existence, dates, eligibility, scale and results; Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied specific leads on Wellcome restructuring, ERC team size/duration and current NSF status. Programme existence does not show efficacy or the inadequacy of other funding.

Two routes remain options: expansion of existing agencies' transdisciplinary programmes, or new mechanisms for research, coordination, communication and graduate training across fields. Compare their opportunity costs, discipline-specific capacity, eligibility, review competence, conflicts, independent quality and alternatives. Long-term funding can support continuity or lock in favoured projects; include milestones, transparent renewal and safe stopping.

The original contrasts three-to-five-year grants with seven-to-ten-year support for integrative research. These are historical candidate durations, not validated optimal periods. Some work requires sustained relationship-building and shared languages, while other projects can use shorter grants. Assess appropriate horizons, milestones, opportunity cost, graduate support and contractual continuity before selecting any design.

### 3.5 Curricular Integration

The curriculum is one interface between the university and students. It may offer disconnected courses or coherent disciplinary and interdisciplinary pathways. Determine actual learning objectives, accreditation, resources and student preferences before identifying a deficit.

Preserve disciplinary majors and investigate supplementary integrative structures. The four designs below are options rather than prescriptions: compare existing programmes, independent disciplinary mastery and synthesis quality, student choice, accessibility, workload, costs, accreditation and valid disagreement. No required agreement or inference of mental-health benefit follows.

**Problem‑based learning** is one such structure. Instead of organising the entire curriculum around disciplinary categories, a portion of the curriculum—perhaps one course per semester, perhaps a full year in the middle of the undergraduate programme—would be organised around complex, multidimensional problems. Students from different majors would work together in teams, bringing their disciplinary perspectives to bear on a problem for which several perspectives may be relevant. A course on climate resilience might include students from environmental science, economics, political science, engineering, and philosophy, each contributing their distinctive expertise while learning to communicate and collaborate across disciplinary boundaries. The course would be co‑taught by faculty from multiple departments, modelling integrative inquiry for their students.

**Team‑taught courses** that bring multiple disciplines to bear on a single issue are another integrative structure. A course on artificial intelligence and society, co‑taught by a computer scientist, a philosopher, a legal scholar, and a sociologist, would expose students to the technical, ethical, legal, and social dimensions of AI simultaneously. The faculty would model for their students the practice of integrative inquiry—disagreeing, translating, synthesising across the disciplinary boundaries that the curriculum otherwise respects.

**Integrated general education programmes** could supplement or replace distribution requirements where they function as a checklist of disconnected courses. Instead of requiring students to take two courses in natural science, two in social science, and two in humanities, an integrated programme would organise the general education curriculum around themes that span these domains—"the nature of evidence," "self and society," "the global and the local," "sustainability and justice." Each theme would be addressed through coordinated courses that bring multiple disciplines into dialogue, culminating in a synthesising seminar that helps students assemble what they have learned.

**Capstone projects** that require students to integrate their education would provide a culminating integrative experience. A student who has completed a disciplinary major and a set of integrative courses would, in their final year, undertake a project that draws on both—a research paper, a policy analysis, a creative work, a community‑based project—that demonstrates their capacity to apply specialised knowledge to a complex, multidimensional problem.

### 3.6 Digital Infrastructure for Knowledge Integration

Separate departmental websites, disciplinary databases and publication platforms may obstruct discovery or preserve useful specialised access. Test interoperability and existing search or directories before building new infrastructure. A map does not establish the quality of knowledge, permission to contact or access, or decision authority.

The design specification is a platform that maps the intellectual landscape of the university—the expertise of its faculty, the content of its courses, the focus of its research centres, the themes of its seminars and working groups—and makes that landscape navigable across disciplinary boundaries. For example, a faculty member in economics developing a research project on the social determinants of health might discover, through the platform, that the university has a medical anthropologist studying the same communities, a public health researcher with relevant data, and a historian working on the political economy of health policy. The platform would not replace the disciplinary organisation of knowledge. It would supplement it with a cross‑cutting map that might help discover relevant connections where disciplinary arrangements impede discovery, subject to independent validation of the mappings.

The platform would also serve students. A student interested in climate change could explore the courses, research groups, and faculty expertise relevant to that interest across the university—the atmospheric science, the environmental economics, the climate policy, the environmental ethics, the clean energy engineering—and identify candidate pathways through the curriculum, subject to actual prerequisites, availability and independent advice. The platform would function as a navigational aid for integrative education where existing arrangements demonstrably make relevant pathways difficult to find or pursue; existing advising and curricular arrangements may already suffice.

A candidate platform would use topical and disciplinary metadata, a navigable interface and sustained maintenance. Technical and organisational costs are unknown. Compare federated maps, existing directories and no new platform. Define minimum data, lawful access, opt-in where appropriate, protected material and IP, accessibility, security, contestable categorisation and correction. No individual rankings or employment surveillance. Test whether the platform actually improves discovery and validated exchange at acceptable burden.

### 3.7 The Shadow University as Bypass — and the Trap It Creates

The original lists AI research labs (DeepMind, OpenAI, Anthropic), independent institutes (Santa Fe, Perimeter and the Institute for Advanced Study), public writers and podcasters, and decentralised science networks as possible contributors to knowledge integration. Their staffing, funding, methods, audiences, openness and results are unverified leads; AI-assisted text is not validated synthesis. Research bodies can also have university affiliations. Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied Perimeter/Waterloo affiliations and IAS school structure as specific unverified leads.

The **Shadow University** is the original's conceptual label for these diverse arrangements. Classify each using ownership, funding, degree-granting, governance/accountability, openness and independent quality, applying identical criteria to universities. Categories can overlap: SFI and IAS are also favourable comparators, SFI and Olin are called hybrids. No category establishes expertise, public mission, academic freedom, peer review or speed. Claude's v0.1 audit review supplied non-university public research sectors (Max Planck, CNRS, Fraunhofer, national academies) as unverified rival frames. Compare actual functions, continuity, independent evidence and legitimate responsibilities.

The opportunity is potential competition or complementarity. External actors may offer useful synthesis, flexible collaboration or public communication; universities may offer depth, supervision, research infrastructure, professional formation and continuity. Both sets of claims require independent evidence. Students, funders and policymakers may use multiple institutions. An adequate distributed research ecosystem need not be a university monopoly.

The original's **bypass trap** hypothesises that external provision could reduce internal pressure to remedy relevant gaps. Its two-tier image of a fast private sector 'accountable to no one but its funders' and a fragmented debt-financed university sector is attributed rhetoric, not a classification or evidence of commitments. Compare substitution, complementarity, legitimate external ownership, diverse financing and public research bodies. Verify accountability and academic freedom institution by institution. A shift of functions may be legitimate and beneficial, rather than a trap.

Hybrid arrangements are candidate mechanisms linking specialised depth, integrative work, accountability and continuity. The original names SFI, Olin and the proposed internal institutes; classify Olin and the other examples by the same criteria above, allowing affiliations and overlapping functions. Their existence does not establish superior outcomes. Compare independent partnerships, university-based designs and public research bodies, with actual rights, budgets, openness, autonomy and valid evidence. No necessary institutional monopoly or compulsory Commons follows from the accepted R4 correction.

### 3.8 Faculty Development for Integrative Work

Faculty development may help researchers who choose or are assigned integrative roles acquire relevant skills in translation, collaboration and synthesis. The original's supply-side hypothesis is that training and incentives may constrain these skills. Test access, existing capability, costs and whether particular roles require them. Disciplinary expertise remains valuable in its own right.

**Sabbaticals for retooling** would enable mid‑career faculty to acquire the knowledge and skills needed for integrative work. A sociologist who wants to collaborate with climate scientists could spend a sabbatical year immersed in the atmospheric science literature, attending seminars, and developing the shared vocabulary that collaboration requires. An economist who wants to integrate psychological insights into her models could spend a sabbatical in a psychology department, learning the methods and concepts she needs. The sabbatical for retooling is a candidate option for voluntary cross-disciplinary learning; evaluate realistic expectations, access across ranks, funding and teaching replacement, without requiring participation.

**Seed funding for cross-departmental collaborations** could support seminars, shared graduate projects, pilot studies and grant development. The original proposes $10,000–$50,000 per project, at least two departments and preference for larger disciplinary distances. These amounts and eligibility are historical options. Disciplinary distance is an unvalidated proxy (W4); withdraw the preference unless task-specific validation and equity review establish relevance. Compare quality, opportunity costs, uneven access and existing small grants.

**Recognition for mentoring that spans disciplinary boundaries** would signal that the university values integrative work. Faculty who supervise doctoral students from other departments, who serve on dissertation committees outside their discipline, who mentor junior colleagues in interdisciplinary institutes—these contributions may be overlooked. Compare service credit, teaching relief or financial recognition against existing arrangements, counting transfers, supervision quality and costs.

**Hiring for integrative capacity** is appropriate only for roles where it is a legitimate task-specific requirement. The original's 'can talk outside her subfield' contrast risks reproducing the selection effect in §2.3 in reverse. Do not infer ability from communication style or apply universal collaboration criteria. Require validation, accessibility and non-discrimination review (including disability and language), qualified assessment and fair appeals. Legitimate specialists need not be integrators; neither scholarly type is superior. Test selection effects symmetrically across roles and career stages.

Institutes, tenure criteria, grants, curricular options, maps, sabbaticals, seed funds and mentoring may complement each other or substitute for one another (W8). Assess net time and resource costs, teaching replacements and workload transfers to precarious staff, graduate students and support workers. Protect choice, early-career opportunities and legitimate nonparticipation.

No claim follows that all reforms are needed or that any one is sufficient. Compare modest alternatives, effective existing arrangements and legitimate external ownership. Independent quality, research integrity, rights and educational outcomes constrain the design, not the volume of integrative activity.
