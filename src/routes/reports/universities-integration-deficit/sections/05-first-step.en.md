## 5. A Concrete First Step: The Integrative Capacity Audit and the Grand Challenge Pilot

### 5.1 The Logic of the First Step

The Integration Deficit may involve interacting mechanisms rather than a single policy failure. The original proposes two first steps intended to make relevant gaps investigable and test alternative arrangements: the Integrative Capacity Audit and Grand Challenge Pilot. Either may prove useful, unnecessary or harmful. Modest changes and existing reviews are comparators, not residual categories.

The first step is a bounded opportunity to investigate rather than an assumed catalyst. Its feasibility, cost, legitimate authority and expected information value must be established before adoption.

Section 2 proposes mechanisms involving departmental filters, tenure, peer review, funding and rankings. None establishes that fragmentation is the primary cause. The Audit may examine actual arrangements and identify either a task-relevant deficit or adequate performance; the Pilot tests a separate intervention hypothesis.

Together these options could improve evidence about the design of universities and the effects of changed arrangements. Neither is required to confirm the observational diagnosis, and neither automatically creates a legitimate case for further reform.

### 5.2 The Integrative Capacity Audit

The Integrative Capacity Audit is a candidate structured assessment of task-relative cross-disciplinary exchange. It may be conducted by an independent external panel, accreditor or consortium with authority and consent appropriate to the jurisdiction. Compare existing reviews and no additional audit. Specify tasks, missions, denominators, time horizons, selection, missingness, comparability and uncertainty; no presumption that greater integration is better. Use separate quantitative process indicators and qualitative assessment, not a public composite or ranking.

The Audit asks a specific set of diagnostic questions across the key dimensions of institutional architecture:

**Hiring and promotion architecture.** What proportion of hires in the past decade involved joint appointments? What criteria actually govern promotion and tenure, including disciplinary publication, synthesis, collaboration, public engagement and teaching innovation? Who reviews the work? Compare appropriate quality, mission, roles and career outcomes rather than assuming joint appointments or integration criteria are desirable for every role. Nonparticipation and legitimate specialisation remain protected.

**Collaboration network structure.** Examine coauthorship, co-teaching, co-supervision and joint grant applications. Describe clusters and sparse connections only within lawful, appropriate data frames. Test whether particular missing links impede a specified task, whether relevant exchange occurs through indirect or external paths, and whether the exchange is valid and timely (W3). No edge-count or individual ranking; absence of a recorded tie is not evidence of absence of collaboration.

**Funding flow analysis.** What share of research funding supports cross-departmental projects or mechanisms recognising collaboration? Do institutes control budgets or depend on departmental allocations? Distinguish actual funds, eligibility, authority, outputs and quality. High expenditure or cross-field allocation is not an independent outcome; compare opportunity costs, adequate ordinary grants and external networks.

**Curricular integration.** What proportion of undergraduate courses are team-taught, and programmes include integrative capstones or problem-based learning? Do general education requirements support coherent learning or are they disconnected distribution requirements? Compare actual learning and mastery, task and student preferences, accessibility, accreditation and independent student feedback. Separate satisfaction, wellbeing and learning. No compulsory disclosure or inferred universal demand.

**Interdisciplinary infrastructure.** Which institutes, centres or programmes have tenure lines, budgets and delegated governance? Which rely on departmental resources? Compare actual authority and task outcomes, not formal status. A limited centre may be adequate; an empowered institute may become a new silo. Investigate rights and conflicts, not supposed performativity inferred from structure alone.

**Administrative burden on integrative work.** What time is devoted to compliance, assessment, reporting and coordination? Does burden fall disproportionately on cross-departmental faculty or on others doing essential teaching, supervision and support? Compare by field and career stage, count necessary safeguards and net burden transfers (W5), and include the Audit's own workload against no additional collection.

The original proposes an **Integrative Capacity Score** aggregating these dimensions into a public institutional comparison. That unvalidated composite, league table and competition goal are withdrawn. W4 shows how normative weights reverse rankings without changing performance. Process measures such as joint appointments cannot establish research quality, synthesis validity or learning; independent integrity and rights constraints cannot be compensated for by a higher score. No pay, sanction or individual rating may be derived from these indicators.

Report proportionate aggregate findings, uncertainty and negative results where lawful, with confidential feedback, data minimisation, secure access, correction and rebuttal. Prevent identification from small groups and coerced disclosure of protected material. Independent appointment and funding safeguards, conflict review, plural expert and student voices and independent outcomes are required. Compare validity, gaming and net costs against existing reviews and no audit; no circular validation against scores or institutional uptake. A finding of no relevant deficit or a decision to stop can be legitimate.

### 5.3 The Grand Challenge Pilot

The Audit investigates possible deficits; the Pilot tests a distinct intervention hypothesis from the original: specified changes in incentives, resources and collaboration arrangements may improve independently judged integration quality and learning relative to suitable existing arrangements, without unacceptable loss of disciplinary depth, autonomy or other outcomes. Do not presume the institutional constraints have been overcome. This is not a test of H0–H5 or of the framework as a whole.

The Pilot is a proposed funded multi-year embedded experiment in a university or college. Select a question with independently justified importance, a demonstrated task need for cross-disciplinary exchange and fit with mission and community. The four historical topics are regional climate resilience, AI and society, health inequality in the surrounding city, and democratic governance in digital media. Establish affected-person participation and legal and ethical authorization before design; do not infer community consent or assume no single department could address the relevant task.

Participating faculty could receive teaching relief, predeclared recognition of appropriate research contributions in promotion and tenure, seed funds and graduate funding independent of departmental teaching assignments. Preserve disciplinary quality, academic freedom and nonparticipation. No retrospective criteria or universal integration requirement; account for teaching and supervision transferred to other workers, graduate students and nonparticipants (W5). Establish written commitments for student funding, supervision, completion and staff contracts even if the Pilot stops. Clarify internal reallocation, grants, philanthropic support and donor independence.

Shared offices, meeting spaces and laboratories are the original's co-location option, intended to support coffee conversations, whiteboard sessions and shared language. Physical proximity is not necessary by theorem. Compare distributed and hybrid arrangements, accessibility, travel and opportunity costs. Separate incentive/resource and coordination/space changes where feasible; W7 shows that even a randomised bundle cannot identify component effects. Predeclare units, treatment versions, assignment, inclusion, comparator, estimands, power/precision, pretrends where relevant, common ascertainment, confounding, spillovers and follow-up. A selected single site may provide feasibility evidence only.

Evaluation should include shared frameworks, synthetic analyses, communication to relevant audiences, independently assessed problem-related outcomes and persistence of collaboration, as in the original. Also measure disciplinary rigour, reproducibility, uncertainty and valid disagreement, student mastery, satisfaction and wellbeing separately, access and equity, career and workload effects, graduate progression, costs and public benefit where independently assessable. A plural independent panel with disciplinary and integrative expertise and appropriate affected-person voice should not let participants judge their own performance alone. No measure of consensus or publication count suffices. Require research ethics, consent, protected data and independent oversight.

The Pilot is temporary, not a permanent institute. Improved research or learning, satisfaction, professional development and sustainable networks might support a local design, subject to independent outcomes and cost. The original expects that successful results could shift the political burden of proof and motivate visits, adaptation and reform. This is an attributed political expectation, not a rule of evidence. Both current and proposed arrangements must face the same standards. Null, adverse or mixed results and justified nonadoption are valid outcomes.

The original calls this a safe-to-fail experiment. Limited duration does not contain harms automatically. Stop or redesign for rights, integrity, safety or accessibility failures, displaced teaching, inequitable burden, loss of supervision or infeasibility. Provide safe rollback and follow-up with enforceable funding, supervision, completion and contract protections. Compare narrower options and record actual costs, including those borne outside the Pilot. A dissolving network or failed intervention does not establish the diagnosis.

Embedding the Pilot within a university may allow use of existing depth and infrastructure and build a constituency for reform, as the original hopes. External collaboration or ownership may also be legitimate. Assess networks, rights and outcomes rather than count advocacy, visits or adoption as evidence. No claim of bypass avoidance or superiority follows from location.

### 5.4 Selection Criteria: Why These Two?

The original selects the two proposals by three criteria: relevance to a mechanism, feasibility and feedback. Each is a question to investigate, not a reason to assume the proposed designs are optimal.

First, assess whether each targets a specified relevant mechanism. The Audit might identify a gap or adequate performance; the Pilot compares outcomes under changed arrangements. Neither establishes the primacy of departmental fragmentation or the necessity of redesign.

Second, assess feasibility within existing lawful arrangements. An external review panel, internal reallocation, philanthropy or external grants may be options. Confirm actual decision rights, consent, ethics, costs, independence and protected commitments; no authority or reversibility follows from a small scale.

Third, assess the quality and use of feedback. Public aggregate findings may inform debate; Pilot results may motivate adaptations. Nonadoption may be justified and a successful local result does not shift the evidential burden. Report negative results and compare alternatives. Formation of a constituency is a political process measure, not evidence of quality.

### 5.5 How to Measure Success

Assess whether the interventions perform their specified tasks and whether their benefits justify their burdens. Opposition, delays or nonadoption may be legitimate. Separate process indicators, independent outcomes, rights constraints and political uptake.

For the Audit, retain completion within an agreed timeframe, appropriate public accessibility, use in institutional deliberation and changes in separate indicators as process observations. The original expects score-based competition; that goal and the score are withdrawn. Test independent relevance, validity, burden, confidentiality, correction and changes in independently assessed quality. No circular inference from an improved indicator, publication of findings or use in a strategic plan. An adequate institution may require no new reform.

For the Pilot, retain independent quality and problem-related impact where appropriate, faculty satisfaction and development, retention, student learning and post-graduation outcomes, and persistence of networks. Account for selection, confounding, costs and rights; satisfaction and retention are not interchangeable with research quality or wellbeing. Track adoption of modified tenure criteria, institutes or curricula and replication elsewhere as process measures only, not proof. No presumed benefit from expanded use.

The original asks whether the first step enables the second. This may be a political question, but it is not the ultimate research metric. A result that supports retaining existing arrangements or stopping the experiment is legitimate. Future reforms need independent authorization and evaluation; no burden-of-proof reversal follows from advocacy or a local result.

### 5.6 The Honest Acknowledgment

The original anticipates obstacles: an Audit could be absorbed into routine assessment, a Pilot become a showcase, a temporary network dissolve and results be ignored. These are possible outcomes requiring evaluation. The new arrangements may also generate new silos, metric distortion or workload. Independent challenge and reason-giving remain required.

No probability of these scenarios is established. H5 tests performative reform against genuine correction, legitimate constraints and justified nonadoption, including scrutiny of the Audit and Pilot themselves.

The alternatives include ordinary coordination, improved staffing and adequate existing arrangements, not only continued fragmentation. No reform is required simply to prevent a predicted spiral.

The Audit and Pilot are the original's wager that evidence could improve institutional decisions and perhaps change political incentives. The wager is not a prediction or validation of the framework. Local feasibility, successful adoption or failure does not prove H0–H5. Compare independently justified alternatives and allow legitimate stopping.

The wager may fail, or the evidence may show the proposed changes unnecessary. The original's concern about the subordination of integration to disciplinary architecture is retained as an attributed normative warning. Protect the inquiry, people and institutions that could test it.

The original closes its wager with the rhetorical invitation: “The audit can make the invisible visible. The pilot can make the impossible seem possible.” It asks whether the university will allow itself to see what the audit reveals, and to build what the pilot demonstrates. This is an attributed aspiration, not a guarantee of valid measurement, effectiveness or a reason to expand without independent evidence; justified nonadoption remains legitimate.
