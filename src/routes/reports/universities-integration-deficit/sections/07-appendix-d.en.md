## Appendix D: Anticipated Objections

### D.1 "This report romanticises interdisciplinarity. Disciplinary depth is the foundation of academic rigour, and integration without depth is superficiality."

The original recognises that disciplinary depth supports rigour and that integration without depth can be superficial. Supplementary arrangements may help when a task requires exchange; existing departments, networks or modest reforms may suffice. Research integrity and academic freedom are constraints, not compensable metrics. Valid disagreement and specialist inquiry are legitimate outcomes; criticism of integration is not evidence of closure.

### D.2 "The Shadow University is an overblown threat. Universities have survived every previous technological disruption and will survive AI."

The original invokes historical adaptation to printing, the internet and mass online education. These examples and causal accounts require verification.

Its response is that survival of earlier disruptions required adaptation: the printing press is presented as a shift from manuscript copying to the printed book, and the internet as a shift from physical libraries toward digital resources. The original argues that AI is “different in kind” because it may undermine the production of legible specialised text as a productivity proxy on which academic incentives partly depend. On that account the question is “not whether the university will survive but in what form”, including whether it retains a role as society’s critical conscience and epistemic infrastructure. These are attributed historical and prospective hypotheses, not verified causal accounts or a prediction of the future.

AI may alter incentives and tasks, but complementarity, successful adaptation and continued credential value are genuine possibilities. External institutes may compete or complement, and their actual quality, accountability and commitments must be evaluated symmetrically. No predicted collapse or universal threat follows.

### D.3 "The Integrative Capacity Audit would be just another metric, intensifying the very audit culture the report criticises."

This objection is substantive. The original calls the Audit qualitative and non-ranking here, but elsewhere proposes a public quantified composite and competition. That contradiction is acknowledged, not re-described away. The corrected candidate uses separate quantitative process indicators and qualitative assessment without composite, league table, sanctions or individual ratings. Its own burden, privacy, gaming, quality, independent validity and correction must be compared with no new audit and existing reviews. If costs or distortions outweigh benefits, nonadoption is legitimate.

### D.4 "The Grand Challenge Pilot would be a Potemkin village—a showcase of interdisciplinarity that leaves the rest of the university unchanged."

A Pilot may become a showcase, benefit selected participants or displace teaching and funding; it may also provide useful local evidence. Separate components or state unidentified effects, assess independent quality, learning, cost and transfers, protect progression and contracts, and require stopping and rollback. Short duration does not guarantee safety. Uptake or failure does not establish the observational diagnosis. A genuine adverse or null outcome may warrant stopping.

The original mitigation logic is that the Pilot might generate credible evidence and build a constituency of faculty and students who have experienced useful integration. In that account the Pilot “alone is not sufficient”: the Audit could help identify differences between the Pilot and other arrangements, alongside sustained advocacy for broader reforms. This is a conditional political pathway, not a requirement to expand or evidence of H0–H5. The Audit must establish its own validity and net value; participants may disagree or decline advocacy, and an adequate existing arrangement or an unsuccessful Pilot may warrant nonadoption.

### D.5 "This analysis is interesting, but it will never be implemented. The Performative Reform Trap is too strong."

The original expects powerful institutional resistance. The default is unknown. Independent evidence of effective existing mechanisms, durable reform or justified rejection weakens the stronger Performative Reform Trap hypothesis. The Audit and Pilot are optional probes, not necessarily feasible or necessary. Criticism of this report or GaE may identify genuine errors; no self-sealing explanation is permitted.

The original identifies three grounds for attempting change: first, the Audit and Pilot might be feasible within existing arrangements without comprehensive reform; second, competitive pressure from the Shadow University and AI-related changes to the credential economy might create leverage; third, the report is “a specification of what overcoming it would require”, not a prediction that the deficit will be overcome. Each is an attributed scenario conditional on resources, authority, evidence and safeguards. Complementarity, effective adaptation and continuing credential value may instead reduce the need for these reforms; feasible first steps need not lead to broader change.
