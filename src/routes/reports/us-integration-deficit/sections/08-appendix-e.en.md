## Appendix E: About this report and how it was made

### How it was made

This report was generated by AI language models (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek) from prompts by Björn Kenneth Holmström. Several models were prompted in parallel to approach the United States' situation from different strategic angles—institutional architecture, political economy, cultural dynamics, and comparative governance—and to apply the systems-thinking framework he had set out. He chose the topic, posed the questions and decided to publish the result, but wrote little of the text himself.

The report has not been systematically fact-checked. Statements about American institutions, laws, court decisions, events, figures and sources may be inaccurate or invented, and should be verified against primary sources before being relied on or cited. Like any work of synthesis, it is provisional. It makes no claim to finality. It claims only that the lens it offers merits testing against reality—and that the testing, in the end, is what matters most.

### Perspective and limits

Neither the models nor the person who prompted them write from within the United States' institutional core. Björn Kenneth Holmström is not American, does not live in the United States and has no formal training or professional experience in American constitutional law, public administration or political science. He studied engineering physics at Uppsala University, in the systems engineering track (*systemteknik*), which included courses in control theory and signal processing, and has worked with mathematical optimisation at TOMLAB Optimization, his father's company. The framework applied here draws on complexity science, developmental psychology (Spiral Dynamics), governance theory, and control‑theoretic approaches to institutional design.

That distance limits access to the granular, day‑to‑day texture of American policy‑making—the unwritten norms, the informal power structures, the lived reality that no formal framework can capture. An outside lens can sometimes raise questions that insiders have learned not to hear, but it can just as easily miss what insiders know. The report does not claim insider knowledge. It offers a lens that may or may not prove useful to those who hold institutional positions and are searching for frameworks that make sense of what they are experiencing. Feedback, criticism, and corrections are welcome.

The report refers to related material on this website—the Global Governance Frameworks, the Governance as Engineering working papers and the EU Subsidiarity Protocol—which were produced in the same way.

### The Country Reports Series

This report is the ninth in a series of Country Reports for Systemic Change, all produced in the same way. The first examined Germany through the lens of an execution deficit. The second examined France through the lens of an integration deficit. The third examined Sweden through the lens of a feedback deficit. The fourth examined India through the lens of a synchronisation deficit. The fifth examined the European Union through the lens of a coherence deficit. The sixth examined the United Kingdom through the lens of a control‑delivery deficit. The seventh examined Brazil through the lens of an accumulation deficit. The eighth examined Russia through the lens of a power‑vertical deficit. Together, the nine reports propose a global diagnostic framework covering a wide range of adaptive capacity failures—nine different ways that governance architectures may fail under complexity, unified by the common structural pressure of the subsidiarity deficit. The series does not claim to be complete. It is meant as a foundation on which further analysis, deeper testing, and better design can be built.
