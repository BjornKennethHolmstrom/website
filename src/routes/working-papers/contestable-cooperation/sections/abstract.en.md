### Abstract

A cooperative arrangement can provide valuable shared goods while denying affected people meaningful control over its terms. Conversely, contestation may interrupt services without necessarily diminishing an arrangement's prospects for legitimate cooperation. This paper argues that assessment must distinguish **operational performance** (what coordination delivers), **corrective capacity** (whether affected people can safely surface problems, challenge their interpretation, obtain accountable decisions and change governing terms), and **normative acceptability** (whether those terms meet defensible standards of rights and non-domination). These properties interact, but none can be inferred from another. Building on established work in peace research, intergroup contact, republican political theory and conflict-sensitive assessment, the paper develops a second claim: **evaluation is itself an intervention**. Metrics and judgments can reward suppression of grievances as readily as their resolution, making an assessment's framing, incentives and contestability part of the object of inquiry. To operationalize these ideas, the paper proposes a provisional *Contestability Module* to extend, rather than replace, competent existing evaluation practice. It distinguishes directional power from outcomes and normative judgments, protects disagreement without treating allegations as automatically established, and tests whether institutions can revise not only decisions but also their **interpretations of what the problem is**. A bounded comparison with governance-engineering working papers motivates this interpretive requirement without claiming that a mathematical model proves it occurs in real institutions. A difficult hypothetical public-service case illustrates the trade-offs; a comparative evaluation design specifies how the module's claimed value could be rejected. The paper proposes no universal causal law, no aggregate peace score and no requirement that emotional reconciliation precede cooperation.

**Keywords:** intergroup cooperation; systemic optimization; correctability; non-domination; contestability; evaluation reactivity; conflict sensitivity; institutional adaptation
