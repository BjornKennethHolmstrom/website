## 1. The evaluation problem: two ways to get cooperation wrong

Two communities share a water system. It supplies safe water on schedule, its finances balance, and visible political conflict is limited. A performance review may correctly say that operational coordination is effective. It cannot conclude from those facts alone that the communities have equal influence over tariffs, that dissent is safe, or that people can challenge the rules without risking access to water or employment. Functioning can be real while the relationship governing it remains unequal.

Now suppose residents organize a boycott over tariff rules and service is interrupted. An assessment centered on uninterrupted delivery or low protest counts may register deterioration. That, too, may be an accurate observation at the level of immediate service delivery. Yet the political implication is indeterminate: the boycott could expose an injustice previously suppressed, create leverage for a better agreement, or instead impose disproportionate costs on vulnerable people without improving accountability. **Disruption cannot by itself tell us whether a cooperative system is becoming more or less defensible.**

These examples capture two errors. A **false success** occurs when collaboration, attitudinal warmth or public calm is promoted to proof of consent, legitimacy or justice. A **false failure** occurs when legitimate challenge is counted as dysfunction merely because it disturbs a current arrangement. The solution is not to romanticize opposition or to disparage functioning services. It is to specify the property being judged, establish what evidence supports that judgment, and ask who can challenge the system—including the standards by which it is assessed.

This is not a new discovery that cooperation can precede reconciliation. Contact theory, peace research, instrumental reconciliation and agonistic political thought have long examined practical accommodation amid disagreement [1–3]. Nor is it new that peace can be unjust. The narrower contribution attempted here is an **evaluation argument**: an institution's ability to produce valuable coordination and its ability to correct its governing terms are distinct, potentially conflicting capacities; because evaluation changes actors' incentives, neither capacity should be judged from an uncontested performance indicator alone.

The discussion applies most directly to continuing institutional arrangements, such as shared services, municipal partnerships, workplaces, schools and power-sharing institutions. In post-atrocity settings, its insistence that emotional harmony is not a prerequisite for immediate joint activity must never be read as a waiver of acknowledgment, restitution, accountability or rights. Reconciliation has multiple meanings; this paper does not seek to settle them. Its larger motivating question is how people can continue acting together across real differences **without requiring disagreement to disappear or turning the ability to dissent into a threat to the whole relationship**.
