## 2. Three properties of a cooperative arrangement

The organizing distinction is between **what an arrangement does**, **whether it can correct how it does it**, and **whether its terms are normatively acceptable**. These are not three ingredients of a single validated index. They are separate questions to which different evidence and standards apply.

### 2.1 Operational performance

Operational performance concerns the delivery of the intended goods: continuity, reliability, safety, accessibility, effectiveness, cost, and the distribution of these outcomes. In a shared water utility, it includes safe supply, maintenance and affordability; in an intergroup program it might include actual costly cooperation across group boundaries, rather than merely a stated willingness to cooperate. The relevant unit and task must be named. Performance should be measured at the levels of both the arrangement and its affected participants, with distributions visible rather than hidden in an average.

Good operational performance is valuable. An institution that cannot supply essential services may worsen distrust and expose people to serious harm. The argument of this paper does not treat service delivery as morally trivial, nor require withholding cooperation until every historical grievance is resolved. It rejects only the additional inference that delivery establishes political consent, equal standing or the capacity to alter unfair arrangements.

### 2.2 Corrective capacity

**Corrective capacity** is the practical ability of affected people to identify problems, communicate and challenge how those problems are understood, obtain accountable decisions, and secure warranted changes to an arrangement's rules or implementation without unacceptable retaliation. It is more than the formal existence of a complaints channel. It includes accessible information, meaningful standing, reasonably safe expression, contestable interpretation, independent review, responsiveness, enforceable remedies and protection from unilateral rollback. Its quality can be investigated by following what happens to concrete grievances and how decisions change, not just by counting forms submitted or hearings held.

A useful analytical decomposition is a **correction chain**: **expression** (can the concern safely become available?), **interpretation** (can different accounts of its meaning be heard and challenged?), **adjudication** (can competing accounts receive an accountable, reviewable decision?), and **implementation and learning** (can justified decisions alter practice, with feedback on whether the remedy worked?). These stages are not a claim that all institutions follow one linear workflow: decisions may iterate, bypass stages in emergencies, or run through multiple parallel channels. The point is diagnostic. A system can receive every complaint yet classify objections to unequal decision authority as mere misunderstanding of technical constraints. It can correctly interpret a complaint but lack authority to act. It can order a remedy but leave the underlying rule unchanged. **Having a channel to speak is not equivalent to having a channel through which the institution's framing of the complaint can be challenged.**

Corrective capacity is not an unlimited right to stop every decision, an obligation to agree with each complaint, or a guarantee of perpetual re-negotiation. Competing claimants also have rights; infrastructure must be maintained; some harmful tactics may legitimately be restricted. A workable system needs procedures that can hear contestation, distinguish competing claims, reach timely decisions, enforce outcomes, and preserve essential coordination where possible. **Correction is a capacity to make justified change possible, not a requirement that every challenge succeeds.**

It also has a temporal dimension. A routine service may be judged over weeks while structural dependency accumulates over years. An annual audit cannot certify long-term correctability from a single quiet period. Historical rule changes, unresolved grievances, appeal outcomes, reprisals, changing entry and exit costs, and future review triggers all matter. Evidence about *interpretation* must include some record of how decision-makers classified the original concern and whether a substantively different characterization was permitted to affect the review; it should not rest on an assessor's speculative diagnosis of anyone's private motives.

### 2.3 Technical and political correctability

**Technical correctability** concerns whether an institution can detect errors, revise methods or governing assumptions, implement changes, and learn from their consequences. **Political correctability** concerns whether people subject to its decisions can meaningfully challenge its problem definitions, objectives, and governing rules, and influence accountable reconsideration. Neither guarantees the other: an institution may adapt skillfully while keeping its agenda closed to those it governs; meaningful participation, in turn, does not by itself supply the resources or expertise needed to implement justified decisions.

Argyris and Schön's distinction between *single-loop* and *double-loop* organizational learning [32] clarifies one side of this boundary. Single-loop learning corrects action within prevailing governing norms; double-loop learning questions or modifies the norms themselves. Yet even effective double-loop learning can remain **politically closed** if only incumbents may initiate, frame, or decide which governing assumptions to revise. The additional requirement concerns legitimate standing and consequential influence over that process, not simply a more sophisticated technical learning mechanism.

Fricker's account of *epistemic injustice* [33] illuminates failures at the **interpretation** link of the correction chain. A person's report can receive unjustly diminished credibility because of prejudice (*testimonial injustice*), or an important experience can be obscured by unfair gaps in shared interpretive resources (*hermeneutical injustice*). Not every disputed classification is unjust: evidence and competing claims still require assessment. The procedural test is whether affected people can challenge the **classification of their grievance**, receive reasons and independent review, and establish whether subsequent adjudication and implementation address the concern they raised. **Expression without contestable interpretation can leave a technically responsive complaints system politically closed.**

### 2.4 Normative acceptability

Normative acceptability asks whether the *terms* of cooperation satisfy a declared and defended standard. This paper uses a provisional specification: basic rights and safety, meaningful remedy, and republican **non-domination**, understood as protection against another actor's unchecked capacity for arbitrary interference [13,14]. This standard is an argued normative choice, not a finding derivable from public-goods games or surveys. Rival specifications—for example, outcome-prioritizing humanitarianism, deliberative legitimacy or more agonistic accounts—must be describable and their implications discussable.

The distinction between interference and domination matters. An authority may never have cut off water to dissenters but may possess unconstrained power to do so. Under a non-domination standard, the **capacity** for arbitrary deprivation is relevant even without an observed exercise of that power [13]. Conversely, a rule-governed restriction with independent review need not be dominating merely because it interferes with a chosen action. That is why silence, lack of recorded sanctions and absence of formal complaints cannot be treated as proof of freedom or consent. Work on preference falsification and public versus hidden transcripts offers independent reasons for caution about what people say under unequal power [18,19].

This point is especially important inside groups. 'Community A' and 'Community B' are not natural homogeneous agents. Internal factions, women, minorities, newcomers and nonparticipants may face subordination by elites who claim to speak on the group's behalf. Identifying group boundaries, representatives and excluded people is an **object of evaluation**, not a neutral preliminary assumption.

### 2.5 Keep the judgments—and their evidence—separate

A strong evidence claim about service reliability does not settle legitimacy; a convincing rights concern does not establish that every aspect of service delivery failed. For a given arrangement, the proposed record therefore distinguishes substantive assessments from the *status of their evidence*:

| Property assessed | Example finding | What remains a separate question |
|---|---|---|
| Operational performance | Reliable, affordable water delivery over a specified period | Who can change tariffs or allocation rules? |
| Corrective capacity | Appeals exist, but no independent power to enforce remedies is documented | What happens when a contested decision is challenged? |
| Normative acceptability | One party retains unchecked discretion to withdraw essential service | Has this power been exercised, and how do affected parties experience it? |
| **Evidence status, attached to each claim** | Supported; provisional; contested; insufficient | A confidence label is **not** a moral verdict |

Normative findings may be recorded as *meets the specified threshold*, *fails it*, *indeterminate*, or *transitionally deficient under stated conditions*, with reasons and competing judgments preserved. These are **not substitutes for the underlying three property assessments**. A provisional overall recommendation—for instance, "continue emergency delivery, but transfer tariff review to an independent body"—must be transparently constructed from the separate findings rather than obtained by adding them into a score.

A second distinction concerns the unit of inference. A group-average association cannot establish every individual's experience. Direction matters: one group can cooperate with another while receiving less consideration in return; one party can fear the other without reciprocal fear. Behavioral cooperation, reported intention, threat perception, documented danger, discretionary power and normative rights are different sorts of evidence. They should not be drawn as if they were interchangeable measurements of a single 'relationship quality' variable.
