## 3. Established insights, evidence boundaries and the specific gap

### 3.1 The literature already warns against equating calm with peace

Galtung's distinction between negative and positive peace directs attention beyond an absence of overt violence to structural conditions [1]. Reconciliation literature distinguishes instrumental cooperation from socio-emotional processes [2], and political accounts allow disagreement to remain part of legitimate common life [3]. The intergroup contact literature has likewise debated whether prejudice reduction is an adequate target for structural change. Saguy and colleagues' *irony of harmony* work suggests that under some conditions improved intergroup contact can reduce disadvantaged groups' willingness to challenge inequality [6]. These are antecedents, not discoveries of this paper.

Conflict-sensitive assessment also has mature approaches. The Do No Harm (DNH) framework already considers dividers, connectors, resource transfers, implicit messages and the effects of interventions on conflict dynamics [7]. Peace-and-conflict impact assessment (PCIA) and bottom-up peace indicators likewise contest narrow, imposed success measures [8,20]. A competent assessor using these practices may already examine who benefits, who is excluded and whether grievances can surface. **The proposed addition therefore cannot claim that power, participation or unintended effects were previously ignored.** Its potential difference lies in making specific inferential and contestation requirements explicit, testable and adaptable to routine institutional evaluation.

### 3.2 Behavior and attitudes need different measurements

Field experiments help show why outcomes must be stated concretely. Scacco and Warren's contact intervention in Nigeria examined discrimination and prejudice separately, finding changes in some discriminatory behavior without correspondingly broad changes in reported prejudice [4]. Mousa's randomized mixed-team soccer intervention in post-ISIS Iraq improved certain behavioral interactions with Muslim teammates, while wider attitudinal and out-of-setting changes were limited or inconsistent [5]. Neither finding licenses the inference that a positive game outcome demonstrates just institutional design. Both make it difficult to justify using an attitude scale as a stand-in for all cooperative outcomes.

Schäfer and colleagues' 2026 survey across 12 European countries found associations between perceived inclusivity norms and stated willingness to cooperate across strongly disapproved opinion differences [9]. A B0 secondary analysis of the deposited responses found these associations positive across several weighting and covariate specifications [B0a]. The result concerns self-reports in a cross-sectional survey, not observed costly cooperation or causal buffering under existential threat. Preserving that distinction is more useful than inflating a correlational result into a universal rule.

### 3.3 Cooperation can coexist with threats and unequal relationships

Schaub's lab-in-the-field research in six Georgian villages recorded public-goods cooperation as well as perceived-threat and aggressive choices in a separate *threat game* [10]. The study reports heightened ingroup-oriented and aggressive behavior in communities more exposed to ethnic outsiders, yet also substantial outgroup cooperation. This is compatible with strategic tension management: offering cooperation to keep mutually beneficial exchange open while maintaining hostile or defensive postures. It is **not** evidence that cooperation establishes reconciliation, nor that threat promotes just cooperation. The B0 reanalysis found a negative within-village individual association between outgroup cooperation and one threat-game measure, but that result is exploratory; six villages cannot support strong exposure-level causal inference [B0b].

Chang and Peisakhin's Lebanon experiment distinguished forms of intersectarian cooperation and showed that a civic-identity expert appeal improved some unconditional choices without neutralizing sectarian clientelistic incentives [11]. A shared identity can be a useful mechanism without being universally necessary. The kind of cooperation, and the incentives around it, matter. Raffoul's comparative analysis of Burundi and Northern Ireland similarly emphasizes the practice of power sharing—interpretation of rules, elite socialization and representation—not merely formal institutional architecture [12]. Neither study identifies an institutional recipe sufficient for legitimate cooperation.

This paper carries one bounded inference from the evidence: **an observed cooperative outcome underdetermines the arrangement's power relations, legitimacy and capacity for correction**. Much existing scholarship says similar things. The specific task here is to articulate what an evaluation must then keep distinct, how evaluation may worsen those errors, and how a small procedural supplement might be tested against competent alternatives.

### 3.4 Match claims to the design that can support them

The B0 research distinguished three claims relevant to cooperation amid perceived threat: **coexistence** in a specified time and unit; **persistence** across repeated observations; and **institutional buffering**, a causal claim that an institutional feature changes how threat affects behavior. A cross-section may support coexistence, repeated measures are required for persistence, and buffering requires credible identifying variation rather than mere statistical interaction. None of these constructs guarantees justice, and no dataset examined in B0 directly identifies all the proposed dynamic relations. Friehs and colleagues' methodological comparison of longitudinal models reinforces the danger of treating panel associations as causal effects without identifying assumptions [21].

This three-way distinction remains an **inference discipline**, not the main theory of Paper 1. Detailed recovery, tipping and hysteresis hypotheses belong to later work if suitable measurement and identification become available.

### 3.5 A bounded connection to governance engineering and human interpretation

A related set of *Governance as Engineering* (GaE) working papers offers conceptual tools for this evaluation problem, but **not independent empirical confirmation**. *Requisite Alignment* (XXVII) demonstrates within an exactly solved control problem that an informative signal can have reduced decision value when it resolves a distinction misaligned with the action-relevant target [23]. For a public-service evaluation, this motivates the question whether an accurate delivery indicator is aligned with a decision about **whose rules govern delivery**. The formal model proves its result for its specified controller; applying it to institutions is an analogy and design hypothesis, not a theorem about municipalities.

*The Observer You Cannot Afford to Excite* (XXIV) explores how observation, access to informative alternatives and adaptive performance interact in a minimal computational system [24]. *When to Stop Learning, and When to Stop* (XXI) distinguishes improved internal learning from effective coupling and argues against treating institutional persistence as an unconditional goal [25]. These are useful prompts for asking whether an evaluation preserves opportunities to detect alternative arrangements, but their model-dependent results cannot establish an empirical suppression loop in any particular society.

The *Self* trilogy extends the question to human observers. *Self I* treats an individual's value commitments as attentional filters; *Self II* explores the reflexive possibility that attending to a state helps change it; *Self III: The Operator* proposes that interpretive blind spots at human decision nodes may shape the institutional view of the governed [26–28]. **Our narrower inference** is conditional: if officials have decisive interpretive control over a complaint and there is no effective route around that interpretation, an otherwise adequate complaints procedure can fail at the interpretation stage. We do not infer from the trilogy that a person's introspective ability is measurable as a general observability rank, that self-awareness is necessary for recognizing another person's grievance, or that better personal development alone secures rights. These claims would need independent evidence.

The Country Reports for Sweden, Japan and Russia propose contrasting diagnoses of competent routine delivery, continuity-biased adaptation and dangerous feedback channels [29–31]. Their qualitative diagnoses illustrate **questions to investigate**—not validated comparative cases, independent replications or proof of the present thesis. Because they are author-curated and, in parts, AI-generated interpretive reports with unverified claims, the present argument does not rest on their country-level accuracy. It rests on the distinction between what can be performed, what can be recognized and revised, and who is entitled to challenge the interpretation.
