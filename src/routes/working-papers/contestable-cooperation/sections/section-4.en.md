## 4. The systemic problem: evaluation changes what it measures

Suppose an authority is rewarded for uninterrupted service and falling protest counts. A governance dashboard assigns a favorable grade when both indicators improve. If the authority can influence how grievances are categorized, who is permitted to demonstrate or how publicly complaints are recorded, it may be rewarded for making dissent less visible rather than solving the underlying dispute. Reduced protest can strengthen funding, prestige or discretionary authority, which in turn supports the same behavior. This is a *candidate reinforcing loop*: performance incentives → reduced visibility of grievances → reported success → stronger incumbent incentives. It is an application of a broader literature on the reactivity of evaluation, not a newly established empirical law [16,17].

The mechanism requires several links, which can be investigated rather than assumed: a measurable reward or sanction attached to the metric; meaningful discretion over recording or suppressing disagreement; an observable change in complaint access or sanction practices; persistence of unaddressed grievances; and a feedback channel from favorable ratings to resources or authority. Evidence against the suppression account might include independent grievance records, genuinely improved conditions, protected opportunities for objection, and documented resolution of the underlying complaints. Lower protest can represent a successful settlement as well as coercive quiet.

A possible **correction pathway** runs differently: accessible information and protected challenge reveal defects; independent review distinguishes justified from unjustified claims; enforceable remedies alter rules or their implementation; participants can renew operational cooperation on more defensible terms. This pathway can fail through captured reviewers, strategic obstruction, unfunded remedies or impossible demands. What matters is that the arrangement has a capacity to process challenge, **not** that complaint rates rise or every challenge wins.

Two further failures can arise **before** a grievance reaches an outcome metric. First, the indicator may be accurate but **irrelevant to the disputed decision**: uninterrupted supply says little about who can unilaterally change tariffs or sanction objectors. Second, officials may interpret an objection through a frame that precludes the relevant political question: a request to share authority is recoded as a failure of public understanding, or justified technical uncertainty is dismissed as malicious obstruction. These are different errors. The first concerns the relation of evidence to the decision target; the second concerns an institution's **contestable classification** of what the grievance is about. Different reviewers might legitimately reach different conclusions. What matters is whether the competing descriptions can be heard, evidenced and resolved without one actor acquiring an unreviewable monopoly over the categories.

An interpretive bottleneck is testable in principle. Case records can compare an initial complaint, the authority's recorded classification, an independent account, the reasons offered for accepting or rejecting each, and any change in remedies. Rival explanations include accurate technical triage, genuine resource scarcity, strategic misrepresentation by claimants, and disagreement about values rather than a hidden perceptual failure. It would be improper to infer an official's psychology merely from a policy decision. **The institutional safeguard is plural and accountable interpretive access, not a requirement that every official be unusually insightful.**

The paths may have different time constants. Daily water delivery, annual performance ratings and decades-long entrenchment of political privilege do not evolve on the same schedule. A short-lived improvement in delivery or a prolonged absence of protest cannot alone certify that corrective capacity is improving. Historical changes in discretion, exclusion, remedies and representation should be examined against a declared observation horizon.

An additional reflexive problem follows: **an audit can be gamed in the same way as the dashboard it replaces**. Authorities may create token grievance channels that resolve nothing; funders may commission the review to legitimate a preferred outcome; challengers may fabricate or strategically package claims; assessors may define group boundaries in ways that privilege local elites. Counting the number of grievance offices or completed audits could then become a new proxy error. Verification must follow complaint trajectories and institutional changes, compare sources independently where safe, and record who commissioned the assessment, controlled evidence and could prevent publication. Silence can be informative only with extreme caution; a structurally unchecked power is observable even where fearful people cannot testify against it.

This is the systemic contribution Paper 1 attempts: **the choice of evaluative objective, the allocation of framing power and the possibility of correcting the evaluative objective jointly shape the system being optimized**. It does not require a fitted differential equation to be a coherent design problem. It does require testable links, rival explanations and safeguards against making the evaluator a new source of domination.
