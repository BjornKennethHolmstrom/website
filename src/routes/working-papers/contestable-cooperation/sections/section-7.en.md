## 7. A difficult worked case: a valuable service on unequal terms

Consider the following **hypothetical case**, constructed to expose competing considerations rather than represent a real community. Two municipalities depend on a joint water utility. Municipality A owns much of the technical infrastructure, contributes most of the engineering expertise, guarantees emergency financing and can stabilize service after severe breakdowns. Municipality B contains more low-income households, has fewer alternative water sources and would be disproportionately harmed by supply interruptions. A's administrators hold a majority of board votes and unilateral emergency powers; B has seats but no independent mechanism for contesting tariff decisions. Both populations receive safe water at prices below the cost of maintaining separate systems. A's technicians have acted competently and no arbitrary disconnection of B households has been documented.

Residents of B now demand greater influence over pricing. A argues that technical responsibility and financial liability justify its control. B argues that the absence of unilateral punishment so far does not make its dependence politically acceptable. A boycott would endanger financially vulnerable households; immediate dissolution of the joint utility might threaten basic safety. A compromise may therefore be necessary even if the present voting arrangement is normatively deficient.

Suppose B's residents submit a petition stating that the central issue is **who has the right to decide tariffs**, not merely the level of the tariff. A's administrator classifies the petition as a request for a technical explanation of infrastructure costs and marks it resolved after publishing an accessible, accurate accounting of the expenses. This could be a good-faith response to a *different* question, not proof of malicious intent or of a defective cost estimate. But if residents cannot contest the classification, the official grievance channel may report success while leaving the governing-power grievance untouched. An independent reviewer should test both interpretations: whether the proposed rule change overlooks genuine engineering and financing obligations, and whether those obligations have been improperly treated as reasons to deny any political review. The decisive evidence concerns **complaint wording, the record of classification, independent reasons and reviewable authority**—not a speculative diagnosis of the administrator's psychology.

A narrow performance review may correctly identify that the utility works and should continue. A competent conflict-sensitive assessment may already flag the power imbalance, relative risks and divisive tariff politics. The CM would add a **required separation of conclusions** rather than a predetermined answer:

- **Operational performance:** strong on continuity, but check who bears future maintenance costs, risks and affordability burdens. Continued emergency supply may be an immediate priority.
- **Corrective capacity:** weak unless B can contest how its complaint is classified and obtain binding, independently reviewable decisions about tariffs and emergency powers. The key structural questions are whether A can arbitrarily punish challenge, indefinitely block revision, **or define a governance grievance out of existence as a technical information request**—not merely whether disconnection has occurred.
- **Normative acceptability:** potentially below the declared non-domination threshold; the conclusion depends on the scope of A's discretion, legal safeguards, necessity of emergency powers and available remedies. Technical expertise alone does not imply unbounded governing entitlement.
- **Evidence status:** observed delivery may be supported, while claims about residents' satisfaction, strategic motivations or retaliation risk may be contested or insufficient. A's own account and B's complaints must both be examined without requiring dangerous testimony.

A provisional decision might be to **maintain essential operations while imposing immediate safeguards on discretionary disconnections and retaliation**, establish independent tariff and complaint-classification review with accessible appeal, require a transparent examination of technical costs *and* governing authority, and adopt enforceable milestones for revising voting and emergency powers. Emergency technical command could continue under reviewable, narrowly defined conditions; a permanent veto over political revision would not be justified by the mere presence of expertise. Whether any particular timetable is adequate would depend on evidence about costs, safety, and legal obligations, not on a universal deadline invented by the audit.

The arrangement could thus be **operationally effective, normatively deficient, and provisionally correctable**. These findings are not logically contradictory. The same case could also support a different conclusion under better evidence—for example, if B actually possesses independent enforceable safeguards not visible in the initial description, or if the proposed power transfer jeopardizes safe supply and no feasible alternative exists. The assessment must state what facts would change its recommendation.

This example cannot prove the module adds value. A trained DNH/PCIA practitioner might reach an equally nuanced decision. Its purpose is to show what the manuscript's main distinction entails under a genuine trade-off: **do not endanger valuable cooperation unnecessarily, but do not purchase operational continuity by making correction impossible**.
