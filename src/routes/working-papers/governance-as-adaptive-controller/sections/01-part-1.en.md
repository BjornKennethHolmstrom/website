# Part I — The learning limit

## 1.1 A reform that worked until it did not

The original motivating example was the changing role of local experimentation in China's reform era. It raises a question, not a verified causal diagnosis: how can a previously successful policy architecture recognize when the evidence on which its success depended no longer applies? Part V retains this and the other country illustrations as research questions.

## 1.2 The pattern across the series

A regulator may be accurate about yesterday's environment, poorly informed about tomorrow's and nonetheless successful at today's task. The corrected Paper VI distinguishes evaluation, observation, modelling, decision and actuation. A learning deficit can appear at several of these interfaces; it does not follow merely from a narrow objective function.

## 1.3 The structural question

Suppose the response to a proposed intervention is uncertain. The relevant issue is whether admissible policies and observation histories distinguish states or parameters that require different actions, at tolerable cost. Passive observation, endogenous experiments, external variation and prior knowledge are all possible sources. Some interventions expose information; others destroy it or are too harmful to attempt. There is no general theorem that more variability improves a system or that insufficient deliberate exploration entails collapse.

## 1.4 The three capacities

Sensing (X), learning (XIV) and execution (IX) form a design vocabulary. Learning may update an estimate, select between models or generate a new hypothesis; execution may be blocked even when the decision is clear. The three capacities interact rather than forming a universally necessary linear sequence. We ask what evidence and capabilities are adequate for the particular task, not whether the system has acquired every possible kind of knowledge.
