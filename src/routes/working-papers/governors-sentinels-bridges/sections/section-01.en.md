## 1. The architecture question

### 1.1 What non-uniqueness motivates

Paper 0's bounded predictors suggest that different factorizations can be adequate under different conditions. That raises a design question rather than a theorem requiring pluralism: which alternatives should be retained, at what cost, and for which tasks? In a stationary setting with an identified adequate predictor, maintaining alternatives may buy little. With regime change, costly identification or poorly known dynamics, preserving alternatives may enable a better response. Neither conclusion follows from the number of models alone (see corrected Papers VI and XX).

A governor is a candidate for action or prediction. A sentinel must supply actionable information before a relevant deadline. A functional bridge must enable some specified interaction, mapping or transfer between otherwise poorly connected actors. Each role requires its own task and counterfactual. A model may perform multiple roles, none, or one role at a cost that offsets its benefit. A performance-only selection rule does not directly evaluate warning or mediation, but might preserve them incidentally. Dissociation and attrition require evidence.

### 1.2 The architectures on trial

The historical experiment compared a fixed best-validation predictor (monoculture), an average of seven predictions (full pluralism), a retrospective selector taking minimum single-model loss at every origin (oracle), a periodically reviewed incumbent (closed WTA), and an adaptive incumbent with rolling loss comparisons. The latter two preserve alternatives for evaluation in different ways. Switching and evaluation costs were not priced. The oracle is a retrospective single-model selection benchmark, not a universal lower bound on achievable error: averaging two predictions of opposite error can beat both. In §3 we separate the historical comparison from a feasible online policy.
