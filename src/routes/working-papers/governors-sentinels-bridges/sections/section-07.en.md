## 7. What the role triad opens

### 7.1 Source terms as a hypothesis

Paper XVI's erosion of alternatives motivates testing whether selection on one score removes valuable alternatives. This paper does not establish the mechanism. Under a specified preservation rule, an alternative can be lost because its assessed performance is low; whether this removes useful warning depends on its incremental information, the remaining portfolio and the timing of decisions. Retention, use and retraining are separate operations. Paper XXIV's exposure and excitation questions offer a complementary route, not proof of this role triad.

### 7.2 A candidate failure mode

Institutions may lose shared language or transfer capability while retaining strong local performance. To test this, define a concrete mediation task (for example, translating a report between incompatible representations), score the joint result with and without the proposed intermediary, and compare to a matched substitute. A change in graph connectivity alone is insufficient; a low-centrality actor might supply indispensable translation and a high-centrality actor might supply none.

### 7.3 Certification as a design question

Paper XVII's certification problem raises the question of which capabilities need assurance. For tasks requiring performance, timely independent information and mediation, each deserves a test calibrated to consequences and cost. Not every institution requires three separate actors or all three tests. The triad is a checklist of candidate failure modes [IP], not a mathematical certification mandate.

### 7.4 Geometry and transformation

Paper XXIII investigates whether graph patterns recur under changes of regime and threshold, and whether behavioral dissimilarity predicts a fixed-budget adaptation-cost proxy. Its squared prediction dissimilarity is not this paper's RMS loss distance. It cannot verify the historical XIX graph results. Both papers now distinguish a graph constructed from behavior from a functional transfer network.
