## 4. Evidence access and selective missingness

The original Measurement Paradox is retained as a hypothesis about evidence access, not a theorem that higher inadequacy necessarily produces lower measured scores. A dataset may disappear because a channel was dismantled, reporting incentives changed, definitions changed, or publication was restricted. Distinguish whether information exists, is available internally, is transmitted to decision-makers, is used, and can be independently checked. An authority may know something it does not publish; an open dataset may never reach a decision.

For each missing item record the responsible source, date of last observation, plausible missingness mechanisms, and alternatives. Where plausible bounds can be justified, show sensitivity to both adverse and favourable missing values. If not, leave the direction of bias unknown. Wide uncertainty intervals do not by themselves identify concealment. Compare independent sources (administrative records, frontline testimony, audit samples, external measurements) with consent, safety and confidentiality protections. Document disagreements rather than resolving them through an unreported correction factor.

Three candidate mechanisms deserve separate investigation [H]: signal suppression (a previously available channel is restricted), definition change (a revised indicator no longer measures the same quantity), and symbolic transparency (publication continues without adequate detail or independent checking). Each has alternative explanations and requires dated evidence; none by itself establishes internal blindness.
