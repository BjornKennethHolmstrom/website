## Part V: Personal meta-governance as a voluntary practice

Personal meta-governance [IP/H] names the possibility of reconsidering a habit, interpretation or priority rather than merely pursuing it more effectively. The institutional analogy suggests making revision possible without making it compulsory. The practices below are optional and have no demonstrated effect size in this paper.

- **Value audit:** What matters to me at present? What is a chosen aim and what is a demand imposed by circumstance? Have I confused a measure with a purpose? Are there things I would rather not quantify?
- **External feedback:** A trusted person may offer another perspective, but need not be correct. Consent, privacy, power differences and the possibility of disagreement matter.
- **Protected reflection:** Make room, if wanted and feasible, for unstructured attention, writing, rest or an activity without a performance goal. A trial need not turn into a permanent commitment. No outcome is guaranteed.
- **Stopping or changing course:** Ask whether an undertaking is still wanted, feasible, or worth its consequences. Sometimes the appropriate response is to seek help or change an external constraint, not to optimise oneself.

The authenticity boundary remains central: mathematics cannot prescribe a meaningful life. Values may remain stable or change, and both can be appropriate. Declining an exercise, choosing not to set a goal or being unable to change one's situation is not evidence of a defective self. Revisions can have costs and uncertainty. There is no duty to seek broader values, self-transcendence or continuous self-improvement.

Examples of voluntary reflection include Socratic examination of an assumption, an Ignatian Examen understood as a reflective review of the day, or a weekly review of where attention has gone and what has been left unattended. Mentorship or spiritual direction may offer external perspectives when freely chosen. Noticing early bodily or relational signals can prompt a question, a rest or a conversation rather than an obligation to act. These examples are not tested interventions; clinical practices belong in appropriate care settings.
