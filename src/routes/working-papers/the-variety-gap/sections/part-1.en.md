## Part I — The Engineering Grammar and Its Generalization

### 1.1 Four Failure Modes, One Diagnostic Setting

Papers I–V develop an engineering vocabulary for institutional adaptation: feedback latency and signal fidelity (I), mismatches of disturbance timescales (II), attenuation of preference signals (III), and the loss of locally relevant information in commons governance (IV). Paper V examines their joint consequences. These are related limitations of bounded systems, not necessarily instances of one mathematical mechanism. Each earlier result retains its own model assumptions; this paper does not re-audit those papers.

A controller can fail because a signal arrives too late, because different disturbances require different response times, because information is distorted in transmission, or because an available intervention cannot be executed. A structural diagnosis is valuable when it distinguishes these possibilities from poor motivation or individual incompetence. It is not a substitute for evidence about the particular institution.

### 1.2 The Unified Perspective

Requisite variety (Ashby, 1956) relates the capacity to regulate disturbances to the variety of responses available under the task's constraints. A model of governance must specify what counts as an acceptable outcome before claiming that some disturbance is inadequately regulated. The static counting and alignment results in Appendix A make this concrete in a restricted setting. Paper XX's corrected action-cover argument gives a complementary finite formulation.

Bounded representation is a shared organizing perspective. Delay, observation noise, representation geometry, causal misspecification and implementation constraints are different mathematical objects. A common vocabulary does not establish a single conserved quantity or a universal threshold.

### 1.3 From Governance to Value

Institutions act according to explicit targets, implicit incentives, legal duties and judgments about acceptable outcomes. Their priorities may determine what receives measurement resources or what alternatives are tried. But an objective is not the sensor, and a measurement is not a value. We can evaluate a wealthy society by wealth alone while observing ecology because productivity depends on it; we can also declare ecological objectives without measuring ecology reliably.

The rest of the paper distinguishes these components, asks when their interaction restricts evidence and explores the institutional implications. The transition from governance to value is an extension of the diagnostic question, not a theorem identifying two architectures.
