### Part V — The Country Reports Re-interpreted Through the Variety Gap

The earlier country reports diagnosed distinctive institutional failure modes. Here five are revisited as candidate explanations, using the original case vocabulary. The case evidence has not been re-audited; these are neither validated estimates of a formal gap nor demonstrations of a threshold crossing. Appendix D records the wider set of hypotheses. Country descriptions refer to the reports' periods, not a fresh assessment of present conditions.

#### 5.1 Japan: Stability and Adaptive Capacity

The Japan report's *Continuity Trap* suggests that institutional continuity, lifetime employment, keiretsu networks and established administrative pathways may favor persistence over renewal. Demographic change and shifts in technology and competition can increase the cost of slow adaptation. Yet the original account itself notes that demographic and economic signals were measured. This is not proof of observational blindness: incentives, coordination constraints, model assumptions and limited response capacity are alternatives. Institutional Kaizen, sunset rules and municipal experimentation are proposed remedies. Test whether they improve adaptation at comparable cost, rather than merely adding indicators.

#### 5.2 Russia: Control and Independent Evidence

The Russia report's *Control–Blindness–Shock Loop* suggests that a priority on centralized control can create incentives to suppress unwelcome information and independent authority. This is a plausible case of selection affecting feedback, not a measurement of rank or a numerical threshold. Distinguish signal suppression, strategic misjudgment despite available reports, and inability to change policy. Gel'man (2015) supplies institutional context; the cited historical shocks do not by themselves identify a common causal mechanism. Test changes in independently documented reporting, information access and subsequent decisions.

#### 5.3 The United Kingdom: Centralized Delivery and Local Context

The UK report's *Centralise–Fail–Centralise Loop* links central targets, standardized delivery, local mismatch and pressure for further centralization. Local knowledge, trust, service capacity and social infrastructure may matter for outcomes. Central actors can know about failures yet lack resources, authority or incentives to correct them. Bevir and Rhodes (2003) provide background, not a test of the proposed loop. Trailblazer Regions, translation layers and delivery metrics are proposals; compare whether they improve information use or implementation relative to funding and authority changes.

#### 5.4 Brazil: Coalition Survival and Representation

The Brazil report's *Breakthrough–Capture Loop* describes institutional breakthroughs followed by capture or erosion of gains. Coalition bargaining can alter whose preferences influence decisions and how resources are distributed. That does not establish that citizen preferences are unobservable: unequal influence, conflicts of interest, transaction costs and intentional prioritization are distinct explanations. The U.S. study by Gilens and Page (2014) cannot establish the same relationship in Brazil. Proposed anti-capture and accountability mechanisms should be tested against rival explanations and actual decision pathways.

#### 5.5 The European Union: Negotiation and Temporal Coherence

The EU report's negotiation-dilution diagnosis suggests that consensus procedures may trade speed and policy coherence for agreement. Cross-border dependencies and asymmetric time pressures may make particular responses difficult. Distinguish delayed or incomplete information from vetoes, legal competence, distributional conflict and execution delays. Multiscale coordination and staged response arrangements are candidate remedies, not a deduction from the number of goals.

#### 5.6 Summary Table

| Case | Candidate inadequacy | Rival or complementary explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Japan | Slow renewal, adaptation | Demographic constraints, policy inertia |
| Russia | Suppressed independent evidence | Strategic choices, model error |
| UK | Local information and delivery | Fiscal and legal capacity |
| Brazil | Preference influence, accountability | Bargaining and distributional conflict |
| EU | Temporal/spatial coordination | Vetoes and competence constraints |

The common diagnostic question is whether the evidence and feasible responses match the task. The common label is not a finding of common causation. No country has been assigned a measured $g_{\mathrm{hidden}}$ or a validated threshold status.
