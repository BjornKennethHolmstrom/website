### Part VI — Meta-Governance: Designing for Open-Ended Value Evolution

#### 6.1 The Higher-Order Governance Problem

Institutions may need to reconsider their goals, evidence and decision procedures as tasks change. This is a higher-order design problem, not a proof that ever-expanding objectives guarantee viability. Sometimes the right response is a simpler metric, a better model, a fixed precaution, or more reliable execution. Sometimes the goal itself is contested.

#### 6.2 Second-Order Cybernetics and Value Architecture

Observing the observer (von Foerster, 1984) invites scrutiny of the information process itself. An audit can ask whether a signal is obtainable, whether it is used, whether its accuracy is independently checkable, and whether corrective competence can be restored. Unused information differs from lost capacity. A frozen belief due to uninformative evidence differs from growing confidence in a false model. Distinguish byproduct-of-success laundering, where successful operation itself degrades the diagnostic (Papers XVIII and XXIV), measurement dependence (Paper XXII), and stock confounding (SO-WP0). Policy-dependent exposure traps (SO-WP1) are another mechanism. These distinctions guide remedies without asserting equivalence among the models.

#### 6.3 Institutional Mechanisms for Value Evolution

**Value audits.** Examine declared and revealed priorities, observation access and quality, model assumptions, feasible responses, and the experience of affected groups. Record what the audit cannot establish; avoid treating a crisis count as both evidence of a gap and its independent validation.

**Standing deliberative bodies.** Citizens' assemblies, intergenerational councils and futures commissions may surface neglected perspectives and value conflicts. Their representativeness, information quality, authority and effects require evaluation. Deliberative legitimacy (Habermas, 1996; Dryzek, 2000) is not reducible to a rank increase.

**Protocols for revisable arrangements.** Amendment pathways, sunset clauses, protected pilots and transfer of functions may enable correction before failure. Costs, rights, commitment and capture risks matter. Dissolution is neither inevitable nor always desirable; retention can preserve valuable institutional competence.

**Fractality of value.** Distributed specification at local, regional, national and planetary scales may preserve local evidence and coordinate wider effects. Polycentric commons governance (Ostrom, 1990) motivates this proposal. Decentralization is not intrinsically superior when information, actions and resources are equivalent; communication, accountability and conflicts across levels must be considered.

#### 6.4 The Asymptotic Nature of Wholism

Wholism is used here as an orientation toward revisable inquiry and attention to neglected consequences, not a mathematical convergence theorem. Peirce's open-ended inquiry and Bateson's emphasis on consequential differences provide philosophical context. A finite institution may deliberately ignore irrelevant distinctions or economize on information acquisition. Broader evaluation can carry costs, and capacity to learn need not expand monotonically with the number of valued attributes.

In the static model of Appendix A, a lossless re-encoding of the existing observations preserves their indistinguishability classes. In particular, if the encoding is injective on $C(D)$, the hidden disturbance space $K$ and $g_{\mathrm{hidden}}$ are unchanged. Refining the processing of existing information can improve decisions but cannot reduce this hidden gap. More informative or better-aligned observations may reduce it; changing the tolerated subspace $L$ can also change the measure, but changes the task. Enlarging the action set can improve regulatory feasibility without changing $g_{\mathrm{hidden}}$.

#### 6.5 Legitimacy and the Boundary of the Framework

Engineering can establish conditional constraints for a specified task, but cannot choose the task, acceptable deviations, legitimate authority or distribution of rights and burdens. Even an informationally adequate regulator may pursue illegitimate ends. Legitimate institutions need procedures through which those affected can contest objectives, evidence and decisions. Whether to include particular values is not answered by the mathematics of variety. The proposals in Part IV have ethical and empirical rationales that require argument in their own right.

#### 6.6 The Meta-Governance Proposal, Restated

A civilization that can optimize but cannot reconsider what it optimizes for may become poorly adapted to changed conditions. That is a conditional concern, not an inevitability result. Meta-governance should be examined as a capacity to identify problems in goals, sensing, models, decisions and action, and to revise arrangements when justified. The institutional proposals remain preliminary. Their adequacy should be compared with simpler or less costly alternatives, not assumed from their holistic intent.
