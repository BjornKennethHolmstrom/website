## 1. Three laws, one suspicion

The series has three load-bearing laws with three different pedigrees. Ashby's law of requisite variety arrives from cybernetics as received wisdom: a controller needs sufficient variety for the task it regulates. Goodhart's law arrives from economics as an aphorism about the risks of turning measures into targets. Certification cost is the series' own construction, introduced in Paper XVII as the price a system pays to check that its model of the world still fits.

They share a concern with what a *bounded* system can do. Ashby concerns holding a factorization and acting through it; Goodhart concerns optimizing a proxy; certification concerns maintaining a factorization's fit over time. The suspicion pursued here is that these can be understood together, without treating their different origins as a reason to study them in isolation.

The distinction between a common organizing premise and a sufficient mathematical premise matters. Finite representation supplies the counting argument of §3, but does not itself imply a harmful proxy ordering or a positive maintenance cost. Section 4 requires a specified intervention set and proxy–target comparison; §5 requires non-refundable accounting assumptions. The contribution is consolidation, not three new theorems. The earlier intervention-set novelty claim is withdrawn in light of the counterexample and prior art in §4.2. The registered demonstration remains useful as a deliberately simple instance of a feasible resource trade-off.
