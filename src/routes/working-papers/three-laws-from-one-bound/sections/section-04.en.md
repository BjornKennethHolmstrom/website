## 4. Goodhart as an ordering criterion — the optimization face [R for the criterion]

Ashby concerns a controller acting through a factorization. Goodhart concerns optimizing a measured quantity that stands in for a target. Bounded representation motivates scrutiny of proxies, but it does not imply that every proxy is lossy or that every loss of information is harmful. The criterion below is a restatement of established reward-gaming theory in the paper's notation; the registered demonstration illustrates a specific mechanism.

### 4.1 The three-term decomposition

*Projection.* Write $M = g(f(E))$ for a proxy obtained from the factorization $f$ of the world $E$, and $T$ for the target. A proxy may collapse distinctions relevant to $T$, but a bounded representation can also retain all distinctions relevant to a particular target. Whether compression is consequential depends on the task and the interventions.

*Optimization.* Let $X$ be a specified nonempty set of attainable intervention outcomes, with real-valued proxy and target functions $M,T$ on $X$. The same set is used for both. A comparison across $X$ tests possible order reversals, not whether a particular search trajectory will realize them. For a specified current state and admissible transitions, a trajectory analysis must additionally respect those transitions.

*Non-factorizability.* An omitted target-relevant distinction can create a risk of divergence, especially when improving the measured quantity consumes resources needed by the omitted quantity. But the fact that an omitted quantity can be changed does not establish such a trade-off, and the existence of a trade-off does not establish that a particular optimization algorithm will take it.

### 4.2 The intervention-set ordering criterion

**Definition and criterion [R; established prior art].** Call $M$ weakly monotone-safe on $X$ if no strict improvement in $M$ between outcomes in $X$ decreases $T$. Equivalently, for all $x,y$ in $X$, $M(y)>M(x)$ implies $T(y)\ge T(x)$. The negation is the existence of a pair with $M(y)>M(x)$ and $T(y)<T(x)$. This equivalence follows directly from the definition and is not a new theorem.

Strict monotonic safety additionally requires $T(y)>T(x)$ whenever $M(y)>M(x)$. Tie-robust safety requires $T(y)\ge T(x)$ whenever $M(y)\ge M(x)$; applied to both directions of a proxy tie, it requires equal target values. Equivalently, tie-robust safety holds exactly when $T=h\circ M$ for a non-decreasing function $h$ on $M(X)$. Indeed, equality of proxy values forces equality of target values, making $h$ well-defined; ordered proxy values then force ordered target values. The converse follows by monotonicity. Thus a target-relevant distinction collapsed by $M$ within $X$ always breaks tie-robust safety, but need not create a strict reversal. A strict reversal additionally requires a feasible adverse ordering. Weak safety permits target stagnation and unsafe tie-breaking. None of these criteria alone promises a target-optimal outcome from an arbitrary starting point. A harmful pair in $X$ is a possibility under unrestricted pairwise comparison, not a prediction of a realized optimization path.

**Counterexample to loss plus reachability.** Take $X=\{(0,0),(0,1),(2,0),(2,1)\}$, $M(a,b)=a$ and $T(a,b)=a+b$. The omitted coordinate $b$ is target-relevant and varies among feasible outcomes. Yet at $M=0$ the target is either 0 or 1, while at $M=2$ it is either 2 or 3. Every strict proxy improvement strictly improves the target. Thus loss and reachability do not imply even stagnation under strict improvement, although changes within a proxy tie can be harmful. By contrast, on $X=\{(0,2),(1,0)\}$ the same functions admit a strict reversal: the proxy rises from 0 to 1 while the target falls from 2 to 1. The decisive extra fact is the feasible trade-off, not merely the omitted coordinate's reachability.

This is a finite deterministic outcome example. Enlarging the feasible set can introduce reversals. In particular, if lotteries over the four outcomes are feasible and scored by expected $M$ and $T$, the lottery equally mixing $(0,0)$ and $(2,0)$ has expected scores $(1,1)$, whereas $(0,1)$ has scores $(0,1)$. A lottery assigning probability $1/4$ to $(2,0)$ and $3/4$ to $(0,0)$ has scores $(0.5,0.5)$, yielding a strict reversal relative to $(0,1)$. Thus safety must be assessed for the actual feasible class, including randomization where available.

**Relation to prior work.** Skalse, Howe, Krasheninnikov and Krueger (2022), [Defining and Characterizing Reward Gaming](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/3d719fee332caa23d5038b8a90e81796-Abstract-Conference.html), define reward hackability relative to a policy set by precisely such a strict reversal of expected returns. The criterion here is the one-step deterministic counterpart. Under their finite-MDP assumptions, Theorem 1 (§5.1) shows that non-trivial unhackable reward pairs must induce equivalent orderings on stationary policy sets containing an open subset, where openness is defined in the affine hull of all stationary policies represented by their action probabilities. Lemma 1 establishes that the visit-count image of such an open subset is open in the corresponding visit-count coordinates. Corollary 1 specializes the theorem to all stationary policies. In particular, for the one-step problem with all lotteries over a finite outcome set and expected scores, nonconstant weakly safe scores must induce equivalent orderings (indeed, one expected score is a positive affine transform of the other). The finite deterministic example does not extend to that lottery class. This restriction concerns the actual feasible class and linear expected scores, not non-convexity as a universal requirement for safety. A reward with constant expected return is another, trivial case. Zhuang and Hadfield-Menell (2020), [Consequences of Misaligned AI](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/hash/b607ba543ad05417b8507ee86c54fcb7-Abstract.html), study incomplete proxy utilities in resource-constrained worlds, including conditions for arbitrarily low true utility (Theorem 2). At a converged proxy optimum under their resource assumptions, omitted attributes reach their lower bounds (Theorem 1). The budget mechanism in §4.3 is a two-attribute instance of this depletion logic: the proxy optimum places the omitted attribute at its permitted lower bound. The degradation magnitude and path in §4.3 are specific to this model; the arbitrarily-low-utility result is not claimed here. Paper XVIII's non-factorizability result motivates examining omitted distinctions, but does not by itself imply a reachable reversal or its realization. The earlier claim that this section supplies a novel general theorem is withdrawn.

### 4.3 A minimal demonstration [R within the model]

The effect of a specific reachable resource trade-off was demonstrated in a study registered in advance and run across thirty worlds. A fixed budget is allocated between a measured dimension $a$ and a hidden dimension $b$; the target rewards both through a concave utility $T = \alpha a^p + \beta b^p$; the proxy is the projection that drops the hidden dimension, $M = a^p$. Because the utility is concave, the target's true optimum splits the budget between the two dimensions; the proxy, seeing only $a$, drives the whole budget toward $a$. Within this model, with its fixed budget and initially target-optimal allocation, degradation depends on one manipulated variable: the reachability $r$ of the hidden dimension, defined as how far the optimizer may starve $b$ below its target-optimal value $b^{\ast}$.

The prediction was that degradation of the target scales with $r$ — zero when the hidden dimension is frozen at its optimum ($r=0$), maximal when it is fully starvable ($r=1$). Across thirty randomly drawn worlds the median target degradation ran $0.000,\ 0.007,\ 0.029,\ 0.079,\ 0.268$ across $r \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$: monotone in every one of the thirty worlds, with a pooled rank correlation of $0.98$ between reachability and degradation. At $r=1$ every world showed degradation above the registered threshold; at $r=0$ every world showed exactly none, since freezing the hidden dimension at $b^{\ast}$ forces the budget constraint to place the proxy's optimum on the target's. Within this budget model, the same proxy — $M = a^p$ throughout — yields increasing degradation as the permitted reduction of $b$ increases. The relation need not be proportional or generalize to other feasible sets.

Two qualifications carry into the paper. The $r=0$ result is analytic: freezing $b$ at $b^{\ast}$ and starting at the target optimum makes zero degradation true by construction. Indeed, with $a(r)=a^{\ast}+rb^{\ast}$ and $b(r)=(1-r)b^{\ast}$, strict concavity for $0<p<1$ makes $T(r)$ strictly decreasing for $r>0$: its derivative at zero is zero and its derivative thereafter is negative for $0<r<1$. The reported numerical scaling summarizes this model across parameter draws; it is not independent evidence of a general law. The study concerns degradation from an initially target-optimal state along this budget trade-off. It does not establish that omitted-dimension reachability is necessary or sufficient for degradation in other models, or that freezing an omitted dimension at an arbitrary value makes the proxy safe.

**Interpretation of the registered tests.** The relative degradation $D(r)$ is independent of $C>0$: the optimal and perturbed allocations scale linearly with $C$ and both target values scale as $C^p$. Thus drawing $C$ does not vary relative degradation. P2 and the per-world monotonicity part of P3 follow by construction; the pooled rank statistic and P1 threshold count remain sample-dependent. P1 is not guaranteed over the registered parameter box: for $p=0.7$, $\alpha=1.2$, $\beta=0.8$, the endpoint degradation is about 0.067, below 0.10. These are post-review analytical qualifications, not a change to the registration or reported outcomes.
