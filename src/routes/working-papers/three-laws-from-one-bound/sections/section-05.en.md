## 5. Certification cost as monotone accounting — the maintenance face [IP]

The preceding sections examine task adequacy and proxy ordering under their respective assumptions. The third face concerns maintenance: what it costs to check and update a factorization as the world changes. This is the series’ certification framework (Paper XVII); the accounting result below is conditional on the stated convention.

### 5.1 What certification is, and why it costs

A factorization adequate today need not be adequate tomorrow, because adequate-action sets or conditions may change (§3): a representation cell may no longer admit the action assigned to it. Certification is the process of checking whether the representation and policy remain adequate — gathering fresh data, comparing the factorization's predictions against it, detecting the discrepancies Paper XVI calls source terms, and deciding whether to keep the factorization or refactor. These steps can consume resources. A certification that concludes "still adequate" supplies evidence under the adopted test, not a guarantee of adequacy; a certification that calls for refactoring may incur additional costs.

### 5.2 The monotone quantity

Define the cumulative certification cost as the running total of four terms over the controller's lifetime: sensing (the cost of gathering data), audit (the cost of comparing prediction to data), refactoring (the cost of expanding or revising the factorization when a mismatch is found), and *closure debt* — the accumulated, not-yet-paid cost of discrepancies that have been detected or have arisen but not yet been resolved. The claim, the series' Second Law, is conditional: unpaid closure debt must not be written down except when converted into an equal or greater paid cost, and no paid costs may be refunded. Under that convention:

> The cumulative cost of staying aligned — sensing plus audit plus refactoring plus accrued closure debt — is non-decreasing over the controller's lifetime.

The reasoning is that each term is individually non-negative and none is ever refunded. Sensing and audit are paid and gone. Refactoring is paid when incurred. Under the stipulated convention, closure debt only grows or is converted into paid cost of at least equal size. So the running total cannot decrease. Under this convention, deferral does not reduce the recorded total; if deferred discrepancies are later resolved, the paid cost may be larger and more concentrated, for example if unresolved discrepancies generate further ones. Monotonicity follows from the accounting convention, not from an irreversible information state or a guarantee of eventual payment. If a discrepancy resolves without expenditure or a liability estimate is written down, the sum including outstanding debt need not be monotone.

### 5.3 The conservation law that is not there

The exploratory work reached for something stronger than a monotone cost, and the honest report is that it is not there. The stronger conjecture was a *conservation* law: a fixed budget of representational complexity, conserved under reform, that could only be *moved* — from institution to individual, from rule to judgment — but never reduced. The intuition is seductive and has real instances. Simplify a tax code and the complexity reappears in the taxpayer's affairs; simplify the taxpayer's life with a comprehensive bureaucracy and the complexity reappears in the institution. It reads like a conservation law: total complexity constant, only its location free.

The exploratory accounting proposed an *inequality*, not an equality: $V_I + V_H + V_S + V_\epsilon \ge V_A$, or, in the original factorization-entropy notation, $H_B + H_R + H_O + H_\epsilon \ge H_W$. The institutional, cognitive, sensing and residual terms were intended to describe how variety is distributed across the controller and its operators. These are retained as heuristic formulations [H], not consequences of §3's action-cover bound. Additivity and the choice of common units require separate justification; the action count $V_A$ cannot simply be identified with an entropy. The narrower point is that a necessary lower bound on adequate response capacity does not establish a fixed complexity budget. A better partition may use fewer cells than an inefficient one while still admitting adequate actions; it cannot go below the action-cover bound while achieving adequacy under the same task and assumptions. No conservation identity is established.

So the monotone quantity is the *cost of staying aligned*, not the *amount of complexity*. This is the correction the section commits to: the Second Law is an accounting inequality about non-refundable cost, not a conservation principle about a fixed complexity budget. The distinction is exactly the kind the series' anti-inflation discipline exists to enforce — a conserved quantity would be a stronger and more elegant claim, and it would be false, and reporting that it is false is worth more to the framework than an elegant result that does not hold. What survives under the stipulated accounting convention is a running total of non-refundable costs and non-written-down debt; it does not establish that every deferred maintenance task must eventually be performed. What does not survive is the tempting picture of complexity as a fluid that can be pushed around but never drained. A more efficient factorization can reduce representational requirements relative to an inefficient one; repeated checking may incur costs when the task changes. The original lower-bound accounting remains heuristic pending separate justification.
