## 8. Conclusion

Working Paper 0 began from the observation that an optimizer can solve the wrong problem correctly. This paper asks what happens next, once the incompleteness is recognised.

The answer from one synthetic flood model is that the three available responses — changing what we believe, changing what we value, and trying to learn — lead to different outcomes, and constrain each other.

- **Assuming the missing mechanism** produced lower mean flood-and-finance costs than **re-weighting the objective toward ecology** at exact parameters [R, confirmatory]. On average it did so even in the branch where the assumption was false [R, descriptive subgroup].
- **The ecological objective** produced better wetland outcomes [R, confirmatory]. Its plans were much less sensitive to the omission [R, prespecified descriptive], but its price was paid whether or not the mechanism operated.
- **Learning** was limited less by inference than by exposure and time. A planner can maintain its own blind spot, and a simple exact learner earned little of what an oracle could [R, structural and exploratory].
- **None of these rankings is unconditional.** Under parameter error the cost advantage of the assumption nearly disappeared, and which omission matters most changed [R, exploratory].

**[IP]** What changes in the programme after this paper is the unit of evaluation. It is not enough to ask whether a model is more complete or an objective more inclusive. The relevant object is the *package*: what is represented, what is optimised, and what the resulting policy allows us to observe. That package should be evaluated across criteria and conditions, against feasible rather than clairvoyant alternatives.

The next studies in the series should therefore test these packages, including robust and belief-state planners, under declared conditions in which their trade-offs can reverse.
