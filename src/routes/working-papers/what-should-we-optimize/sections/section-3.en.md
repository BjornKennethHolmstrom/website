## 3. Experiment A1: the world may be misrepresented

### 3.1 The mechanism isolated

A1 evaluates an 11-period regional flood system with 31,104 states. The common evaluation criterion charges financial outlays, flood damage and monetized harm, with an additional worst-off component in a secondary version. **F0** minimizes that criterion under its planning model. **F1-eco** adds an auxiliary penalty for missing upstream wetlands; **F1-eq** adds one for the worst-off municipality's net damage. **F2** uses a robust hydrological transition ambiguity set. Premium-matched reserve policies and a myopic policy serve as controls. The same chosen policies are scored against the true world.

Two kinds of model imperfection are intentionally separated. **Parametric error** perturbs known mechanisms in parameter-appropriate coordinates, without prescribing which way the errors point. **Structural error** is a bundled simplification: the narrow planning model removes the downstream effect of upstream wetland retention, sets peak synchronisation to zero, and removes correlation between the two tributaries' rainfall. A separate evaluation shift makes heavy rainfall more or less likely than anticipated.

The distinction matters: an extra wetland reward is not universal insurance. In a dry world it may induce costly restoration. In a structurally simplified model, the same reward can steer the planner toward investments neglected by that simplification. **Because the narrow model changes three mechanisms at once, the experiment cannot identify downstream retention as the sole cause** of this substitution effect.

### 3.2 Confirmatory results [R within A1]

| Claim and test | Estimated effect | Adjusted interval | Frozen verdict |
|:---|---:|:---|:---|
| **H1′**: wetland objective, unknown-direction parameter error ($s=0.25$) | −0.021 | [−0.121, 0.071] | Inconclusive |
| **H1′**: worst-off objective, same cell | 0.001 | [−0.015, 0.017] | Refuted: negligible |
| **H1″**: wetland objective substitutes for omitted structure | 0.343 | [0.293, 0.393] | Supported |
| **H1″**: worst-off objective | 0.020 | [0.007, 0.033] | Refuted: negligible |
| **H2′a**: ambiguity aversion under wetter shift | 0.831 | [0.805, 0.854] | Supported |
| **H2′b**: ambiguity aversion over premium-matched reserve control | 1.727 | [1.635, 1.828] | Supported |

Intervals are 97.5% adjusted intervals under the frozen family rules. H1′ and H2′ use the specified F0-minus-oracle regret gaps; **H1″ is not a recovery share**: it is the additional absolute improvement under the narrow relative to the full planning model, divided by the narrow-model gap. A separate exploratory narrow-model recovery share is about 0.330. The P0 optimality null passed in all 800 worlds. Premium matching failed in 0.4% of worlds, below its pre-specified limit.

**The first result is a limitation, not a triumph:** adding a wetland term does not establish a reliable improvement under noisy parameters of unknown direction. Its interval spans benefits and harms that matter. By contrast, the *structural-substitution* contrast is positive: the wetland term improves performance substantially more in the jointly simplified model than in the full model. This is a **substitution for a bundle of omitted mechanisms**, not an isolated causal effect of retained-water hydrology. The exploratory retention-tertile pattern is consistent with retention contributing, but it does not isolate its contribution. In other cells F2 also partly offsets structural misspecification (approximately 0.31 of regret in the narrow, exact-parameter, unshifted condition).

**The robust result is directional and cell-specific.** The 83.1% reduction in F0 regret is at parameter-error scale $s=0$, with a wetter evaluation shift $\psi=+0.1$. At that same shift the exploratory recovery falls to approximately 29.3%, 9.6%, and 6.7% as $s$ rises to 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5. Under $s=0$ and the drier shift $\psi=-0.1$, recovery is about −1.79, corresponding to **0.48 higher cost units** than F0; the large ratio partly reflects a small mean oracle gap (about 0.27). Critically, the wetter shift lies **outside** the F2 L1 ambiguity ball of radius 0.2: the post-result review computed distances of 0.23–0.32 in the normal regime and 0.21–0.24 in the wet regime across the sealed worlds. Thus the observed benefit is **not** an in-set coverage guarantee of robust-MDP theory (Iyengar, 2005; Nilim & El Ghaoui, 2005). The 1.727 statistic compares F2 with a premium-matched reserve policy that is itself worse than F0; it does not mean F2 recovered 173% of F0's regret.

### 3.3 Resilience without generalized adaptability [R, exploratory]

The separately run G6 stress battery adds a useful behavioral check. Its questions were outlined in advance, but the specific G6 cells, continuation definition, ledger conventions, tolerance, interpolation and PRIM settings were fixed **after G5 confirmatory verdicts were known** and before G6 results were calculated. These analyses are therefore exploratory. At $s=0.25$, with the full model, the standard criterion and no rainfall shift, average early cost (periods 0–5) is **14.98** for F0 and **16.54** for F1-eco. The latter is *total modeled evaluation cost*, not simply cash expenditure. Under a later storm shock, excess cost with each policy's own continuation is **5.36** for F0 and **4.67** for F1-eco. F2 shows an intermediate pattern: early cost **15.27**, storm excess **5.11**. The associated full-horizon costs remain close: **5.98**, **6.03** and **6.02**, respectively. Earlier preparation therefore buys some shock protection, but its cost is visible elsewhere.

The proposed *value of acting after a shock* measure generated a methodological counterexample. Myopic and reserve-constrained policies, which had often failed to invest, appeared unusually adaptable because optimal corrective action from their neglected states was worth a great deal. Their high future action value reflected **unfinished protective work**, not necessarily superior response capacity. A1's pre-registered hoarding guard did not establish option preservation beyond matched reserve rules, and the diagnostic cannot distinguish genuine flexibility from accumulated need. A later study must measure response quality together with the damage already allowed to occur.

### 3.4 Mutual benefit is a separate question [R, exploratory]

A1 also compares three *fixed programmes*: wetland restoration (W), emergency-resource pooling (P), and hard protection (H). It evaluates changes in nine distinct financial, harm, wetland and habitat accounts rather than collapsing them into a single sum. **Mutual benefit** means that every applicable account is no worse and at least one is better. **Synergy** requires a positive interaction effect, which is not the same as Pareto improvement.

With fixed interventions and a stringent nine-account definition (every account non-worse to tolerance $10^{-9}\max(1,|Y(\varnothing)|)$), at the unshifted evaluation condition, restoration alone is mutually beneficial in **14.5%** of worlds. Pooling alone qualifies in **0.25%**, hard protection in **none**. For pairs, W×P is mutually beneficial in **4.25%**, W×H and P×H in **none**. W×P is synergistic in **2.25%**, but *not in the same worlds* where it is mutually beneficial. Across all 800 worlds and tested pairs, **no pairing is both mutually beneficial and synergistic**. W×H shows interfering effects in **42%** of worlds, reflecting substitution between two sources of downstream flood protection.

The tested programmes did **not** furnish a clear positive engineering complementarity: W×H was predominantly substitutive, while apparent P×H synergy in upstream relief can reflect the redistribution of a fixed emergency resource when downstream levees reduce downstream need. Consequently this is **not** evidence that strong engineering complementarities fail to create mutual benefit; it is a model-specific record of **no tested pair with both properties**. It is not an impossibility result. The ledger uses strict nine-account Pareto improvement, hard protection mechanically lowers the river-habitat sentinel, and the fixed programmes exclude downstream **co-financing of restoration**, although that action exists in the full model. The next question is whether adaptive programmes, transfers or compensation alter those outcomes.
