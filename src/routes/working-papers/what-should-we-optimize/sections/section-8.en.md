## 8. Research agenda and relation to Governance as Engineering

A defensible next sequence should preserve the same methodological restrictions used here: sealed worlds, independent verification, negative controls, explicit nulls, and clear separations between model findings and interpretations. Three directions follow naturally.

**A2: structure-aware optimization under model incompleteness.** Compare better causal representations, targeted auxiliary objectives, robust ambiguity sets and combinations of these. Vary the relevance of omitted mechanisms independently of the direction of parameter error. The principal question should be *when* a surrogate objective helps, rather than whether complexity-affirming objectives are generically superior.

**A3: mutual benefit under adaptive programmes and bargaining constraints.** Replace fixed interventions with adaptive regional and municipal strategies. Track the full nine-account consequences, check transfers and compensation, and report which accounts block feasible mutual benefit. Distinguish positive engineering interaction from gains that everyone can accept. In particular, test whether the fixed-programme substitution of wetlands and levees persists when policies can adjust timing and intensity.

**B6 or a future epistemic-options study:** allow agents to investigate possible goals, but also to *create* new activities or learn new capabilities that expand the space of feasible futures. Compare the value of expanding that space with merely maintaining existing capabilities. This study would test a phenomenon B and B5 exclude by construction.

These directions have precedents in adaptive policy pathways, where decisions are deliberately staged as information arrives (Haasnoot et al., 2013). They are compatible with GaE but do not validate its governance mechanisms by themselves. The link is architectural: an institution's observation channels, objectives, decision rights and feedback costs determine what optimization problem it can even formulate. GaE concerns the organizations and boundaries through which those choices are made; Systemic Optimization investigates their decision-theoretic consequences.

A working-paper series can make this complementarity productive by treating governance extrapolations as **[IP]**, not **[R]**, until appropriate empirical or institutionally realistic models test them. The series should welcome genuinely negative results, since the point is to improve problem construction, not to demonstrate that one favorite vocabulary always wins.
