## 9. Conclusion

The question “what should we optimize?” cannot be answered by selecting a more expansive objective function alone. A planner may have the wrong causal model, the wrong expectations about future goals, or the wrong way of paying for information. Three experiments test those mismatches separately.

A1 shows that objective augmentation and ambiguity aversion have conditional, sharply different strengths and failures. B shows that, at the frozen possibility-measure weight, structural option metrics can be diagnostically informative without beating the specified premium-matched reserve controls. B5 shows that information may be valuable while **its modeled method of acquisition** costs more than the information is worth: in this reduced environment, a period of foregone productive activity often overwhelms the value of two noisy signals. Changing the acquisition channel alters the practical decision without changing the signal's statistical meaning.

The joint conclusion is deliberately modest. **[IP] The architecture of an optimization problem should be matched to the particular uncertainty and institutional constraint it faces.** No single hedge, diversity score, or learning policy is justified across all four dimensions by these results. The design task is to ask which world mechanisms are represented, which future values are plausible, what commitments are reversible, and when information can still change an action.

That is the proposed foundation of *Systemic Optimization*: not a universal method for finding the best outcome, but a disciplined way to investigate **why an apparently correct optimization problem may not be the right problem to solve**.
