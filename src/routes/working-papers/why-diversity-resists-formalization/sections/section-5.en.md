## 5. What this does not show

The elicitation does not establish a common causal mechanism or formal isomorphism, a measurable global index of contestability, or a universal tendency toward depletion. Nor does the absence of a demonstrated unification establish that the mechanisms are independent. The parallels are hypotheses about particular models and can motivate explicit cross-model comparisons.

The population experiments use two different diversity summaries. A correlation-based effective count \(N/[1+(N-1)\max(\bar\rho,0)]\) is a heuristic for observer dependence in a suitable exchangeable setting, whereas the inverse Simpson count \(1/\sum_k p_k^2\) measures opinion-share concentration. Neither alone measures accuracy, information value or decision adequacy. Many independent mistaken opinions may have high diversity; unanimous correct opinions may have low diversity.


The initial belief-vector model explicitly inserts mean correlation into the herding coefficient, \(d(\kappa+\max(\bar\rho,0))\), with independent resets. The later local model instead uses agreement weights and a saturating pull toward the weighted mean. The difference is important: the first builds global reinforcement into the rule, the second asks whether local correlation neglect creates a similar effect. Neither finite-horizon branch comparison proves multiple stationary laws. In the belief-vector code the effective count clips negative mean correlation at zero and is computed after temporal averaging (in the initial script also after averaging seeds). In the opinion model the inverse Simpson count is evaluated at each retained sweep and averaged.

The bounded-conviction experiment reports no detected branch separation in sampled runs [HR], not a proof of a single attractor or rapid mixing. With independent positive reset probability \(p>0\) for each agent, an all-agent reset event has probability \(p^N\); for the specified common reset distribution this permits regeneration and a coupling argument. That argument fails at zero reset rate and may give an impractically weak bound. The preferential-copying experiment illustrates concentration across sampled population sizes; a few sizes and two seeds do not prove an extensive-to-intensive phase transition or asymptotic scaling law. The historical plots and code are archival evidence, with no full sweep reproduction.
