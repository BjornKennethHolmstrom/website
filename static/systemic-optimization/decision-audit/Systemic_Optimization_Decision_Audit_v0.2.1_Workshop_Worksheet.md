# Systemic Optimization — Decision Audit v0.2.1

**WORKSHOP WORKSHEET · DRAFT v0.2.1 · 7 NOVEMBER 2026**  
**Status:** Exploratory decision-support instrument, **not validated**. It does not produce a risk score, optimize a policy, or establish that a real-world decision is safe or correct.

**Purpose.** Examine whether a decision is distorted by its objectives, causal assumptions or the evidence its own policy makes available; compare feasible remedies and their unintended consequences.

**New in v0.2.1.** A worked Q3 selective-evidence probe, explicit combined packages, a 45-minute mode, an option to reframe the decision, and an optional one-page after-action review. **Freeze for pilot testing after this revision.**

## How to run the workshop — quick or full

| Phase | Quick · 45 min | Full · 85 min | Workshop output |
|:--|--:|--:|:--|
| 0. Frame decision and roles | 5 | 8 | Decision, owner, affected people, failure condition |
| 1. Decision relevance | 7 | 10 | What plausible assumption could change the action? |
| 2. Four probes (Q3 mandatory) | 20 | 35 | Objectives, model, blind spots, feasible learning |
| 3. Package + coupling matrix | 8 | 20 | Comparable responses, exposure and costs |
| 4. Decision and review record | 5 | 12 | Action or reframe, owner, safeguards, review trigger |

**Phase 2 allocation:** Quick Q1 3 min · Q2 3 min · **Q3 10 min** · Q4 4 min. Full: **at least 15 min for Q3**. Q3 is never skipped; mark unknowns if time runs out.

**Facilitation:** Phase 1 starts with 3 minutes of silent individual work. Q3 also starts with 3 silent minutes. Name an assumption challenger: *“Suppose this belief is false. What could our policy still hide?”* Record disagreements, including the decision owner's objections; absent stakeholders have not consented.

**Evidence key:** **[O]** observed · **[M]** model estimate · **[H]** hypothesis · **[U]** unknown. Example-only: **[C]** confirmatory · **[D]** prespecified descriptive · **[E]** exploratory · **[S]** model-structural · **[IP]** interpretive. None is direct field validation.

## Q4 · Can feasible learning improve the decision *in time*?

1. **Exposure:** When will the suspected mechanism, group or state first be encountered?
2. **Evidence:** When will competing hypotheses first predict **different observations**? Exposure alone does not guarantee identification.
3. **Inference and action:** Who can interpret the evidence, when, and how much time remains to change course?
4. **Alternative to inference:** Would a simple state-contingent rule—*“if X is observed, do Y”*—capture most of the benefit without identifying the causal mechanism?
5. **Decision consequence:** **What would we do differently if we knew the answer?** If nothing, learning may have little decision value.
6. **Net value:** Relative to keeping or hedging, is the benefit of a **feasible** learner greater than delay, money, risk and burdens imposed on others? Do not compare only with an omniscient oracle.

# Appendix A · Worked example: A2 synthetic flood management

**Illustrative application, not a real catchment assessment or validation of this instrument.** Sources: Working Paper 1 v0.2, §§4–7, Appendix B (T5), and its evidence-status appendix. [Manuscript in the research repository](https://github.com/BjornKennethHolmstrom/systemic-optimization/blob/main/papers/wp1/manuscript.md).

## Decision, scenarios and confirmatory package result

Three municipalities allocate flood-adaptation measures over **11 periods**. A physical retention mechanism **R** may be present or absent. The planner may include or omit R in its model. Conventional objective **U1** and ecological objective **F1-eco** value outcomes differently.

| Confirmatory package | Model assumption | Objective |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **REP** | Assume R | Minimize conventional U1 |
| **COMP** | Omit R | Optimize F1-eco |

**[C/M]** Over **40 independently drawn synthetic worlds**, with declared 50/50 hypothetical weighting of R present and absent: **REP − COMP = −1.112 U1** (95% CI **−1.737 to −0.487**). Negative favours REP on U1. The interval half-width, about **0.625**, **missed** the target **0.50**. COMP performed better on wetland outcomes. Because both model and objective differ, this is a *package comparison*, not an isolated estimate of adding R.

## Filled diagnostic probes

**Q1 · Values.** **[C/M]** U1 and wetland criteria favor different aspects of the policies; an audit must retain both outcomes rather than collapsing the trade-off into a claim of overall superiority.

**Q2 · Model.** **[E/M]** The omission's decision relevance changes with parameter errors and other model assumptions. The observed mean U1 advantage of REP nearly disappeared under a planning-parameter-error scenario. Assuming R is not evidence that R exists.

**Q3 · Blindness.** **[S/M]** Without upstream wetland, the retention-present and retention-absent models have identical transitions for the specified unexposed conditions; the evaluated likelihood yields no Bayes-factor evidence there. **[E/M]** In development world d05, omitting R increased true U1 cost by **2.36**, while the omitting policy never occupied R-exposed states. In the explored development/stress worlds, there were **seven exact exposure traps across six worlds**. These are model results, not measurements from real catchments.

**Q4 · Learning.** **[E/M]** In d05 a planned probe produced retention exposure probabilities **0.77 / 0.95 / 0.99** at commitment years 1 / 2 / 3, but posterior movement probabilities **0 / 0 / 0.15**. **Exposure is not yet distinguishable evidence.** The exact feasible learner reached a maximum ratio to **fixed-probe oracle X\*'s margin over the restricted fixed-policy menu B_extended** of **0.107 (10.7%)** in the primary variant and **0.179 (17.9%)** in sensitivity; its declared screen was not met. Most apparent learner gain came from *state-contingent commitment*, not posterior updates. The oracle X\* is constrained to the specified probe; it is the **comparison menu** that is called restricted.

# Appendix A · Flood case: response-and-coupling matrix

These rows are **not** a single confirmatory five-arm comparison. The evidence labels must stay attached to each observation.

| Response | Outcome / price if assumptions differ | Effect on what can be observed | Status / limitation |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Keep:** omit R | In d05 the true U1 cost increased by 2.36 when R mattered | Can retain zero exposure | **[E/M]** selected world |
| **Change objective:** COMP (F1-eco, omit R) | Better wetland outcomes; conventional U1 price | **[IP]** Wetland investment could expose R as a by-product; this coupling was **not tested as a learning intervention** | **[C/M]** package comparison; **[IP]** learning effect |
| **Revise model:** REP (assume R, U1) | Lower mean U1 than COMP under confirmatory conditions; less reliable advantage with parameter error | **[IP]** May reduce incentive to learn R for this decision; not tested as a complete feedback loop | **[C/M]** package result; **[E/M]** parameter error; **[IP]** learning effect |
| **Probe / observe** | Probe cost, delays and weak posterior gains in examined worlds | Exposes mechanism before evidence arrives | **[E/M]** selected worlds; **alternative sensor untested** |
| **Hedge:** MIX(R,q) | Equal-weighted mean U1 **6.465–6.638**, below pure F0 plans **6.924** (assume R) and **6.967** (omit R); wetland deficit between pure plans | Learning effect not established | **[E/M]** heuristic, *world means only*, not optimized belief-state planning |

**Comparison caution.** MIX was evaluated as an exploratory per-step mixed-model heuristic, not as a confirmatory arm or optimal adaptive belief-state policy. The published T5 table compares MIX with **pure F0 commitments**, not every possible policy. Its wetland-deficit outcomes lay between those F0 commitments. Thus REP's confirmatory superiority to COMP on mean U1 never established REP as the best U1 policy in the study.

**Coupling [IP].** Values influence wetland investment → investment determines whether R can operate in observable transitions → evidence may update beliefs → updated beliefs may alter later policies. **WP1 did not confirm this entire loop as one intervention.**

**Provisional audit conclusion.** The example warrants comparing multiple objectives, explicitly testing exposure and **time to informative evidence**, and benchmarking a feasible learner against simpler state-dependent and hedged rules. It does **not** select a universally best flood policy.

**Status key.** [C] confirmatory (missed precision) · [E] exploratory / development-world comparison · [S] exact property under the specified model · [M] model-derived · [IP] interpretation, not directly tested.

# Appendix B · Hypothetical transfer: lending selection

**Entirely fictional teaching example.** No data, lender, statistical effect or intervention outcome is claimed here. It illustrates the structure of selective feedback; it is **not** a recommendation to experiment on applicants without safeguards.

## Case card and decision relevance

An organization uses a model to approve or reject loan applications. It records repayment outcomes only for loans it actually issues. It is deciding whether to retain its threshold, revise its underwriting criteria, improve its measurement, or use a safeguarded learning strategy.

**Gate:** If rejected applicants' unobserved potential repayment outcomes would change the preferred approval policy, the missing counterfactual is decision-relevant. If every plausible model supports the same action, learning may have limited value—but **Q3 is still mandatory**.

## The four probes

- **Q1 — Objective:** Does the organization assess only default risk and immediate revenue, or also fairness, access, long-term wellbeing and costs borne by applicants? Legitimate criteria and legal constraints must be specified, not guessed.
- **Q2 — Model:** Might risk predictions be inaccurate for applicants seldom approved? Could economic conditions, customer selection or unmeasured factors change the conclusion?
- **Q3 — Observability:** For rejected applicants, the counterfactual outcome *“repayment if this lender had approved”* is not directly observed. Approved-only outcomes can reinforce confidence in the selection rule. Outside credit records might inform some questions but do not automatically identify this loan-specific counterfactual.
- **Q4 — Learning:** Consider safer lawful options such as consent-based data collection, external validation, natural experiments or tightly governed trials. Ask when competing models would predict **different, observable outcomes**, who bears credit risk and whether a simpler conditional decision rule suffices. Random approvals are **not** presumed ethical or appropriate.

## Response-and-coupling sketch

| Response | Potential benefit | Missing evidence / risk |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Keep current threshold | Operational continuity | Selective labels may persist |
| Revise objective | Makes excluded outcomes visible in deliberation | Could change who is approved and whose outcomes are observed |
| Revise model | May improve ranking with current evidence | Does not by itself reveal rejected applicants' counterfactual repayments |
| Observe / evaluate safely | Could improve decision-relevant evidence | Privacy, fairness, financial and consent/oversight duties |
| Robust / reversible hedge | May limit exposure to uncertain predictions | Could reduce the value of further learning, at a cost |

**Workshop prompt:** Which people and outcomes are selected into the training and evaluation evidence? Which assumptions could the organization check without changing applicant treatment? What external oversight is required before any intervention? Who participates in defining an acceptable outcome? Which analogy with A2 fails? (One key difference is that financial outcomes involve human agency, law and potential harm; wetlands in A2 are synthetic state variables.)
